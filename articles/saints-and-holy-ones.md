Paul's letter to the Philippians opens with a greeting that the King James Version gives as "to all the saints in Christ Jesus which are at Philippi." A modern reader may picture a small group of exceptional Christians, the kind whose names are given to churches and whose faces appear in stained glass. Paul meant every believer in the city, including the two women in chapter 4 whom he begs to stop quarrelling.

The word behind "saints" is the ordinary Greek adjective for "holy", used as a noun. This article looks at what it meant, why English Bibles have rendered it in different ways, and what Open Word Bible does.

## What "holy" means

In the Bible "holy" first means set apart for God, belonging to him. Things are holy: the holy place, the holy city, holy garments, holy food. Times are holy: the Sabbath. People are holy, not because they have reached a high moral level but because God has claimed them.

That is how the idea starts in the Old Testament. At Sinai God tells Israel what it is to be:

> {{OWB:EXO.19.6.s1:B}}

The meaning decision explains: "'Holy' means set apart for God and marked by his character (Leviticus 11:44; 19:2)." Both sides are there. Israel is holy because God chose it, and it is called to live in a way that matches the God it belongs to: "You must be holy, because I, the LORD your God, am holy" (Leviticus 19:2). The record for that verse notes that the commands that follow, about parents, the poor, workers, the deaf and blind, courts and outsiders, "show what a holy people looks like in daily life, not only at the altar."

## The holy ones in the New Testament

The Greek word is {{LEMMA:PHP.1.1:9}} (*hagios*). In the plural, used as a noun, it means "the holy ones", and in the New Testament letters and Acts it is one of the commonest names for believers. Paul addresses whole churches this way (Romans 1:7; 1 Corinthians 1:2; 2 Corinthians 1:1; Ephesians 1:1; Philippians 1:1; Colossians 1:2). He collects money for "the holy ones" in Jerusalem, the poor among the believers there (Romans 15:26). He tells the Corinthians, who were suing each other in court, that "the holy ones" will judge the world (1 Corinthians 6:1–2).

The meaning decisions say the same thing in many ways. At Philippians 1:1 the word "describes every believer, not a select few: the whole church is God's holy people, as Israel was (Exodus 19:6), set apart 'in Christ Jesus'." At 1 Corinthians 1:2 they "already belong to God, set apart by him in Christ", and the record adds that this "is all the more striking because the letter goes on to correct serious faults in this church." At Philippians 4:21, where Paul says "greet every holy one", the word is singular: "each believer is greeted, with no one left out."

So "holy" here is something God has done before it is something believers do. It is a status given, and a calling to live up to.

## Why "saints" can mislead

English "saint" comes through Latin *sanctus*, "holy". The early English Bibles used it for these verses, and the King James Version made "the saints" familiar.

Over the centuries, though, the English word changed. In ordinary speech a saint is an unusually good person ("she's a saint to put up with him"), or, in Catholic and Orthodox use, a person the church has recognized after death as being with God and worthy of honor. Both senses have deep roots, and the second grew directly out of the New Testament's own language. But neither is what Paul means when he writes to a congregation in Corinth.

The rendering decisions say this plainly: "in current English 'saints' usually means unusually good people or those honored by a church; Paul is writing to the whole congregation."

English translations have answered in several ways. Many traditional and formal translations keep "saints", sometimes with a footnote. The New International Version (2011) and the New Living Translation often say "God's holy people" or "the Lord's people". Others say "God's people", "believers" or "holy ones".

Each has a cost. "Saints" is familiar and short, and it keeps a link with the church's later use of the word, which some readers value. "Holy ones" is exact but unusual English. "God's people" or "believers" is clear but drops the word "holy", which is the point of the name.

## What Open Word Bible does

The records render the word differently at each level, and the same pattern runs from Acts to Revelation.

**Literal says "holy ones".** In Paul's letters and in Acts, Literal keeps the shape of the Greek: "the holy ones". In a few books, Hebrews, Jude and Revelation, and once in Acts (26:10), Literal keeps the traditional "saints" instead; the rendering decision at Revelation 5:8 says "Literal keeps the traditional word." The two words mean the same, and a reviewer may decide to make them agree.

**Balanced says "God's holy people".** This keeps the word "holy" and makes clear that the whole people is meant. Compare Philippians 1:1 at the two levels:

> {{OWB:PHP.1.1.s1:L}}

> {{OWB:PHP.1.1.s1:B}}

Where the speaker addresses God or Christ, Balanced says "your holy people", as Ananias does when he protests about Saul:

> {{OWB:ACT.9.13.s1:B}}

The meaning decision notes that this is the first time Acts uses the word for believers, and that "your" makes them the Lord Jesus's own people. It rejects two alternatives by name: "especially good people" and "people declared saints after death".

**Readable usually says "God's people".** For a new reader, "God's people" is clearest. In the opening address of several letters Readable keeps "God's holy people", so that the opening of Philippians reads the same at Balanced and Readable.

None of these levels uses "saints" for believers outside the Literal level. The reason is the same each time: the word means every believer, and the translation says so.

## Holy ones in the Old Testament

In the Hebrew Bible the plural of *qadosh*, "holy", does something different. Most often "the holy ones" are heavenly beings, the angels who stand around God. Job is asked:

> {{OWB:JOB.5.1.s1:B}}

The meaning decision explains that "'Holy ones' is a name for the beings who stand around God in heaven (Job 15:15; Psalm 89:6, 8; Zechariah 14:5)." So Readable says "the heavenly beings" here, because "holy ones" alone "could be taken for holy people on earth". In Psalm 89:6 Readable says "the angels in heaven".

But sometimes the holy ones are God's people on earth, as in Psalm 34:

> {{OWB:PSA.34.10.s1:B}}

Literal says "his holy ones"; Balanced says "his people set apart". The record says that here the word {{LEMMA:PSA.34.10:4.1}} "usually means angels, but here it is his people, set apart for him (Leviticus 19:2; Deuteronomy 33:3)."

## Daniel 7: whose kingdom?

The hardest case is Daniel 7, where the Aramaic {{LEMMA:DAN.7.18:3}} (*qaddishin*) appears in the interpretation of Daniel's vision:

> {{OWB:DAN.7.18.s1:B}}

Who are "the holy ones of the Most High"? The meaning decision sets out the two views. "Holy ones" usually means angels in the Bible and in Daniel itself (4:10, 14), and some read the phrase so here. But these holy ones are persecuted and worn down by the small horn (7:21, 25), which fits God's people on earth better, and verse 27 gives the kingdom to "the people of the holy ones of the Most High". "Most readers take the holy ones to be faithful Israel, God's people", and the decision carries a footnote.

The levels follow that. Literal and Balanced keep "the holy ones of the Most High", so the phrase stays open as it is in the text. Readable says "God's holy people", and its rendering decision says that it "takes the reading that the holy ones are God's faithful people on earth, which verses 21, 25 and 27 favor".

The New Testament has a similar case. Paul prays that the Thessalonians will be blameless "when our Lord Jesus comes with all his holy ones" (1 Thessalonians 3:13). The words echo Zechariah 14:5, where the holy ones are probably angels, but in Paul's letters the word means believers, and the next chapter speaks of God bringing the believers who have died with Jesus. The decision is marked uncertain: "Paul may include both."

## A name given, not earned

The New Testament name for Christians is not "the good" or "the faithful", though they are called to be both. It is "the holy ones", the people God has set apart for himself, as he did with Israel. That is a comfort before it is a demand. Paul calls the Corinthians holy in the first verses of a letter that goes on to rebuke them for division, lawsuits and sexual sin, and then he asks them to live as what they already are.
