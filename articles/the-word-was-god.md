John's Gospel begins with one of the most discussed sentences in the Bible:

> {{GREEK:JHN.1.1:1-17}}

Open Word Bible's Balanced level reads:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.1.s1:B}}

Seventeen Greek words, three short clauses, and every phrase was a decision. Here are the main ones, as the reasoning record for the verse gives them.

## Where the sentence stands

John 1:1 opens a prologue of eighteen verses that works like an overture to the Gospel. The Word is with God before anything exists. All things come into being through him (verse 3). He is the light that the darkness has not overcome (verse 5). Then comes the turn: "the Word became flesh" (verse 14). The prologue ends where it began, with the Son close beside the Father, making known the God no one has ever seen (verse 18).

So verse 1 is not a stand-alone statement about God. It introduces the person whose life the rest of the book tells. Whatever John says the Word is here, he is saying about Jesus.

## "In the beginning"

The first two words, {{GREEK:JHN.1.1:1-2}}, are the same two words that open Genesis 1:1 in the Greek Old Testament. A reader who knew the Scriptures in Greek could not miss it. John is retelling creation, and he places the Word there before the first thing was made. So every level keeps the echo, and the record rejects "at the start" and "before time", which would lose it. A footnote points to Genesis. See ["In the beginning"](/articles/in-the-beginning/) for the Hebrew of [Genesis 1](/gen/1/).

## "The Word"

"The Word" translates {{GREEK:JHN.1.1:5}}, and it is a key term: every level and every passage uses the same English word for it. In Genesis 1, God creates by speaking: "God said, let there be light". The translation keeps that link. The record considered three other options. "The Message" is too narrow. "Reason" imports Greek philosophy that John does not develop. "The Logos" is not a translation at all, only the Greek word in Latin letters.

Other languages meet the problem in their own way. In Spanish and French, the ordinary words for "word", *la Palabra* and *la Parole*, show the link to speech most plainly, but both are feminine, so the Word would be "she" until he becomes a man in verse 14. The Spanish uses the traditional *el Verbo* and the French *le Verbe*, both from the Latin *Verbum*. Swahili uses *Neno*, the everyday word, but gives it the agreement used for a person: "he was" rather than "it was". All three are rendering decisions, flagged for a native speaker's review. The meaning they express was decided once, for every language.

## "With God"

The second clause says the Word was {{GREEK:JHN.1.1:10-12}}. The preposition normally means "toward". Here it describes presence and relationship, so the translation says "with God". The note carries the fuller sense the record gives as an alternative: turned toward God, face to face. Verse 2 repeats the point for emphasis: "He was with God in the beginning."

Notice the small word before "God" in this clause, {{GREEK:JHN.1.1:11}}. It is the article, the Greek word for "the". Word for word, the clause says the Word was "toward the God". That article matters for the next clause.

## "The Word was God"

The last clause is {{GREEK:JHN.1.1:14-17}}. Step by step:

- {{GREEK:JHN.1.1:14}} is "God". It comes first, without the article.
- {{GREEK:JHN.1.1:15}} is "was".
- {{GREEK:JHN.1.1:16-17}} is "the Word", with the article.

So word for word it is "God was the Word". But Greek word order does not mark the subject the way English does. When "was" joins two nouns, the one with the article is normally the subject. "The Word" has the article, so it is the subject, and "God" says what the Word is. English puts the subject first, so every level reads "the Word was God".

Putting "God" first gives it weight, as English might by stress: the Word was *God*. Leaving out the article does something more precise. In the clause just before, "the God" with the article meant the Father. Had John written "the God" again, he would have made the Word and the Father one and the same person, when he has just said the Word was *with* God. Without the article, "God" says what the Word is, in the record's words, "without making the Word the same person as 'the God' just mentioned".

## Why not "a god" or "divine"?

These are the two alternatives the record weighs and rejects. Both have been printed in English Bibles.

"The Word was a god" appeared in the Unitarian *Improved Version* of 1808, and is best known today from the New World Translation of the Jehovah's Witnesses (1950). The case for it is real: Greek has no word for "a", and a noun without the article can be indefinite. But here it would contradict John's insistence that there is one God. The same Gospel calls the Father "the only God" (5:44) and "the only true God" (17:3). It would not then introduce a second, lesser god beside him.

"The Word was divine" appeared in James Moffatt's New Testament (1913), which reads "the Logos was divine", and in Edgar Goodspeed's (1923). It catches something true. Many grammarians, following a study by Philip Harner in 1973, read a noun placed like this as describing the Word's nature. But in English "divine" is weaker than the noun John chose. People speak of divine music. Greek had an adjective for "divine", and John did not use it. He wrote "God".

So the translation says "the Word was God", with a footnote that gives the alternatives.

## A question with a long history

Christians have argued about this sentence since the early centuries. Origen, writing in the third century, noticed that John uses the article with "God" for the Father and leaves it out for the Word, and he drew conclusions from that about how the Son relates to the Father. In the fourth century Arius taught that the Son was the first and greatest of created beings. The Council of Nicaea in 325 answered that the Son is "true God from true God", of one being with the Father. John 1:1 was at the center of that debate, as it is in conversations with Jehovah's Witnesses today.

Open Word Bible does not try to settle these debates by translation. It translates what the Greek says and shows its reasons. The decision on this clause is not marked uncertain, because the grammar and the rest of the Gospel point the same way. Anyone who disagrees can read the reasoning and the alternatives in the record and judge them.

## How the Gospel ends

John comes back to the point at the end of his book. After the resurrection, Thomas sees Jesus and says to him:

> {{OWB:JHN.20.28.s1:B}}

The Greek, {{GREEK:JHN.20.28:6-12}}, says "the Lord of me and the God of me". The record for John 20:28 notes that the words are spoken to Jesus, and that they close the frame the prologue opened: the book that began by saying who the Word is ends with a disciple saying it to his face. Between the two stands John 1:18, which calls the Son "the one and only God" who makes the Father known. See ["One and only" or "only begotten"?](/articles/one-and-only-son/) for that verse.

## Readable

The Readable level reads: "{{OWB:JHN.1.1.s1:R}}" The word "already" is not in the Greek. It is there because the verb "was", {{GREEK:JHN.1.1:3}}, describes something continuing, and John sets it against "came into being" in verses 3, 6 and 14. All things came into being. A man named John came. The Word became flesh. But in the beginning the Word did not come into being; he simply was. When the beginning began, the Word was already there. A rendering decision records the addition and its reason.
