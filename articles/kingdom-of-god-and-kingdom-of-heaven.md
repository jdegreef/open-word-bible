Mark sums up the whole of Jesus's preaching in one sentence:

> {{OWB:MRK.1.14.s1:B}}

Everything else in the Gospels unfolds from "the kingdom of God has come near": the parables, the healings, the Lord's Prayer, the arguments with the Pharisees, the charge on the cross. Yet the phrase raises two questions for a translator. The first is what "kingdom" means: a place, a people, or God's rule itself. The second is why Matthew almost always says something different, "the kingdom of heaven".

## A kingdom or a reign?

The Greek word is {{LEMMA:MRK.1.15:10}} (*basileia*). In English, "kingdom" usually means a territory: the United Kingdom, the kingdom of Denmark. The Greek word can mean that too. When Jesus says "if a kingdom is divided against itself" (Mark 3:24), or warns that "kingdom will go to war with kingdom" (Matthew 24:7), he means realms ruled by kings.

But the word's first meaning is the act and authority of ruling: kingship, reign, royal power. Jesus tells a parable about a nobleman who goes to a distant country "to receive a *basileia*" and come back (Luke 19:12). He does not bring back land in his luggage; he comes back as king. The meaning decision for the verse explains that "the word means kingship more than land", and compares Herod's son Archelaus, who went to Rome to be confirmed as king. So Balanced and Readable say what receiving a kingdom means here: he travels "to be made king".

The same is true of Jesus's answer to Pilate:

> {{OWB:JHN.18.36.s1:B}}

The record says the word here means "his kingship and rule, not only a territory." Jesus answers Pilate's question, "Are you the king?", by speaking of his reign.

Many scholars have argued that in Jesus's preaching the "kingdom of God" means chiefly God's reign: God acting as king, putting the world right, rather than a region on a map or a place where the dead go. The Hebrew Bible's hope was that God himself would reign over all the earth (Zechariah 14:9; Daniel 2:44; 7:14, 27). Some translations have tried to bring this out with "the reign of God"; the Scholars Version of the Jesus Seminar went as far as "God's imperial rule". Others reply that the word also includes a realm and a people: people "enter" the kingdom, sit at its feast and inherit it, which a reign alone does not allow.

## What Open Word Bible does with "kingdom"

The records keep "kingdom" as the usual rendering at the Literal and Balanced levels, because English has no other word that holds both sides, the rule and the realm, and because the phrase "kingdom of God" is fixed in the language of the church. The meaning decisions then say which side is in view.

At Matthew 3:2 the meaning decision defines Matthew's phrase as "God's rule, the kingdom of God" and gives as the rejected alternative "a place in heaven where people go after death". At Mark 1:15 another decision explains "has come near": "God's rule is breaking in through Jesus' words and works." At Luke 17:21, where the Pharisees ask when the kingdom will come, Jesus answers:

> {{OWB:LUK.17.20.s1:B}}

The older rendering "the kingdom of God is within you" is given in a footnote. The decision explains the choice of "among you": Jesus is speaking to Pharisees who opposed him, "and elsewhere the kingdom is something people enter, not something inside them." It adds that the reading "within you" "is old and has long been held."

The Readable level goes further where "kingdom" might mislead a young reader. In 1 Corinthians 4:20 Paul says that the kingdom of God is not a matter of words but of power. A rendering decision notes that "for a young reader 'kingdom' suggests a country", so Readable says:

> {{OWB:1CO.4.19.s1:R}}

Similarly, the criminal on the cross asks Jesus to remember him "when you come as king" (Luke 23:42, Readable), and at Revelation 11:15 the Balanced level says "The rule over the world now belongs to our Lord and to his Christ." These are the exceptions. Across the 162 places the word occurs, "kingdom" remains the rule, and Readable often shortens "the kingdom of God" to "God's kingdom".

## Why Matthew says "kingdom of heaven"

Mark and Luke always say "the kingdom of God". John uses the phrase only twice (John 3:3, 5). Matthew uses "the kingdom of heaven" 32 times, and "the kingdom of God" only four times. Where Mark has Jesus proclaim "the kingdom of God has come near", Matthew has:

> {{OWB:MAT.4.17.s1:B}}

Why the difference? The usual answer, and the one the records give, is reverence. Many Jews of the time avoided saying God's name, and often avoided even the word "God", using another word in its place: "heaven", "the Power", "the Name". The prodigal son says "I have sinned against heaven" (Luke 15:18), meaning against God. The meaning decision at Matthew 3:2 explains that Matthew says "kingdom of heaven" "as Jews often spoke of heaven out of reverence for God's name. It is God's reign coming to earth, as in the prayer 'your kingdom come' (6:10)."

That last point matters, because "the kingdom of heaven" is easy to misread as "heaven", a place above the sky where souls go after death. Matthew does not mean that. The kingdom of heaven comes near; it is preached; it is like a mustard seed and like yeast. It is the kingdom that belongs to heaven, God's own rule, arriving on earth. The prayer Jesus teaches says exactly this:

> {{OWB:MAT.6.9.s1:B}}

Some scholars, notably Jonathan Pennington, have argued that reverence is not the whole story: Matthew loves the pairing of heaven and earth, and "of heaven" sets God's kingdom against the kingdoms of the earth. The two explanations do not exclude each other, and neither makes "kingdom of heaven" a different kingdom from "kingdom of God".

Matthew himself shows that the two phrases mean the same thing. In 19:23–24 Jesus says the same thing twice, once with each:

> {{OWB:MAT.19.23.s1:B}} {{OWB:MAT.19.24.s1:B}}

The meaning decision at 19:24 says it plainly: "the kingdom of God: the same as the kingdom of heaven in 19:23". The decisions at 12:28 and 21:43, where Matthew also says "kingdom of God", say the same.

## Why the translation keeps Matthew's phrase

If the two phrases mean the same, why not write "kingdom of God" everywhere, as some readers have suggested, so that no one thinks Matthew means heaven after death? Open Word Bible keeps the difference, for three reasons.

First, it is Matthew's own wording, and it shows something true about him: a Gospel written with Jewish readers and Jewish ways of speaking in view. Second, a reader comparing the Gospels side by side ought to see that Matthew and Mark word the same saying differently. Third, the risk of misreading is met by explanation rather than by changing the text: the meaning decision at 3:2 explains the phrase where it first appears, and the reader shows that decision beside the verse.

The rendering decision at 3:2 sets out the levels:

- **Literal** says "the kingdom of the heavens", because the Greek word is plural, following the Hebrew word for "heavens", which has no singular.
- **Balanced and Readable** say "the kingdom of heaven", as English does, and keep Matthew's phrase.

Readable does make one change: where Matthew himself says "kingdom of God" (12:28; 19:24; 21:31, 43), it uses the shorter "God's kingdom".

## Already and not yet

One more question hangs over the phrase: is the kingdom present or future? The Gospels say both. Jesus says it "has come near" and is "among you"; he also teaches his disciples to pray "may your kingdom come" and speaks of a feast to come with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob (Matthew 8:11). The meaning decisions do not choose one side. At 1 Corinthians 4:20 the decision calls it "God's rule, already at work now and still to come in full", and at Mark 1:15 it explains "has come near" as "close at hand, already pressing in" rather than "fully arrived". That tension is part of the message, and the translation leaves it where the text puts it.
