Readers of English Bibles disagree about some things that are, strictly, matters of wording. Should "brothers" be "brothers and sisters" when the whole church is meant? Should Jesus's title be "Christ" or "Messiah"? Should pronouns for God have capital letters? Should God's name be written "the LORD" or "Yahweh"? Open Word Bible lets each reader choose.

## The settings

- **Gender language.** Inclusive by default, Traditional as an option. Where the original means everyone, the default says so: John 1:4 reads "{{OWB:JHN.1.4.s1:B}}", and Traditional reads "the light of men". God, Christ and named men are never changed.
- **Title.** "Christ" by default, "Messiah" as an option. The Greek word is a title meaning "anointed one", and John's Gospel itself explains it in 1:41.
- **Pronouns for God.** Lowercase "he" by default, like the original, which has no capital letters; "He" as an option.
- **Name of God.** In the Old Testament, God's personal name is written "the LORD" by default, following the ancient custom of not saying it aloud, and "Yahweh" as an option.
- **Measures.** Where a passage has a measurement, modern units by default and the original units as an option. Noah's ark is 450 feet long, or 300 cubits (Genesis 6:15); the jars at Cana held 20 to 30 gallons, or two or three measures (John 2:6). The Literal level always keeps the ancient unit.
- **Spanish plural "you".** *Ustedes*, as spoken in Latin America, or *vosotros*, as in Spain.

A setting for UK spelling is planned.

## Why these, and not others

A setting is only allowed when both options mean the same thing. "Brothers and sisters" and "brothers" (in its older, inclusive sense) both refer to the whole church. "Christ" and "Messiah" are the same title in two languages. Capitalising "he" adds reverence but not meaning. "The LORD" and "Yahweh" name the same God; one follows a reading custom, the other spells out the name. Three hundred cubits and 450 feet are the same length.

Disagreements about **meaning** are different. When scholars disagree about what a verse says, there is no setting. The translation commits to one reading, the record explains why, and a footnote gives the other. A reader cannot switch to a different theology with a tap, and should not be able to.

That is why you cannot set John 1:14 to "only begotten". The record decides that the Greek word means "one and only", unique, and explains where the older rendering came from. That is a claim about meaning. It belongs in a decision, with a footnote, where it can be argued over, and not in a menu.

## Where a setting does not apply

The gender setting is the one readers ask about most, so its limits are worth seeing. It is used only where the original word itself includes women and men.

In Philippians 4:1 Paul calls the church in Philippi his "brothers". The record notes that the next verse names two women in that church, so the inclusive default reads:

> {{OWB:PHP.4.1.s1:B}}

In John 1:12 the believers become "children of God". The Greek word for children is already inclusive, so there is no setting to switch: both options would say "children". One verse later, in John 1:13, the Greek word for "husband" is a male word in a sentence about conception. It means a husband, so the setting does not touch it either. In each case a decision in the record says why.

The title setting has its own fine print. In John 1:41 Andrew says, "We have found the Messiah", and John explains the Hebrew title with the Greek one. If the setting changed both words, a reader could end up with "the Messiah (that is, the Messiah)". So Andrew's word stays "Messiah" in every setting, and only John's explanation switches, between "Christ" and "Anointed One".

## Fair questions

**Is inclusive language a modern agenda?** It is a question of what the original words mean. The Greek word in John 1:4 is the ordinary word for human beings, and the old English "men" meant the same thing. Many readers today hear "men" as males only. The default says what the Greek says; the Traditional option keeps the older English for those who prefer it. Both are in the record, and neither changes the sentence's meaning.

**Why "the LORD" by default, when the name is Yahweh?** Because that is how the name has been read aloud for many centuries, in synagogues and in churches, and it is what most readers know. The record says plainly that the four letters are God's personal name, and that its first pronunciation is not recorded; Yahweh is the form most scholars reconstruct. Exodus 3:15 calls it God's name "forever". The setting is one tap away.

**Does every setting need its own review?** No. Each setting is stored once, inside the text, as a marked pair of options, so the alternatives sit side by side in the same sentence. A reviewer checks both together, and a setting never becomes a second translation.

## How it works

Your choices are remembered in your browser, and they follow you from book to book. When you share a link to a verse, it can carry your settings, so a friend who opens it sees "Messiah" or "Yahweh" just as you did, until they change it.
