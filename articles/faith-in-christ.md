Galatians 2:16 is at the heart of Paul's argument in Galatians. It contains a phrase that has divided careful readers for a generation: does Paul speak of our faith in Christ, or of Christ's own faithfulness? This article walks through the verse, the grammar, the history of the debate, and what Open Word Bible chose at each level.

## Antioch

The verse comes at the end of a story. In Antioch, Jewish and Gentile believers ate together, and Peter, whom Paul calls Cephas, ate with them. Then visitors came from Jerusalem:

> {{OWB:GAL.2.12.s1:B}}

Other Jewish believers followed Peter, even Barnabas (2:13). Paul confronted him in front of everyone: you live like a Gentile yourself, so how can you force Gentiles to live like Jews (2:14)? By withdrawing from the table, Peter was acting as if Gentile believers had to keep the Jewish law to belong.

Where Paul's words to Peter end is not marked. Greek has no quotation marks, and the record for 2:14 closes the quotation there, where the direct question ends, with a note that verses 15–21 may continue what Paul said. Either way, verses 15–16 say what both apostles already knew:

> {{OWB:GAL.2.15.s1:B}}

Most of this sentence raises no doubt about meaning. One phrase does.

## The grammar, step by step

The phrase is {{GREEK:GAL.2.16:12-15}}. Take it a word at a time:

- the first word means "through";
- the second is a form of {{LEMMA:GAL.2.16:13}} (*pistis*), which can mean faith, trust, or faithfulness;
- then the name "Jesus Christ", in the genitive case, the case English usually renders with "of".

So word for word it says "through faith of Jesus Christ". The King James Version kept exactly that: "by the faith of Jesus Christ". But "of" hides the question. English has the same problem with "the love of God", which can mean God's love for us or our love for God. When Greek puts a person's name after the word for faith, it can mean:

- faith directed *to* that person: **faith in Jesus Christ**, or
- faith that person *has*: **the faithfulness of Jesus Christ**, his own faithful obedience to God, shown in his death.

Grammarians call the first an objective genitive and the second a subjective genitive. The Greek allows both. Paul uses the phrase twice in this verse, the second time as {{GREEK:GAL.2.16:24-26}}, "by faith of Christ".

## The shape of the verse

Before weighing the two readings, it helps to see how the verse is built. Paul makes the same contrast three times:

- a person is not justified by works of the law, except through faith of Jesus Christ;
- we believed in Christ Jesus, so that we might be justified by faith of Christ and not by works of the law;
- by works of the law no flesh will be justified.

Each time, works of the law stand on one side. Faith stands on the other, or in the third line is simply implied. In the middle stands the one plain statement about believers: {{GREEK:GAL.2.16:17-21}}, "we have believed in Christ Jesus". The verb is a simple past. It looks back to the moment Paul and Peter, both Jews by birth, put their trust in Christ. Even they, with the law in their hands, had to come this way.

That shape matters for the question that follows, because each side reads it differently.

## The case for each

Both sides have good arguments, and the record for the verse sets them out.

**For "faith in Christ":** the middle of the verse says just that, in words that cannot be read any other way. In the next chapter Paul speaks of believers having faith as Abraham did (3:6–9). Defenders of this reading add that repetition is how Paul presses a point: writing to people tempted to add the law to Christ, he says it three times on purpose.

**For "the faithfulness of Christ":** read the first way, the verse says the same thing three times. And Paul does use the same construction elsewhere for someone's own faith: "the faith of Abraham" (Romans 4:16), "the faithfulness of God" (Romans 3:3). On this reading, Christ's faithfulness is the ground of justification, and "we have believed in Christ Jesus" is the believer's answer to it.

## How it has been read

For most of church history, readers took the phrase as faith in Christ. Roy Harrisville III surveyed the Greek church fathers in a 1994 article and found some ambiguous passages but none that clearly meant Christ's own faithfulness. Ian Wallis, in a 1995 study, read the early evidence differently. Martin Luther's German Bible says "through faith in Jesus Christ", and the Reformation's teaching on justification by faith drew on this verse.

The modern debate took off with Richard Hays's book *The Faith of Jesus Christ* (1983), which argued for the subjective reading. James Dunn answered him at the Society of Biblical Literature in 1991, and their papers became a reference point for both sides. When Hays's book was reissued in 2002, both papers were printed with it, so readers could weigh the exchange for themselves. One of Dunn's arguments, made also by Arland Hultgren, is grammatical: when Paul clearly means someone's own faith, he usually puts "the" before "faith", and here he does not. A 2009 volume edited by Michael Bird and Preston Sprinkle gathered scholars from both sides.

Translations moved with the debate. The 1989 New Revised Standard Version put "faith in" in its text and "the faith of" in a footnote. Its 2021 update moved "the faith of" into the text. Most English versions still read "faith in".

## What each reading means for a reader

On the "faith in" reading, the verse names the way a person receives God's verdict: by trusting Christ. The stress falls on the believer's response.

On the "faithfulness" reading, the verse names the ground of the verdict: Christ's own obedience, carried through to the cross. Believing in him follows as the answer.

The record makes one more point that is easy to miss. Both readings agree on what matters most in the verse: a person is put right with God through Christ, and not by the works of the law. The question is where Paul puts the weight in this phrase. Neither reading makes faith or Christ unimportant.

## What the translation does

Open Word Bible follows the first reading at every level, marks the decision *uncertain*, and gives "through the faithfulness of Jesus Christ" in a footnote. The Literal level says "faith in" too. The Greek has no preposition, so even the most literal rendering has to choose a reading. English cannot leave the question open here the way "grace for grace" can in John 1:16; "faith of" would sound to most readers like a mistake, or would quietly lean toward Christ's own faith.

> {{OWB:GAL.2.15.s1:L}}

In the reader, the decision carries the uncertain label. Tap verse 16 and the record shows both readings, the reasons for each, and the footnote with the wording we did not choose.

The same question comes back in other places, and the records answer it the same way. In Galatians 2:20, Paul lives {{GREEK:GAL.2.20:16-23}}, "by faith ... of the Son of God". The record follows "faith in" and notes that the words after it, "who loved me and handed himself over", could describe the Son's own faithful act. Galatians 3:22, Romans 3:22 and Philippians 3:9 are also marked uncertain, each with a footnote. The note on Galatians 3:22 adds a point for the other side: verses 23 and 25 speak of faith "coming", as an event in history, which suits Christ's faithfulness. Romans 3:26 is marked uncertain too. Ephesians 3:12 is not, because in Ephesians faith is plainly the believers' trust in Christ (1:15).

## The other decisions

The rest of the sentence carries several decisions shared by every language:

- **Gentile sinners** is how Jews spoke of Gentiles, who lived without God's law. Paul takes up the phrase in order to turn it: even Jews must be justified by faith. Readable makes clear it is a label, not Paul's judgment.
- **Justified** is a law-court word: God declares a person to be in the right, acquitted and accepted. Literal and Balanced keep "justify", the word that ties this verse to Romans; Readable says "put right with God".
- **Works of the law** means doing what the law of Moses requires, as the ground of being accepted by God. Food laws and circumcision were the issue in Antioch and Galatia, but Paul's argument goes on to the whole law (3:10–12; 5:3).
- **Except through faith** could look as if works and faith together justify. The rest of the verse sets them against each other three times, so Balanced states the positive side as its own clause: "a person is justified only through faith".
- **No flesh** echoes Psalm 143:2, a Hebrew way of saying "no human being". Balanced and Readable give the meaning, since "flesh" in English would suggest the body.
