Many readers meet the word "Christ" long before they know what it means. It sounds like a surname: Jesus Christ, as one might say John Smith. In fact it is a title, and the New Testament writers knew exactly what it meant. The Greek {{LEMMA:JHN.1.41:18}} (*christos*) means "anointed", and it translates the Hebrew *mashiach*, which came into English as "Messiah". The two words say the same thing, in two languages.

That is why Open Word Bible treats the choice between them as a reader setting, not as a translation decision. This article explains what each word carries, why the choice is yours, and where the setting stops.

## What "anointed" meant

In Israel, oil was poured on a person's head to set them apart for an office God had given. Priests were anointed (Leviticus 4:3), and above all kings. When David has Saul at his mercy in a cave, he refuses to touch him, for one reason:

> {{OWB:1SA.24.7.s1:B}}

The meaning decision for this verse explains that the phrase "names the king by his relation to God: to raise a hand against him is to strike at the One who appointed him." The word here is {{LEMMA:1SA.24.7:12.2}}, *mashiach*, and in the Old Testament it is nearly always a description of a living king or priest, not yet a title for one who is to come.

Psalm 2 sets the nations against God and "his anointed":

> {{OWB:PSA.2.2.s1:B}}

The record notes that the psalm speaks of the king on Zion, the son of David, and that the New Testament applies it to Jesus (Acts 4:25–27). By the time of Jesus many Jews hoped for an anointed king from David's line who would set Israel free. "The Anointed One", in Hebrew or Aramaic *meshiḥa*, had become the name of that hope.

The Greek translators of the Old Testament rendered *mashiach* with *christos*, and the New Testament, written in Greek, used the same word. When Peter says to Jesus "You are the *christos*", he is saying "You are the Messiah", the king Israel was waiting for:

> {{OWB:MAT.16.16.s1:B}}

## John translates it himself

Only John's Gospel uses the Hebrew title in Greek letters, twice, and both times John stops to translate it. When Andrew finds his brother, he says:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.41.s1:B}}

Andrew's word is {{LEMMA:JHN.1.41:14}}, *Messias*, and John adds that it means *christos*. The Samaritan woman does the same at 4:25: "I know that Messiah is coming." John's readers, many of them Greek speakers, needed to be told that the two words are one title. Modern readers often need to be told the same.

## Why each word is a good choice

**"Christ"** is the word the New Testament authors actually used, brought into English through Latin. It is the form English-speaking Christians have used for more than a thousand years, in creeds, prayers and hymns, and it carries all of that. It also reflects something real in the New Testament: by the time of Paul's letters, *christos* had become so closely joined to Jesus that it works almost like a name. Paul writes "Christ Jesus" and "Jesus Christ" side by side, and the believers in Antioch were called "Christians", Christ's people (Acts 11:26).

**"Messiah"** shows what the title means. It reminds a reader that Jesus was proclaimed as Israel's promised king, the son of David, and that the gospel was first a Jewish claim made in a Jewish setting. For a reader who hears "Christ" as a surname, "Messiah" can turn familiar verses into claims: "God has made him both Lord and Messiah" (Acts 2:36) says something "Christ" may not say to a newcomer.

English translations have moved between the two. The King James Version and most traditional translations say "Christ" throughout. The New Living Translation and the Christian Standard Bible say "Messiah" in many places in the Gospels and Acts, where it is plainly a title, and "Christ" in the letters. Some translations aimed at Jewish readers, and some scholars' own versions, use "Messiah" almost everywhere.

Both words mean the same. Neither changes what a sentence says about Jesus. That is exactly the test Open Word Bible uses for a setting: [settings change wording, never meaning](/articles/settings-change-wording/).

## How the setting works

Every *christos* in the New Testament is written in the records as a pair of options, with "Christ" first as the default. In the drafts there are 530 of them, in every book from Matthew to Revelation that uses the word, and every one carries the setting. The reader's settings panel offers "Christ" ("the familiar form") or "Messiah" ("shows it is a title meaning anointed king"), and your choice is remembered from book to book.

The levels write the pair slightly differently, because English needs an article with "Messiah" that it does not need with "Christ":

- **Literal** writes "Christ" or "Messiah", following the Greek closely: "Jesus Christ" or "Jesus Messiah".
- **Balanced and Readable** write "Christ" or "the Messiah": "Jesus Christ" or "Jesus the Messiah", "in Christ" or "in the Messiah".

So Philippians 1:21 reads, at the default:

> {{OWB:PHP.1.21.s1:B}}

With the setting changed, it reads "For me, living is the Messiah, and dying is gain."

Where the Greek has the article and the word is plainly a title, every level writes "the" outside the setting: "the Christ" or "the Messiah". This matters in verses like Matthew 16:16 above, or John the Baptist's denial at John 1:20. The rendering decision there explains: "The usual Balanced span would give 'I am not Christ', which reads as a name."

Other small cases follow the same rule. When Jesus warns that false messiahs will come (Matthew 24:24; Mark 13:22), the Literal level offers "false christs" or "false messiahs"; in Matthew, Balanced says people will falsely claim "to be the Christ" or "the Messiah". When Christ's name is in the possessive, the pair is "Christ's" or "the Messiah's".

## Where the setting does not apply

Three kinds of words never take the setting.

**"Messiah" in John 1:41 and 4:25.** Here the Greek word is *Messias*, not *christos*, and John's explanation follows. If both words switched, the setting could produce "We have found the Messiah (that is, the Messiah)". So Andrew's word is written "Messiah" plainly, at every level and every setting, and only John's explanation switches, between "Christ" and "Anointed One". The rendering decision gives the reason: "so no setting can produce 'the Messiah (which means the Messiah)'."

**"Christians."** The name the disciples were given in Antioch is a different Greek word, made from *christos*. It is the name of a group, and it stays "Christians".

**"Anointed" in the Old Testament.** The Hebrew *mashiach* is translated "anointed" at every level, as in 1 Samuel 24:7 and Psalm 2:2 above. There is no setting, because in most of these verses the word describes a particular king or priest, and "the Messiah" would claim more than the verse says. Instead the meaning decisions point forward. At Psalm 18:51 the record says the word "is the word behind 'Messiah' and 'Christ', and the promise was later understood to point to the Messiah." At Daniel 9:25–26, where "an anointed one" will be cut off, the decision is marked uncertain and lists the ways readers have taken it, from the high priest Onias III to Jesus the Messiah. That is a question of meaning, and meaning is never a setting.

## A setting, not a second translation

Some readers ask why the project does not simply choose one. The answer is that both choices are right, and each serves some readers better than the other. A Christian who has prayed "through Jesus Christ our Lord" all her life should not have to read "Jesus the Messiah" to read accurately. A reader who has never thought of Jesus as a Jewish king may need "Messiah" to hear what Peter heard. A Jewish reader exploring the New Testament may find "Messiah" the more honest word, since it names the claim directly.

Because the pair is stored in the sentence itself, there is still one translation. A reviewer checks both options together, and a link to a verse can carry your setting, so that a friend who opens it sees the same word you did.
