When William Tyndale was executed in 1536, there was no English Bible that could lawfully be read in England (see [William Tyndale and the English New Testament](/articles/william-tyndale/)). Two years later, every parish church in England was required to have one. By the end of the century English readers could choose between several translations, each with its own character, its own supporters and its own politics. All of them fed into the King James Version of 1611.

## Coverdale's Bible, 1535

The first complete printed Bible in English was the work of **Miles Coverdale**, a former Augustinian friar who had worked with Tyndale on the Continent. It appeared in 1535, most likely printed in Antwerp by Merten de Keyser.

Coverdale did not claim to work from Hebrew and Greek. His title page says the Bible was translated "out of Douche and Latyn," that is, from German and Latin. Where Tyndale's translations existed, Coverdale used them, revising lightly. For the rest he relied on Luther's German, the Zurich Bible, the Vulgate and the Latin of Santes Pagnini. His gift was for English rhythm rather than scholarship. His **Psalms**, revised for the Great Bible, were taken into the Book of Common Prayer and are still sung in Anglican churches.

## Matthew's Bible, 1537

Two years later came a Bible under the name of "Thomas Matthew," a pen name for **John Rogers**, another associate of Tyndale. It was the first to print Tyndale's unpublished translation of Joshua to 2 Chronicles, which Rogers seems to have obtained after Tyndale's arrest. The rest was Tyndale's published work and Coverdale's.

Remarkably, Matthew's Bible was licensed by the king. Within a year of Tyndale's death, most of the Bible the crown allowed to be sold in England was his. Rogers later became the first Protestant burned under Mary I, in 1555.

## The Great Bible, 1539

Henry VIII's chief minister, **Thomas Cromwell**, wanted an official Bible for the churches. In 1538 he ordered every parish to set up "one book of the whole Bible of the largest volume in English." Coverdale revised Matthew's Bible, and the result, printed in 1539, was called the **Great Bible** for its size. The 1540 edition carried a preface by Archbishop **Thomas Cranmer**.

Copies were often chained to a desk or pillar in church, to keep them from being stolen. For the first time, ordinary English people could hear the whole Bible read in their own language. The freedom did not last unchanged. An Act of 1543 restricted private Bible reading by women and by men below a certain rank, and under Mary I, from 1553, Protestant Bible publishing in England stopped and many Protestants fled abroad.

## The Geneva Bible, 1560

Some of those exiles settled in **Geneva**. There a group of English scholars, led by **William Whittingham**, produced a New Testament in 1557 and a complete Bible in 1560, dedicated to Elizabeth I.

The **Geneva Bible** was a new kind of book for English readers:

- It was the first English Bible translated throughout from Hebrew and Greek, revising earlier English versions against the originals.
- It was the first complete English Bible with **numbered verses**, following those Robert Estienne had introduced in his Greek New Testament of 1551.
- It was printed in clear **roman type**, usually in an affordable quarto, with maps, woodcuts, chapter summaries and cross-references.
- It had extensive **marginal notes**, explaining hard words, giving alternative renderings and offering Reformed interpretation.

It became the household Bible of English Protestants for about eighty years: the Bible of Shakespeare, whose allusions after his earliest plays most often match Geneva, of John Bunyan, and of the Puritans on the *Mayflower*. It is nicknamed the **Breeches Bible**, because in Genesis 3:7 Adam and Eve "made themselves breeches," where the World English Bible has "made coverings for themselves."

A few of its notes could be read as allowing disobedience to rulers. James I later called some of them "seditious," and that was one reason he backed a new translation.

## The Bishops' Bible, 1568

The bishops did not want Geneva's notes in their pulpits, but the Great Bible was clearly inferior. Archbishop **Matthew Parker** organised a revision of it by bishops and scholars, published in 1568 and revised in 1572. This **Bishops' Bible** became the official Bible read in churches.

It was uneven, because different revisers worked with different care, and private readers kept buying Geneva Bibles. But it mattered for one reason above all: the King James translators were told to take it as their base (see [The King James Version](/articles/the-king-james-version/)).

## Rheims and Douai: the Catholic translation

English Catholics in exile also produced a Bible. At the English College at Douai, moved for a time to Rheims, the scholar **Gregory Martin** translated the whole Bible. The New Testament was published at **Rheims** in 1582, and the Old Testament at **Douai** in 1609–10.

It was translated from the Latin **Vulgate**, which the Council of Trent had declared authentic (see [Jerome and the Latin Vulgate](/articles/jerome-and-the-vulgate/)), though Martin compared the Greek and Hebrew. It kept close to the Latin even at the cost of smoothness, with words like *pasch* for Passover and, in the Lord's Prayer, "supersubstantial bread" where the World English Bible has "our daily bread." Its notes answered Protestant arguments, as Geneva's answered Catholic ones.

It has often been dismissed as stiff, but it was careful, and the King James translators consulted it; their New Testament sometimes follows its vocabulary, as James Carleton showed in 1902. Bishop **Richard Challoner**'s eighteenth-century revision remained the standard English Catholic Bible into the twentieth century.

## What the King James translators inherited

By 1604 there were three main English Bibles in use: the Bishops' Bible in the churches, the Geneva Bible in homes, and the Rheims New Testament among Catholics. All but Rheims rested heavily on Tyndale. They had been made by exiles and bishops, Puritans and Catholics, sometimes under persecution and sometimes under royal licence. The same Greek word could be "church" or "congregation," "elder" or "priest," depending on who translated and why, and a Geneva reader and a Rheims reader met very different explanations in the margin. When the King James translators wrote that they meant "to make a good one better," it was this line of Bibles they had in mind.
