"Repent" is the first word of John the Baptist's preaching in Matthew, and the first word of Jesus's:

> {{OWB:MAT.3.1.s1:B}}

It is also one of the most misunderstood words in the Bible. To many English speakers "repent" means to feel bad about something, to be sorry, perhaps to say so in public. In older English it could suggest a religious duty, the sinner kneeling in sackcloth. The Greek and Hebrew words behind it say something larger and simpler: change your mind, turn around, come back.

This article looks at those words, the history of how they were translated, and what Open Word Bible does at each of its three levels.

## The Greek: a change of mind

The New Testament verb is {{LEMMA:ACT.3.19:1}} (*metanoeō*), and the noun is *metanoia*. They are built from *meta*, "after" or "change", and *nous*, "mind". In ordinary Greek they meant to change one's mind or think again, often with regret for what one had thought before.

In the New Testament the change goes deeper than an opinion. The meaning decision at Matthew 3:2 defines the word as "change one's mind and way of life, turning back to God", and adds: "In the prophets' call to Israel to return to God, it is a turning of the whole life, which John asks to see in deeds (3:8)." The two alternatives it sets aside are "feel sorry" and "do penance".

The two words occur 56 times in the New Testament, most of them in Luke, Acts and Revelation. In Acts the decisions stress the turning: "Repentance in Acts is turning to God (14:15; 26:20). For these hearers it means leaving idols" (17:30).

Paul shows that the word is not the same as sorrow. Writing to the Corinthians after a painful letter, he sets two kinds of grief side by side:

> {{OWB:2CO.7.10.s1:B}}

Grief can lead to repentance, but it is not the same thing. The meaning decision describes the other kind as "sorrow that has no turning to God in it: remorse, self-pity or bitterness."

Matthew makes the same distinction with a different verb. After Jesus is condemned, Judas is filled with regret:

> {{OWB:MAT.27.3.s1:B}}

Older translations said "Judas repented himself". The record points out that the Greek verb here, *metamelomai*, means to feel sorry afterward about what one has done, and "is not the word for repentance in 3:2 and 4:17". It adds: "Judas regrets his act, but the story does not say he turned to God."

## The Hebrew: turn back

The Old Testament has no single noun for repentance. It uses a verb of movement: {{LEMMA:EZK.18.30:11}} (*shuv*), "turn", "return", "go back". It is one of the commonest verbs in the Hebrew Bible, used more than a thousand times in the books drafted so far, most of them for ordinary going and coming back: Noah's dove does not return; dust returns to dust. But when the prophets call Israel to "return" to the LORD, the same verb becomes the word for repentance. The meaning decision at 1 Kings 8:33 says so directly: "The verb 'turn' or 'return' is the main word for repentance in the Old Testament (Deuteronomy 30:2)."

The picture is of someone walking the wrong way who stops, turns around and comes home. Hosea's last chapter opens with it:

> {{OWB:HOS.14.2.s1:B}}

The decision explains that the preposition means "all the way to": turning back "to God completely", not making a gesture in his direction. Amos uses the same words as a refrain. After each disaster in chapter 4 comes the line "Yet you did not return to me, declares the LORD."

Ezekiel puts two forms of the verb together:

> {{OWB:EZK.18.30.s1:B}}

The Literal level keeps the Hebrew picture, "Turn, and turn away from all your transgressions." The meaning decision says that the two forms together "call for a full change of life".

## Sorrow, penance and turning

How did "repent" come to mean something narrower? Part of the answer lies in Latin. Jerome's Vulgate rendered John the Baptist's call *paenitentiam agite*, which can mean "repent" but was long understood in the church as "do penance": perform the acts of satisfaction that the sacrament of penance required. The Catholic Douay-Rheims translation of 1582 made Matthew 3:2 "Do penance".

Humanist scholars questioned this. Erasmus, in the Latin translation he published beside his Greek New Testament, preferred a word meaning "come to your senses". Martin Luther built his first thesis of 1517 on the same verse: when Jesus said "Repent", "he willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance." Modern Catholic translations say "Repent", and Christians across traditions now largely agree on what the Greek word means, even where they differ about the practice of confession and penance.

English "repent" itself comes from French and Latin words for regret, and its everyday sense has always leaned toward sorrow. That is why many teachers point out that the biblical word is more about a change of direction than a change of feeling, though feeling is often part of it.

## What Open Word Bible does

The three levels answer the problem in different ways.

**Literal and Balanced say "repent" and "repentance".** The word is still current English, it is the word of the church's preaching, and it lets a reader follow one Greek word through the New Testament. Of the 56 places, the Balanced level uses "repent" or "repentance" in almost all; a few say "turn" where the context names what one turns from, as when Revelation speaks of turning away "from her sexual immorality".

**Readable never says "repent" in the New Testament.** It says what the word means, in a phrase a young or new reader understands. John the Baptist's call reads:

> {{OWB:MAT.3.1.s1:R}}

The same pattern runs through the Gospels and Acts: "turn away from your sins and come back to God", "turn away from their sins", "turns away from sin and comes back to God". The rendering decision at 2 Peter 3:9 states the aim: Readable "explains repentance: a change of mind and life, turning from sin to God." Both halves matter. "Turn away from your sins" alone could sound like self-improvement; "come back to God" names the One the turning is toward, as the Hebrew does.

Luke 15 shows the difference between the levels. In the parables of the lost sheep and the lost coin, Jesus says:

> {{OWB:LUK.15.7.s1:B}}

At the Readable level the sinner "turns away from sin and comes back to God", and the ninety-nine "do not need to turn back". The meaning decision gives the sense, "turning from sin back to God", and the rejected alternative, "feeling sorry".

**When Greek has two verbs, the levels keep both.** In Acts 3:19 Peter says "repent and turn back", using both *metanoeō* and a second verb, "turn". Balanced says "Repent, then, and turn around"; Readable joins them in one plain phrase, "turn away from your sins and come back to God", and its decision says it "says what both verbs mean together."

**In the Old Testament, "return" stays "return".** The records keep the Hebrew picture at most levels, "return to the LORD", "turn back", because the movement is the meaning. Readable sometimes says "come back" or adds "to me" to make the direction plain, as in Ezekiel 18:30. Balanced uses "repent" for *shuv* only in a few places, such as Ezekiel 14:6 and 18:30, where the call is unmistakably a call to change one's life.

## When God "repents"

One more word needs care. The King James Version says that "it repented the LORD that he had made man" (Genesis 6:6) and that God "repented of the evil" he had threatened against Nineveh (Jonah 3:10). The Hebrew verb in these verses is not *shuv* but *naḥam*, to be sorry, to relent, to change one's course. God does not turn from sin.

Open Word Bible does not use "repent" for God. Genesis 6:6 says the LORD "regretted" making human beings, and the record explains that this "shows that human evil truly grieves God; it does not mean he made a mistake or did not foresee it." In Jonah:

> {{OWB:JON.3.10.s1:B}}

The decision sets this beside the verses that say God does not change his mind as a human does (Numbers 23:19; 1 Samuel 15:29): "Both speak of the same God: his purpose stands, and he answers those who turn to him." Nineveh turned, and God relented. The two verbs are different, and the translation keeps them apart.

## A word that points somewhere

Repentance in the Bible is not chiefly about how bad one feels. It is about where one is going. John the Baptist asked for fruit that showed it; the prophets asked Israel to come home; Jesus told of a shepherd who goes looking for the sheep and a father who runs to meet his son. The Literal and Balanced levels keep the church's word, "repent", and the Readable level spells out what it has always meant: turning away from sin and coming back to God.
