The earliest manuscripts of the New Testament were written in capital letters, often with no spaces between words, very little punctuation and no quotation marks at all. The commas, full stops, question marks and quotation marks in every modern Bible, in every language, were put there by editors and translators.

Most of the time this raises no difficulty. The grammar makes it plain where one sentence ends and the next begins. But now and then a comma decides what a verse says. Open Word Bible records these choices as meaning decisions in the category *punctuation*, and marks the hard ones uncertain. It usually follows the punctuation of the SBL Greek New Testament, the edition it translates, but the editors' commas are a judgment too, and the record says so when the translation reads them differently.

## Romans 9:5: a comma or a full stop?

Paul lists what belongs to Israel: the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the law, the worship, the promises, the patriarchs, and finally the Messiah himself. Then come these words:

> {{GREEK:ROM.9.5:7-21}}

Word for word: "the Christ according to the flesh, the one being over all, God blessed to the ages, amen." Where you put the punctuation changes who is called God.

- With a **comma** after "flesh", the rest describes the Messiah: "the Messiah, who is God over all, blessed forever."
- With a **full stop** after "flesh", the rest becomes a separate praise of God: "May God, who is over all, be blessed forever."

The SBL edition has the comma, and so does the translation:

> {{OWB:ROM.9.3.s1:B}}

The record gives three reasons. The words "who is" most naturally refer to the person just named. "According to the flesh" leads the reader to expect what the Messiah is beyond his human descent. And when Paul blesses God in a separate sentence, "blessed" normally comes first ("Blessed be the God and Father", 2 Corinthians 1:3), while here it follows the one just named, as in Romans 1:25.

The record also states the other side honestly. Paul nowhere else plainly calls Christ "God", and some manuscripts and many readers put a full stop after "flesh". So the decision is marked *uncertain*, and a footnote gives the alternatives.

## John 1:3: where does the sentence end?

Sometimes Open Word Bible reads the punctuation differently from its own Greek edition. John 1:3 ends with two words, {{GREEK:JHN.1.3:11-12}}, "what has come into being". They can close verse 3 or open verse 4:

- "Nothing came into being that has come into being. In him was life."
- "Nothing came into being. What has come into being in him was life."

The SBL edition prints the second, and several early Christian writers read it that way. The translation takes the first, which most English Bibles also follow:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.3.s1:B}}

The record's reason is simple: "what has come into being in him was life" is hard to make sense of. It marks the decision uncertain, says plainly that the SBL edition punctuates the other way, and gives that reading in a footnote.

## Luke 23:43: "today"

One of the criminals crucified with Jesus asks to be remembered when Jesus comes into his kingdom. Jesus answers:

> {{GREEK:LUK.23.43:4-13}}

Word for word: "Truly to you I say today with me you will be in the paradise." The word "today" sits between the two halves. A comma before it gives "Truly I tell you, today you will be with me". A comma after it gives "Truly I tell you today, you will be with me", which some translations print.

Open Word Bible puts "today" with the promise:

> {{OWB:LUK.23.43.s1:B}}

The record notes that Jesus's many sayings beginning "Truly I tell you" never add "today" to those opening words, and that Luke often uses "today" for salvation that arrives now: "today a Savior is born" (2:11), "today this Scripture is fulfilled" (4:21), "today salvation has come to this house" (19:9). The decision is not marked uncertain, but a footnote gives the other punctuation.

## 1 Corinthians: whose words are these?

Quotation marks are punctuation too, and Paul's letters raise a particular problem with them. In 1 Corinthians, Paul answers a letter the church had written to him (7:1), and at times he seems to quote its slogans back before answering them. Nothing in the Greek marks where a quotation starts or stops.

The clearest case is 6:12. The words "all things are permitted to me" come twice in the verse and twice more in 10:23, each time followed by Paul's "but". Taken as Paul's own rule with no limit, they would contradict what he has just said in verses 9 and 10. So the record marks them as a saying used in Corinth, and the translation puts them in quotation marks:

> {{OWB:1CO.6.12.s1:B}}

The next case is harder. In 7:1, "it is good for a man not to touch a woman" may be a saying from the Corinthians' letter, which verses 2 to 5 then argue against. But Paul also says that staying unmarried is good (verses 7–8), so he may agree with it in part. The translation marks it as a quotation, flags the decision uncertain, and gives the other view in a footnote.

## John 3: where does Jesus stop speaking?

In John 3, Jesus speaks with Nicodemus. At some point the words may stop being Jesus's and become John's own comment, and the manuscripts do not say where.

Some scholars end the quotation at verse 15. From verse 16 the verbs move to the past ("God loved", "he gave"), Nicodemus is not addressed again, and some of the phrases are ones John uses in his own voice elsewhere. Others carry it on to the end of verse 21. Verse 16 begins with "For", tying it to what Jesus has just said, and the themes run on without a break.

Open Word Bible keeps the quotation open to the end of verse 21, marks the decision uncertain, and gives the other view in a footnote. The same question comes up again in John 3:31–36. See ["For God so loved the world"](/articles/how-god-loved-the-world/) for the rest of that verse.

## Why it is a meaning decision

Punctuation might look like a question of style, the kind of thing each language could settle for itself. It is not. Whether Romans 9:5 calls the Messiah God, or whether a saying in 1 Corinthians is Paul's own view or one he is correcting, is a question about what the author meant, and every language has to give the same answer. So the decision is made once, in the meaning layer, and every translation follows it. Where the decision is close, every reader in every language sees the same uncertain label and the same footnote.

To see one of these decisions for yourself, open [Romans 9](/rom/9/) or [Luke 23](/luk/23/) and tap the verse.
