Most Western Christians know the story of the Bible in Greek and Latin. Fewer know that a third great tradition grew up at the same time, east of the Roman world, in a language close to the one Jesus spoke. That language is **Syriac**, and its Bible, the **Peshitta**, is still read in churches from the Middle East to southern India.

## Edessa and the first Syriac Gospels

Syriac is a dialect of Aramaic, the language family that also produced the Jewish [Targums](/articles/the-targums/). It was the local language of **Edessa**, now Şanlıurfa in south-eastern Turkey, and became the literary language of Christians from Syria to Persia. There was a Christian community in Edessa by the late second century.

The first gospel book many Syriac Christians knew was not four Gospels but one. Around 170 CE **Tatian**, a former pupil of Justin Martyr, wove the four into a single narrative, the **Diatessaron** ("through the four"). It was the standard gospel text in Syriac churches for two centuries or more. In the fifth century Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, wrote that he had found more than two hundred copies in his churches and replaced them with the four separate Gospels. No complete copy survives in Syriac.

The separate Gospels were translated from Greek by about the third century. This **Old Syriac** version survives in only two manuscripts. One has a remarkable story. In 1892 two Scottish twin sisters, **Agnes Smith Lewis** and **Margaret Dunlop Gibson**, visited St Catherine's Monastery at Mount Sinai. Agnes photographed a palimpsest, a manuscript whose writing had been scraped off so the parchment could be reused, and beneath the later text recognised the Gospels in Syriac. The **Sinaitic palimpsest**, from about 400, is one of the oldest Gospel manuscripts in any language but Greek. Like the oldest Greek manuscripts, it ends Mark at 16:8.

## The Peshitta

By the early fifth century a new standard version had replaced both. It came to be called the **Peshitta**, usually understood as "simple" or "common."

Its **Old Testament** was translated directly from Hebrew, probably in the second century CE. Some scholars, notably Michael Weitzman, think it was begun by a Jewish community; others think it was Christian from the start. Because its translators used Hebrew manuscripts older than the surviving Masoretic ones, textual critics still consult it (see [How textual criticism recovers the text](/articles/how-textual-criticism-works/)).

Its **New Testament** revises the Old Syriac Gospels towards the Greek and adds Acts and the Letters. It was once credited to Rabbula, bishop of Edessa from 411 to 435, but it was in use before his time and has no named translator. It contains **twenty-two books**, lacking 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude and Revelation, which the Syriac churches had not accepted when it was made (see [How the New Testament canon took shape](/articles/gathering-the-new-testament/)). Later, more literal Syriac versions, above all the **Harklean** of 616, supplied the missing five.

## East to India and China

After the controversies of the fifth century, Syriac Christianity divided into the **Church of the East**, centred in Persia, and the **Syriac Orthodox Church**, centred in Roman Syria. Both kept the Peshitta.

The Church of the East became one of the great missionary churches of the first millennium. The **St Thomas Christians** of Kerala in southern India had links with it from at least the early medieval period and used the Syriac liturgy and the Peshitta. In the 1620s a stone erected in 781 was dug up near **Xi'an**, the old Chinese capital. The **Xi'an stele** records, in Chinese with some Syriac, that a missionary named **Alopen** arrived in 635 bringing scriptures, and that the emperor had them translated. None of those early Chinese scriptures survives in full.

## Is the Peshitta the original?

The Peshitta is sometimes said to preserve the original words of the New Testament, because Jesus spoke Aramaic. Some in the Church of the East hold this view. Most scholars, including scholars of Syriac, disagree. The New Testament books were written in Greek, the Peshitta agrees with the Greek manuscript tradition and shows signs of translation from it, and it grew out of earlier Syriac versions that can be traced. Syriac is also a later dialect than the Galilean Aramaic Jesus spoke.

For Open Word Bible, which translates the SBL Greek New Testament and the Westminster Leningrad Codex, the Syriac versions are among the witnesses weighed when a reasoning record discusses a textual question. In [Genesis 4:8](/gen/4/), for example, the Syriac is one of the versions that gives Cain words the Hebrew lacks, and the reasoning record says so.
