In the most holy room of Israel's sanctuary stood one piece of furniture: the ark, a wooden chest covered with gold, holding the tablets of the covenant. On top of it lay a slab of pure gold with two gold cherubim, their wings spread over it. English Bibles have called that slab "the mercy seat" for nearly five hundred years. Others call it "the atonement cover", "the atonement lid" or simply "the lid". The choice matters more than it seems, because the same family of words runs from the lid of the ark to the heart of the New Testament's teaching about the cross.

## The lid of the ark

God gives Moses the instructions in Exodus 25:

> {{OWB:EXO.25.17.s1:B}}

The Hebrew word is {{LEMMA:EXO.25.17:2}} (*kapporet*). It comes from the same root as the verb *kipper*, "make atonement". The meaning decision for this verse explains that the word "names the solid gold lid laid on the ark", and that "it is more than a cover." Two things happened there.

First, God met with his people there:

> {{OWB:EXO.25.22.s1:B}}

The lid is the place where God is present, "enthroned above the cherubim" as other verses say (1 Samuel 4:4; Psalm 99:1). The record adds a warning against one misreading of the English name: "it is not a seat to sit on."

Second, it was the place of atonement. Once a year, on the Day of Atonement, the high priest went behind the curtain with the blood of the sin offerings:

> {{OWB:LEV.16.14.s1:B}}

So important was the lid that the inner room of Solomon's temple could be called "the house of the mercy seat" (1 Chronicles 28:11).

## Where "mercy seat" came from

The name "mercy seat" was coined by William Tyndale in his translation of the Pentateuch in 1530, following Martin Luther's German *Gnadenstuhl*, "throne of grace". It is not a literal rendering of the Hebrew, but it is a good interpretation: the place where God sat enthroned and where his mercy was shown in the forgiveness of sins. The King James Version kept it, and so have many translations since.

Its weakness is that it says nothing about atonement, which is the root of the Hebrew word, and that "seat" can mislead. So other translations have tried to say more. The New International Version says "atonement cover"; the NET Bible says "atonement lid". These are closer to the Hebrew root, but they are less familiar, and "cover" can suggest that the word simply means "lid", which is only half of it.

## What Open Word Bible does with the lid

The records treat the lid in three ways.

- **Literal and Balanced keep "mercy seat"** in Exodus, Numbers and 1 Chronicles. The rendering decision at Exodus 25:17 gives the reason: the traditional name "goes back to William Tyndale's translation of 1530", readers know it, and it "points to what the lid is for". A footnote gives "atonement cover" and "place of atonement".
- **In Leviticus 16, Literal says "the cover"**, closer to the Hebrew, while Balanced keeps "the mercy seat". The meaning decision there explains the lid in full: the ark, the gold slab, the cherubim, God speaking "from above it", and its link to atonement.
- **Readable says what it is.** The first time, it gives a short explanation:

> {{OWB:EXO.25.17.s1:R}}

After that Readable says "the lid" in the same passage, repeats the explanation when the lid comes up again in a later chapter, and in Leviticus 16 says "the cover of the ark".

## What "atonement" means

The verb behind *kapporet* is *kipper*, usually translated "make atonement". In the drafts it occurs about a hundred times, nearly half of them in Leviticus, and "make atonement" is its usual rendering at every level.

The English word itself has an interesting history. "Atonement" was built from "at one": to make two parties at one, to reconcile them. Tyndale did much to bring it into the English Bible. But what does the Hebrew verb mean? Scholars have offered three main pictures, and the records keep all three in view.

**To cover.** Jacob, afraid of meeting Esau, sends gifts ahead to "cover his face", that is, to appease him, so that he no longer sees the wrong (Genesis 32:21). The meaning decision there notes that the verb "is the one later used for atonement, covering or wiping away an offense." Some Psalms speak of God "covering" sin: "you cover over our rebellions" (Psalm 65:4, Balanced).

**To wipe clean.** Leviticus uses the verb for cleansing the sanctuary itself from the people's impurity (16:16), and it states the purpose plainly:

> {{OWB:LEV.16.30.s1:B}}

The decision explains: "atonement wipes away sin like a stain, so that the people are clean."

**To ransom.** A related noun, *kopher*, means a ransom, the price paid for a life (Exodus 30:12). The central verse on the blood of sacrifice joins life to life:

> {{OWB:LEV.17.11.s1:B}}

Here the record marks a real question. The small Hebrew word in "by means of the life" can also mean "in exchange for": the blood, as a life, makes atonement for a life. "Both readings join the life in the blood to the life of the worshiper." The decision is marked uncertain and has a footnote.

The decision at Leviticus 1:4 draws these together: to make atonement is to "put right what stands between the worshiper and God", which "covers wiping away the stain of sin and impurity (16:30), and paying a ransom for a life (Exodus 30:12-16); the blood given on the altar is the means (17:11)."

Readable sometimes puts the result into plain words. When the coal touches Isaiah's lips, Literal and Balanced keep the temple word, "atonement is made for your sin", and Readable says "Your sin is forgiven" (Isaiah 6:7).

## The mercy seat in the New Testament

The Greek translation of the Old Testament rendered *kapporet* with *hilastērion*, a word meaning a place or means of atonement. Hebrews uses it for the lid of the ark, and the records translate it "mercy seat" there (Hebrews 9:5). Paul uses the same word once, of Christ:

> {{OWB:ROM.3.25.s1:B}}

This is one of the most debated verses in the New Testament. The word {{LEMMA:ROM.3.25:5}} can be read in three main ways.

- **The mercy seat.** Since the Greek Old Testament and Hebrews use the word for the lid of the ark, Paul may be saying that Christ is the new mercy seat, the place where God meets sinners and atonement is made, now set out in public instead of hidden behind a curtain. Some translations put "mercy seat" in a note.
- **Propitiation.** The King James Version and the English Standard Version say "propitiation": a sacrifice that turns away God's anger. Paul has spoken of God's wrath since 1:18, and Leon Morris and others argued that the word family keeps that sense.
- **Expiation.** The Revised Standard Version said "expiation": a sacrifice that wipes away sin. C. H. Dodd argued in the 1930s that in the Greek Bible the words are about removing sin, not appeasing God.

The New International Version's "sacrifice of atonement" tries to hold both sides. Open Word Bible says "the means of atonement" at Literal and Balanced, and its meaning decision says why: the word is the one used for the mercy seat, "where blood was sprinkled on the Day of Atonement", and "atonement in the Old Testament both wipes away sin and turns away God's wrath, which Paul has spoken of since 1:18. The levels keep both." The decision is marked uncertain and has a footnote listing the other renderings. Readable spells out both sides, "the sacrifice that takes away sins and turns away God's anger", and its decision asks reviewers to check that this does not narrow the meaning.

## Atoning sacrifice

A related noun appears twice in 1 John:

> {{OWB:1JN.4.10.s1:B}}

Here Literal and Balanced say "atoning sacrifice". The rendering decision explains why not "propitiation" or "expiation": they "are unfamiliar to most readers and each takes one side of the debate". The meaning decision adds what matters most in this verse: "the sacrifice comes from God's own love: it is God's gift, not something that wins over an unwilling God."

The verb from the same family appears at Hebrews 2:17, where Jesus becomes a high priest "making atonement for the sins of the people", and in the prayer of the tax collector in the temple, "God, be merciful to me, the sinner!" (Luke 18:13). The record notes that this is "not the usual word for mercy": standing in the temple at the hour of sacrifice, the man asks God to deal with his sin and accept him.

## One thread

Read together, these words tell one story. God gave Israel a place where he would meet them and where their sin would be dealt with. The New Testament says that what happened once a year behind the curtain has now happened once for all, in public, in Christ. The words for it are hard, and each English rendering catches part of their meaning. The records keep the traditional names where readers know them, say plainly where the meaning is debated, and set out the alternatives so that a reader can weigh them.
