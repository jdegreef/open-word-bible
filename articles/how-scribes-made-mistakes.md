Copy a page of text by hand, carefully, and have someone check it. You will probably find a word left out, a line skipped, or a letter wrong. Ancient scribes were far more practised than most of us, but they were human, and they made the same kinds of mistakes.

That might sound worrying. In fact it is one of the reasons we can recover the text with confidence. Copying errors fall into recognisable patterns, and once you know them you can often see exactly how a mistake happened and which reading came first. Here are the main kinds, with examples from both Testaments.

## Mistakes of the eye

Most copying errors happen as the scribe's eye moves between the exemplar and the new copy.

### Skipping: same ending, same beginning

The most common major error is caused by two words or lines that end (or begin) the same way. The scribe copies up to the first one, looks back at the exemplar, and the eye lands on the second. Everything between is lost. When the similar endings cause it, this is called **homoeoteleuton** ("same ending"); with similar beginnings, **homoeoarcton**. The general term for leaving something out by accident is **haplography** ("writing once").

A clear New Testament example is **1 John 2:23**: "Whoever denies the Son, the same doesn't have the Father. He who confesses the Son has the Father also." In Greek, both halves end with the same words, "the Father." Many later manuscripts skip from the first "the Father" to the second and leave out the second half of the verse. The King James translators of 1611 included the words but printed them in italics, the type they used for words they had supplied. The earliest manuscripts have them, and Open Word Bible prints them with a footnote ([1 John 2](/1jn/2/)).

An Old Testament example is **1 Samuel 14:41**. In the Masoretic text, Saul's prayer is very short: literally, "Give a perfect lot" (the World English Bible has "Show the right"). The Greek Septuagint has a much longer prayer, which in a literal rendering runs: "LORD God of Israel, why have you not answered your servant today? If the fault is in me or in my son Jonathan, give Urim; but if it is in your people Israel, give Thummim." The longer text contains "Israel" twice; a scribe's eye seems to have jumped from the first to the second, dropping the words between. Many modern translations restore the longer reading.

### Repeating

The opposite mistake is **dittography** ("writing twice"): the eye goes back, and a letter, word or line is repeated. The result is usually obvious nonsense, easily corrected.

### Letters that look alike

Some letters are easily confused, especially in a worn or poorly written exemplar. In Hebrew square script, *dalet* and *resh*, which differ only by a tiny corner, are notoriously alike, as are *waw* and *yod*. One result can be seen by comparing parallel passages in the Old Testament. **Genesis 10:4** names a grandson of Japheth as "Dodanim"; the parallel list in **1 Chronicles 1:7** has "Rodanim." The only difference is a *dalet* or a *resh*. The Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch support "Rodanim" in Genesis too, and the name may refer to the people of Rhodes.

Greek manuscripts were written in capitals without spaces between words, so letters could be confused and words divided in the wrong place.

## Mistakes of the ear and the memory

### Sounds alike

Scribes sometimes wrote from dictation, or spoke the words under their breath as they copied, so words that sounded alike were confused. In later Greek several vowels came to be pronounced alike (**itacism**), and the words for "we" and "you" (plural) are constantly interchanged in manuscripts.

A famous example is **Romans 5:1**. Some manuscripts read "we have peace with God," others "let us have peace with God." The two Greek words differ only in one vowel, a short *o* or a long *o*, which were pronounced alike. Here "let us have" actually has strong early support, but most scholars think "we have" fits Paul's argument better and is original.

### Memory and familiarity

Scribes who knew the Bible well might write what they remembered instead of what was on the page. In the Gospels, parallel passages invite **harmonisation**. The best-known case is the **Lord's Prayer**. Luke's version (Luke 11:2–4) is shorter than Matthew's (Matthew 6:9–13): it begins simply "Father," and lacks "your will be done" and "deliver us from evil." Many later manuscripts of Luke add those phrases from the more familiar Matthew, and the King James Version has the longer form in Luke.

Similarly, the doxology "For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen" at the end of Matthew 6:13 is absent from the earliest manuscripts of Matthew. A similar form appears in the *Didache*, an early church manual, and it seems to have entered Matthew from use in worship.

## Changes made on purpose

Some changes were deliberate, usually made with good intentions.

**Correcting an apparent error.** **Mark 1:2–3** introduces a quotation, in the earliest manuscripts, with "As it is written in Isaiah the prophet," but the quotation combines words from Malachi 3:1 with Isaiah 40:3. Many later manuscripts change this to "in the prophets," which avoids the difficulty. "In Isaiah the prophet" is almost certainly what Mark wrote.

**Clarifying.** Scribes sometimes wrote "Jesus said" where the original had only "he said." Such additions are easy to spot, because different manuscripts add different words in different places.

**Theological changes.** In a small number of places, scribes seem to have changed wording to protect or emphasise a belief about Christ. Bart Ehrman argues in *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* that such changes were more common than once thought; others think he overstates the case. Either way, they are detectable, because we have so many manuscripts to compare.

## Errors that are hard to see

Some errors leave the text grammatical and sensible. One example is **1 Thessalonians 2:7**. Paul says that he and his companions were either "gentle among you" or "infants among you." The two Greek words differ by a single letter, *n*, and the preceding word ends in that same letter, so a scribe could easily add or drop it. The earliest manuscripts mostly read "infants," but many scholars think "gentle" fits the comparison to a nursing mother that follows. Translations divide, and honest ones show their reasoning.

## How we catch them

Each kind of error points to a rule of thumb. A reading that could have come from a skip is probably the shorter, mistaken one. A reading that matches a parallel passage is probably the harmonised one. A reading that smooths a difficulty is probably the scribe's correction. Put simply, **the reading that best explains how the others arose is most likely to be original.** Weighed together with the age and spread of the manuscripts, that is the method described in [How textual criticism works](/articles/how-textual-criticism-works/).

Most mistakes leave fingerprints. With thousands of manuscripts to compare, a slip made in one copy is usually absent from many others. The errors of individual scribes do not disappear; they are caught, and they become evidence.
