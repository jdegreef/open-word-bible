People read the Bible for different reasons. A student comparing a verse with the Greek wants to see its structure. Someone reading a chapter aloud in church wants natural English. A new reader, or a child, wants plain words. One translation cannot serve all three equally well, so Open Word Bible gives every sentence at three levels.

Most English Bibles choose one point between word for word and meaning for meaning, and readers who want another point buy another Bible. The difference here is that the three levels are made together, from the same decisions, so you can move between them and know they agree. The article [Word for word or meaning for meaning?](/articles/word-for-word-or-meaning-for-meaning/) tells the longer story of that old debate.

## The three levels

**Literal** follows the original's wording and order wherever English allows. Words that English needs but the original does not have are shown in [brackets], so you can see exactly what was added. It keeps the original's long sentences and repeated words.

**Balanced** reads like ordinary modern English while staying close to the original. One clause of the original usually becomes one clause of English, and nothing is added that is not implied. It is the default, and the level meant for reading aloud.

**Readable** uses plain language, short sentences and everyday words, at about a 6th to 8th grade reading level. It explains idioms rather than reproducing them, and may say plainly what the context implies.

## One sentence, three ways

Here is Philippians 4:4 at each level.

> **Literal:** {{OWB:PHP.4.4.s1:L}}

> **Balanced:** {{OWB:PHP.4.4.s1:B}}

> **Readable:** {{OWB:PHP.4.4.s1:R}}

The Literal level shows "[the] Lord" in brackets, because the Greek has no article there. Balanced breaks the sentence in two and lets the repeated command land on its own. The Readable level says "be full of joy" because "rejoice" has become mostly church language. The record explains that choice, as it explains every other. All three keep what the record decides the sentence means: a command, not a greeting (the same Greek word was an everyday "farewell"), and a joy whose ground is the Lord.

## A longer sentence

The levels grow further apart as the sentences grow longer. Philippians 4:6–7 is a single sentence in Greek. Literal keeps it that way:

> {{OWB:PHP.4.6.s1:L}}

Balanced makes it three English sentences and turns "let your requests be made known" into an active verb:

> {{OWB:PHP.4.6.s1:B}}

Readable goes further. It says what "prayer and petition" means in practice, and it spells out the phrase "in Christ Jesus":

> {{OWB:PHP.4.6.s1:R}}

That last step is the kind the rules watch most closely. "Because you are joined to" adds a reason that the Greek leaves implicit. The record therefore has a decision for it, explains that "in Christ" describes being united to him, and marks the decision *uncertain* so that reviewers will check whether Readable has said more than Paul did. Every expansion that adds a reason, a subject or a connection must be recorded in this way.

## The rule that holds them together

The levels may differ in how much they say, but never in what they mean. Readable may spell out what Literal leaves implicit. A level may keep an ambiguity that the original has. But no level may choose a different reading from the others. If the Greek could mean two things and we have decided it means one, all three levels follow that decision; the note gives the other.

John 1:16 shows the difference between keeping an ambiguity and choosing against it. The Greek says, word for word, "grace for grace". The record decides it means one gift of grace following another, and marks the decision uncertain, because the small word in the middle often means "in place of". Literal keeps the open phrase, which can bear either reading:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.16.s1:L}}

Balanced and Readable follow the decision:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.16.s1:R}}

What no level may do is say "grace in place of grace". That would choose the other reading, and the levels would no longer agree. The article [Grace upon grace](/articles/grace-upon-grace/) sets out both sides.

## Words supplied, and words kept

Sometimes a level needs a word the original does not have. In John 1:18 the Greek speaks of "[the] one and only God". Said in plain English as "the only God", that would suggest the Father is not God. Balanced and Readable supply "Son", as verse 14 does, and a rendering decision says so:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.18.s1:B}}

Literal does not supply it, and shows its own added words in brackets.

Key terms work the other way: they never change. Words such as grace, glory, truth, peace, righteousness, faith, joy and salvation keep the same English word at every level, and the software rejects a record that varies them. In John's Gospel the list includes "sign", which is never turned into "miracle", because John uses the word to say that the deeds point beyond themselves. In John 1:14 the record considered "full of unfailing love and faithfulness", which would bring out an echo of God's words in Exodus 34:6. Every level keeps "grace and truth", and the note gives the echo. A reader moving between levels is never left wondering whether a different word means a different idea. Gospel is the one deliberate exception: Readable says "good news", which is what the word means.

## Fair questions

**Is Readable a paraphrase?** It is plainer, but it is held to the same meaning decisions as the other levels, and it may not add application or commentary. Where it explains more than the words say, a decision records it, and a reviewer checks it.

**Which level should I read?** Balanced, if you are not sure. Readable for reading with children or for a first reading of a book. Literal when you want to see how the original is put together, or when you are studying a verse closely with the gloss beside it.

**Why not just print a word-for-word gloss?** The record has one, and it is useful. But a gloss is not English; "In beginning was the word, and the word was toward the God" shows John's shape, not his meaning in our language. Literal is the closest level that still reads as English.

You can switch levels at any time in the reader, and the switch is remembered. The panel beside each verse also shows all three levels together, so you can compare them without leaving your place.
