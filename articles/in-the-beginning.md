The Bible opens with seven Hebrew words:

> {{HEBREW:GEN.1.1:1-7}}

Open Word Bible's Balanced level gives them in the familiar way:

> {{OWB:GEN.1.1.s1:B}}

That familiarity hides more decisions than you might expect. Two of them are close calls, and the record for each verse marks them *uncertain*.

## Where the Bible starts

Genesis 1:1 opens an account that runs to 2:3. It is built in days: on the first three God gives the world its shape, separating light from darkness, the waters above from the waters below, the sea from the dry land; on the next three he fills what he has shaped, with lights, with birds and fish, with animals and human beings. On the seventh day he rests. The account closes with the same pair of words it began with, the heavens and the earth (2:1).

So the first verse is more than the first event. It names the whole subject of what follows, and it names the one who acts. The next thirty verses unfold it, rising to the sixth day and the making of human beings (see ["In our image"](/articles/in-our-image/)). The pair of words goes further still. Near the close of its last book, the Bible returns to them: "I saw a new heaven and a new earth" (Revelation 21:1).

## "In the beginning" or "When God began"?

The first word, {{HEBREW:GEN.1.1:1}}, is two parts written as one: a small prefix meaning "in" and the noun {{LEMMA:GEN.1.1:1.2}}, "beginning". The noun has no article. Strictly it says "in beginning", and that has led some translators to read the verse as a time clause that leads into what follows: "When God began to create the heavens and the earth, the earth was formless and empty..." Genesis 2:4 opens the second account in a similar way, with "in the day that the LORD God made".

Open Word Bible reads verse 1 as a sentence that stands on its own. The record gives two reasons. The Masoretic accents, the ancient marks that guide how the text is read aloud, put a pause after the first word, so that it reads as complete in itself. And the Septuagint, the oldest translation, reads it as a main clause, as John 1:1 does when it echoes it: "In the beginning was the Word". See ["The Word was God"](/articles/the-word-was-god/).

The other reading has a long history too. Rashi, the great Jewish commentator of the eleventh century, argued that the first word is always followed by what it is the beginning *of*, and read the verse as "at the beginning of God's creating". The Jewish Publication Society's 1985 translation, the Tanakh, takes the verse as a time clause, as do several other modern versions.

The difference matters less than it might seem, but it is real. Read as a main clause, verse 1 makes a sweeping statement: God created everything, and the rest of the chapter shows how. Read as a time clause, it sets the scene for verse 3, and verse 2 describes the state of things when God began. Both readings are old, and both are defensible. So the decision is marked uncertain and a footnote gives the other reading. The record adds that read either way, God is there and acting before anything else is described.

The Literal level shows the missing article by putting it in brackets:

> {{OWB:GEN.1.1.s1:L}}

## Created, God, the heavens and the earth

The rest of the verse carries three quieter decisions:

- **Created.** The verb {{LEMMA:GEN.1.1:2}} is used in the Old Testament only of what God does, and never with a material that something is made from. Genesis 1 uses it at three points: the heavens and the earth, the first living creatures (verse 21) and human beings (verse 27). The record is careful not to claim too much. The chapter also uses the everyday verb "made" for God's work, so this verb alone does not settle *how* God created. What the opening verse does settle is that God stands before and above everything that exists.
- **God.** {{HEBREW:GEN.1.1:3}} is plural in form, and elsewhere it can mean "gods". Here its verb is singular, "he created", as it is through the whole chapter, so it names the one God. Hebrew often uses a plural to express greatness.
- **The heavens and the earth.** Naming two opposite ends to mean everything between them is a common Hebrew way of speaking, and Hebrew has no single word for the universe. So Readable says what the pair means and keeps the words: "God created everything: the heavens and the earth".

## Spirit, or wind?

Verse 2 describes how things stood before God first spoke:

> {{OWB:GEN.1.2.s1:B}}

"Without form and empty" translates two rhyming words, {{HEBREW:GEN.1.2:3-4}}. The record notes that the rest of the chapter answers both: on days one to three God gives the world its form, and on days four to six he fills it.

The close call is {{HEBREW:GEN.1.2:9-10}}. The noun {{LEMMA:GEN.1.2:9.2}} means breath, wind or spirit. With "God" it could mean "a mighty wind", since Hebrew sometimes uses "of God" to mean "very great", and after the flood God sends a wind over the waters (8:1).

This is an old reading, not a modern one. Targum Onkelos, the ancient Aramaic translation read in synagogues, says that a wind from before the LORD blew over the face of the waters. Some modern versions say "a wind from God" or "a mighty wind".

Open Word Bible says "the Spirit of God". Elsewhere the phrase is God's own Spirit at work (Exodus 31:3; 1 Samuel 10:10). And the verb that follows, "hovering", is the one used of an eagle over its young in Deuteronomy 32:11. It suits a living presence better than a wind. Psalm 33:6 joins God's word and his breath in the making of the heavens, the same pairing that verses 2 and 3 set side by side. The decision is marked uncertain, and a footnote gives "a wind from God". The Literal level lets you see the shape of the Hebrew under all this:

> {{OWB:GEN.1.2.s1:L}}

One more decision in verse 2 is worth knowing. The verse puts "the earth" before its verb, a Hebrew way of pausing the story to describe a scene. So the record reads it as "the earth *was* formless", how things stood when God spoke, and not "the earth *became* formless", as if an earlier creation had been ruined. The same construction describes a state in 3:1: "the serpent was more cunning".

The "became" reading has a history of its own. In 1814 the Scottish preacher Thomas Chalmers proposed a long gap between verses 1 and 2, to make room for the ages the geologists of his day were describing, and the idea spread widely in the century that followed. The record does not reject it on scientific grounds. It rejects it on the grammar: verse 2 pauses the story to describe a scene, and nothing in it says that anything was ruined.

## "Let there be light"

Verse 3 is the first time God speaks:

> {{OWB:GEN.1.3.s1:L}}

The command and its result are two forms of one verb, "let it be" and "it was", so the report repeats the command word for word. The Literal level keeps that echo. Balanced says "light came into being" and Readable "light began to shine", each giving the result a verb of its own. The pattern runs through the chapter: God speaks, and what he says happens. The record also notes that light comes on the first day and the sun only on the fourth. In this account light comes from God's word, not from the lights in the sky.
