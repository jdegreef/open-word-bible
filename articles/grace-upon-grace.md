Every translation hides its close calls. A committee votes, the majority reading goes into the text, and the reader never learns that the vote was close. Open Word Bible marks such decisions as *uncertain*, explains both sides, and shows a label in the reader. Here are two.

## John 1:16: grace upon grace

Verse 14 has just said that the Word was "full of grace and truth". Verse 16 picks up that fullness: everyone has received from it. Then it ends with {{GREEK:JHN.1.16:10-12}}: literally "grace for grace". The King James Version kept that open wording.

The middle word usually means "in place of". That supports one reading: the grace that came in Christ *replaced* an earlier grace, the grace given through the law of Moses, which verse 17 mentions next. John Chrysostom read it this way in the fourth century, "the new in the place of the old", and many modern commentators agree, among them D. A. Carson in his 1991 commentary on John. Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish writer of the early first century, uses the same word when he says God holds back his first gifts of grace and gives others in their place. On this reading, John is contrasting two stages of God's giving: Moses, and then Jesus Christ.

The other reading is accumulation: one gift of grace following another, grace upon grace. Open Word Bible chose this one, and the record says why: verse 17 does not call the law grace. John sets law and grace side by side, with no "but" between them, and a separate decision on verse 17 keeps it that way. He does not describe the law as a grace now superseded.

> **Balanced:** {{OWB:JHN.1.16.s1:B}}

> **Readable:** {{OWB:JHN.1.16.s1:R}}

Because both readings are defensible, the decision is marked uncertain, and a footnote gives "grace in place of grace". Notice the Literal level:

> **Literal:** {{OWB:JHN.1.16.s1:L}}

The rule for levels is that a level may keep an ambiguity the original has, but may not pick the other side. Literal keeps the open "grace for grace", which can be read either way. It does not say "grace in place of grace".

## John 1:5: overcome, or understand?

John 1:5 says the darkness did not {{GREEK:JHN.1.5:13}}, a form of {{LEMMA:JHN.1.5:13}}. The verb can mean to seize or overpower, or to grasp with the mind. The King James Version said "the darkness comprehended it not"; most modern versions say "has not overcome it".

Open Word Bible says "overcome". John 12:35 uses the same verb of darkness overtaking someone:

> {{OWB:JHN.12.35.s2:B}}

But John may intend both senses, a darkness that neither defeated the light nor understood it. The record marks the decision uncertain and says so in a note.

Two smaller decisions sit beside it, and neither is uncertain. The first verb, "shines", is present tense, though the verses around it tell a story in the past. John is saying the light is still shining, so every level keeps the present. The second verb looks back on the whole history of darkness opposing the light. Balanced uses the English perfect, "has not overcome", because the result still holds. Readable keeps the picture of a lamp:

## Why say so?

Marking uncertainty can look like weakness. We think it is the opposite. A reader who knows a choice was close reads the verse more carefully, compares the alternatives, and trusts the confident decisions more because the uncertain ones are labelled. It is what any careful scholar would tell you in person. In the reader, an uncertain decision carries a label, and its footnote shows the reading we did not choose, so you can weigh it for yourself.
