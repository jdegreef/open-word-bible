It is easy to tell the history of the Bible as a story that moves from Jerusalem to Rome and then north into Europe. But some of the earliest translations of the Bible were made in Africa. In Egypt, Christians translated the scriptures into **Coptic** from the third century onwards. In the kingdom of Aksum, in what is now Ethiopia and Eritrea, they were translated into **Ge'ez**, probably beginning in the fourth or fifth century. Both translations are still read in worship today.

The New Testament itself records an African reader of scripture: an official of the queen of the Ethiopians, reading Isaiah in his chariot (Acts 8:27–35). His "Ethiopia" was probably Kush, in today's Sudan, but Ethiopian Christians have long claimed him as a forerunner.

## Coptic: the last stage of Egyptian

**Coptic** is the final stage of the ancient Egyptian language. Earlier Egyptian had been written in hieroglyphs and in a cursive script called Demotic, both hard to learn. Coptic used the Greek alphabet, with a few extra letters from Demotic for sounds Greek lacked, and it became the vehicle of Egyptian Christian literature.

Coptic had several regional dialects, and the Bible was translated into a number of them. Two matter most:

- **Sahidic**, the dialect of Upper (southern) Egypt, was the main literary dialect of early Coptic Christianity. Most of the Bible was translated into Sahidic, probably beginning in the third century.
- **Bohairic**, the dialect of the Nile Delta, gradually became the standard and, from around the eleventh century, the liturgical language of the Coptic Orthodox Church.

Some Coptic codices of the fourth and fifth centuries are among the oldest copies of New Testament books in any language other than Greek.

## Monks and memorisation

Coptic translation grew up with Egyptian monasticism. According to Athanasius's *Life of Antony*, the young **Antony** heard the Gospel read in church, took Jesus's command to the rich young man as addressed to himself, and gave away his property. In the fourth century **Pachomius** organised communal monasteries in Upper Egypt. The rules attributed to him required that a monk who could not read be taught to read, and that monks learn by heart at least the New Testament and the Psalter. In such communities the scriptures were heard, recited and copied constantly, in Coptic.

Coptic was used for many other religious books too. The thirteen codices found near **Nag Hammadi** in 1945, copied in the fourth century, contain works such as the *Gospel of Thomas*. They were not part of the Coptic Bible, but they show how much was being translated into Coptic. After the Arab conquest of Egypt in the 640s, Arabic gradually replaced Coptic as the spoken language, and Copts came to read the Bible in Arabic (see [The Bible in Arabic](/articles/the-bible-in-arabic/)).

## Aksum and the conversion of Ezana

The kingdom of **Aksum** was one of the great trading powers of late antiquity, controlling the Red Sea coast and minting its own gold coins. In the fourth century its king, **Ezana**, became a Christian. His early coins carry the pagan crescent and disc; later ones carry the cross. He was one of the first rulers anywhere to put a cross on his coinage.

**Rufinus**, writing around 400, tells the story (*Ecclesiastical History* 10.9–10). Two young Christians from Tyre, **Frumentius** and **Aedesius**, were captured on the Red Sea coast, taken to the royal court, and rose to positions of trust. Frumentius later went to Alexandria and asked Bishop **Athanasius** to send a bishop to Aksum. Athanasius consecrated Frumentius himself. Ethiopian tradition remembers him as Abba Salama, "father of peace." The story has legendary touches, but its core is confirmed by a letter of the emperor Constantius II to the rulers of Aksum about Frumentius, which Athanasius preserved (*Apology to Constantius* 31).

## Translating into Ge'ez

**Ge'ez** is a Semitic language, related to Arabic, Hebrew and the languages of southern Arabia. Its script developed from the ancient South Arabian alphabet. Around the time of Ezana it began to mark vowels by small changes to each consonant, creating the syllabic script still used for Amharic and Tigrinya (see [Alphabets invented for the Bible](/articles/alphabets-for-the-bible/) for other scripts shaped by scripture).

The Bible was translated into Ge'ez from Greek, probably over a long period from the fourth or fifth century to the sixth or seventh. The Old Testament was translated from the [Septuagint](/articles/the-septuagint/), not the Hebrew. Ethiopian tradition credits much of the work to the **Nine Saints**, monks from the Roman world who came in the late fifth century and founded monasteries in the north. Their lives were written centuries later, so the details are uncertain.

From about the thirteenth or fourteenth century, the Ge'ez Bible was revised against Arabic versions used by the Coptic church in Egypt, so manuscripts often mix older and revised readings.

## The Garima Gospels

At the monastery of **Abba Garima**, near Adwa in northern Ethiopia, the monks have kept two ancient illuminated Gospel books. Tradition says Abba Garima, one of the Nine Saints, copied a Gospel book in a single day while the sun stood still.

Scholars long assumed the books were medieval. Radiocarbon tests carried out in Oxford and reported in 2010 suggested dates in the late antique period, perhaps between the fourth and seventh centuries. On any reckoning the **Garima Gospels** are among the oldest surviving illuminated Gospel books in the world and the oldest surviving manuscripts in Ge'ez. Radiocarbon dates the parchment, not the writing, and gives a range rather than a year, so the dates should be held with some caution.

## The broadest canon

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church has the broadest biblical canon of any church. Its list is often counted as eighty-one books, though the count and contents vary between lists.

It includes books found in no other church's Bible. The most famous are **1 Enoch** and **Jubilees**, both written in Jewish circles before the time of Jesus. Fragments of both were found among the [Dead Sea Scrolls](/articles/what-the-dead-sea-scrolls-show/), but the only complete copies of either survive in Ge'ez. The letter of Jude quotes 1 Enoch directly:

> About these also Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied, saying, "Behold, the Lord came with ten thousands of his holy ones." (Jude 14)

Without the Ethiopian church, these books would be known today only in pieces.

## Africa's place in the story

Ge'ez remains the liturgical language of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox churches, and the Coptic church still uses Bohairic alongside Arabic. Christianity has been an African religion from its first centuries, and African translators have carried scripture into their own languages for as long as anyone.

That story continues. Open Word Bible is drafting Swahili alongside English, Spanish, French, Vietnamese and Korean, each from the Hebrew and Greek rather than from English (see [The Bible in Swahili](/articles/the-bible-in-swahili/)). The Swahili work is AI-assisted, and none of it will be marked reviewed until named native-speaking reviewers have checked it.
