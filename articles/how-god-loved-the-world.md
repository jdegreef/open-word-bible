Many English readers know John 3:16 by heart, in the words "For God so loved the world". Open Word Bible's Balanced level reads a little differently:

> {{OWB:JHN.3.16.s1:B}}

The change is deliberate, and the record for the verse explains it. Every decision about the meaning of the verse is shared by every language; the English wording follows from them.

## Where the verse comes

John 3 begins at night. Nicodemus, a Pharisee and a member of the ruling council, comes to Jesus with questions, and Jesus tells him that no one can see the kingdom of God without being born from above. Nicodemus cannot follow. Jesus then turns to an old story from Israel's wilderness years:

> {{OWB:JHN.3.14.s1:B}}

In Numbers 21, poisonous snakes struck the people. God told Moses to set a bronze serpent on a pole, and anyone who had been bitten and looked at it lived. The record for 3:14 explains the comparison: as looking at the lifted serpent brought life, believing in the lifted-up Son brings eternal life. John says plainly that "lifted up" points to the way Jesus would die (12:32–33).

Verse 16 follows directly. It begins with "For", and that small word ties it to the serpent.

## "So" or "in this way"?

The verse opens with {{GREEK:JHN.3.16:1}}. In English, "God so loved the world" is usually heard as "God loved the world so much". But the Greek word normally means "thus" or "in this way", and that is how John uses it elsewhere: the wind blows where it wills, and "so" is everyone born of the Spirit (3:8); Jesus, tired from the journey, sat down "thus" by the well (4:6).

It has also just appeared two verses earlier, in the sentence about the serpent: as Moses lifted it up, {{GREEK:JHN.3.14:10}}, "so", must the Son of Man be lifted up. Read together, the two verses say: this is how God loved the world, by giving the Son to be lifted up. 1 John 4:9 says the same thing in other words:

> {{OWB:1JN.4.9.s1:B}}

## The grammar step by step

The Greek sentence has three parts.

- **"In this way God loved the world."** The adverb comes first for emphasis, and the verb is a simple past: God loved.
- **"That he gave the Son, the one and only."** The link word is {{GREEK:JHN.3.16:8}}. With a past-tense verb, as here ({{GREEK:JHN.3.16:13}}, "he gave"), it states what actually happened. God did not only feel love; his love took the form of a gift.
- **"In order that everyone who believes in him may not perish but may have eternal life."** This is the purpose of the gift.

The Literal level follows that structure:

> {{OWB:JHN.3.16.s1:L}}

## How it has been read

The question is old. Jerome's Latin Bible has *Sic enim dilexit Deus mundum*, and *sic* means "thus", the same word Latin uses for "in this way". The Wycliffe Bible followed the Latin closely: "God louede so the world". William Tyndale, and then the King James Version, wrote "God so loved the world". English "so" can also mean "in this way", as in "do it so", but most readers today hear it as a word of degree, as in "I am so tired".

Modern scholars have looked again. In 1999 Robert Gundry and Russell Howell published a study of this verse's syntax in *Novum Testamentum*, examining how the "so" of 3:16 relates to the comparison in 3:14. Commentators still differ on how much weight to give manner and how much degree.

## Why the decision is marked uncertain

The other reading has real support. The size of the love is plain from what it gave, and readers have long heard "so loved" as "loved so much". The record marks the decision uncertain and gives the familiar wording in a footnote.

That is why Balanced says "God's love for the world was such that". In English, "such" can mean "of this kind" or "so great". It keeps the question open, where "so much" would close it in one direction. It does depart from the familiar wording, and the rendering decision says so.

Readable spells out the "how":

> {{OWB:JHN.3.16.s1:R}}

## The rest of the verse

A few more decisions in the record are worth knowing:

- **The world** is humanity as a whole, the world that did not know its maker (1:10) and loves darkness (3:19). That God loves *this* world is the surprise.
- **One and only** translates {{GREEK:JHN.3.16:12}}, the word older Bibles render "only begotten". It means unique; see ["One and only" or "only begotten"?](/articles/one-and-only-son/). A footnote gives the older wording.
- **His Son.** Many manuscripts read "his Son"; the Greek text Open Word Bible follows reads "the Son". The meaning is the same, so there is no footnote. Literal keeps "the Son", and Balanced and Readable say "his", because English needs it.
- **Gave** includes both sending the Son into the world (verse 17) and giving him up to death, the lifting up of verse 14.
- **Everyone who believes** is already inclusive English, so this sentence needs no gender setting.
- **Perish** is the opposite of eternal life: final ruin, not physical death, since believers still die (11:25). Readable says "be lost", the sense the same verb has in 6:39 and 10:28.

The record also notes a possible echo of Genesis 22, where God tells Abraham to take "your son, your only son, whom you love". Here God gives his own Son. John does not draw the link himself, so this decision too is marked uncertain.

## What follows

Verse 17 says why the Son was sent, and it uses "the world" three times:

> {{OWB:JHN.3.17.s1:B}}

Verse 18 then says that the verdict is already in place: whoever believes is not condemned, and whoever does not believe stands condemned already. The two verses belong together: the Son was sent to save, and the one who refuses him is left under the judgment that already stands.

## Where does Jesus stop speaking?

One more uncertain decision concerns the whole paragraph. The manuscripts have no quotation marks, so no one knows for sure where Jesus's words to Nicodemus end. Some think they end at verse 15, and that verses 16 to 21 are John's own reflection. Open Word Bible keeps the quotation open to the end of verse 21, since nothing in the text marks a break, and gives the other view in a footnote.
