In May 1521, on his way home from the Imperial Diet at Worms, where he had refused to recant his writings, **Martin Luther** was seized by armed horsemen. The "kidnapping" had been arranged by his protector, the Elector **Frederick the Wise** of Saxony. Luther was taken to the **Wartburg**, a castle above Eisenach, where he lived in disguise as "Junker Jörg," a knight, grew a beard, and wrote.

There, in roughly eleven weeks in the winter of 1521–22, he translated the whole New Testament from Greek into German. It became one of the most influential books ever printed in German.

## German Bibles before Luther

Luther did not give the Germans their first Bible. Parts of the Bible had been translated into German since the early Middle Ages, and complete German Bibles circulated in manuscript in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The first Bible printed in any modern European language was a German Bible printed by **Johann Mentelin** in Strasbourg in 1466. By one common count, eighteen complete printed German Bibles appeared before Luther's New Testament: fourteen in High German and four in Low German.

These earlier Bibles were translated from the Latin Vulgate and were based on an old translation that had been revised over time. Their German was often stiff, and by Luther's day it sounded old-fashioned. Historians have debated how widely they were read; some older Protestant accounts implied that the Bible was unknown before Luther, which is not true, while the real popularity of the earlier versions is hard to measure.

## The September Testament

Luther translated from the Greek. He used the second edition (1519) of **Erasmus's** Greek New Testament, which printed the Greek alongside Erasmus's own Latin translation (see [From manuscripts to printed Bibles](/articles/from-manuscripts-to-print/)). He also knew the Vulgate thoroughly, having used it all his life as a monk and professor.

Back in Wittenberg, Luther revised the translation with his colleague **Philip Melanchthon**, a fine Greek scholar. It was published in September 1522, without the translator's name, and is known as the **September Testament**. The book of Revelation was illustrated with woodcuts from the workshop of **Lucas Cranach**. The first printing is usually estimated at about three thousand copies. It sold quickly, and a corrected second edition, the **December Testament**, followed within three months.

Luther added prefaces to the books, and these were part of what readers received. The best known, his preface to Romans, became a classic of Protestant teaching. More controversially, in his 1522 prefaces he ranked the books of the New Testament by how clearly they proclaimed Christ, famously calling James "an epistle of straw" compared with books such as John and Romans. He placed Hebrews, James, Jude and Revelation at the end of his New Testament. Later editions dropped the "straw" remark, though the order remained.

## The whole Bible, 1534

The Old Testament took much longer. Luther published it in parts from 1523 onward, working from the Hebrew text. For this he relied on a team of Wittenberg colleagues, including Melanchthon, the Hebrew professor **Matthäus Aurogallus**, the pastor **Johannes Bugenhagen**, and others such as Caspar Cruciger and Justus Jonas, with Georg Rörer as corrector. They met regularly to go through the text verse by verse. Luther later wrote that they had often spent two, three or four weeks hunting for a single word, and that on Job he, Melanchthon and Aurogallus sometimes managed barely three lines in four days. Readers who now glide over the text, he said, are walking on a planed board, and never see the boulders the translators sweated to clear from the road.

The complete Bible was published in 1534 by the Wittenberg printer **Hans Lufft**. Luther placed the books of the Apocrypha in a separate section between the Testaments, describing them as not equal to holy scripture but useful and good to read. He kept revising the translation for the rest of his life. Between 1539 and 1541 the team went through the whole Bible again, and Georg Rörer kept minutes of their sessions, which record their discussion of particular passages. The last edition Luther supervised appeared in 1545, the year before his death.

## "Look at their mouths"

In 1530, while he waited at Coburg castle during the Diet of Augsburg, Luther wrote a short book defending his translation, the *Sendbrief vom Dolmetschen*, or **Open Letter on Translating**. It is vigorous, often rude to his opponents, and full of practical wisdom.

Luther argued that a translator must not ask the Latin letters how to speak German. Instead, he wrote, one must ask *die mutter jhm hause, die kinder auff der gassen, den gemeinen man auff dem marckt*, "the mother in the house, the children in the street, the common man in the market" (in the spelling of 1530), and look at their mouths to hear how they speak. One of his examples is Jesus' saying:

> For out of the abundance of the heart, the mouth speaks. (Matthew 12:34)

A literal German rendering, Luther said, would sound strange, as if the heart had too much of something. A German would say instead something like *Wes das Herz voll ist, des gehet der Mund über*: "when the heart is full, the mouth runs over." Similarly, in the angel's greeting to Mary (Luke 1:28), he objected that a literal rendering of the Latin "full of grace" would make a German think of a keg full of beer or a purse full of money. In 1522 he had printed *holdselige*, "gracious one." If he had followed his own ear, he said, he would have written something like "God greet you, dear Mary," which is what the angel would have said in German. Not every reader is convinced by his argument: as the Oxford editors of the letter point out, Luther himself called Jesus "full of grace" at John 1:14.

## The word "alone"

The *Open Letter* was largely a reply to one criticism. In **Romans 3:28**, Luther had translated Paul's statement that a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law as justified *allein durch den Glauben*, "by faith alone." The World English Bible, following the Greek more closely, reads:

> We maintain therefore that a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the law. (Romans 3:28)

The word "alone" is not in the Greek, and Catholic critics said Luther had added it to support his doctrine. Luther answered that he knew perfectly well it was not in the text, but that German idiom requires "alone" when one thing is affirmed and another excluded, and that it expressed Paul's meaning. He also pointed out that earlier Christian writers had spoken of justification by faith alone, a point later documented in detail by the Catholic scholar Joseph Fitzmyer, who lists such uses from the early church and the Middle Ages in his commentary on Romans.

The debate has two sides that are worth stating fairly. Luther's defenders argue that the word makes explicit what "apart from works" already implies. His critics reply that a translation should not supply a word that settles a disputed question of doctrine, and that "faith alone" in the sense of the Reformation debates was exactly what was in dispute. Many modern translations, Protestant and Catholic, render the verse without "alone," leaving such interpretation to notes and commentaries. Open Word Bible does the same at all three levels. ## The Bible and the German language

Luther's Bible had an enormous influence on the German language. Germany in the sixteenth century had many dialects, and Luther chose a form of German based on the language of the Saxon chancery, which he said was understood across both northern and southern Germany. Through the wide reach of his Bible, catechisms and hymns, many of his words and phrases entered common German. He is credited with words such as *Feuereifer* ("fiery zeal") and *Lückenbüßer* ("stopgap"), and his Bible made familiar sayings that were older but little known, such as casting pearls before swine (Matthew 7:6).

Historians now caution against saying Luther "created" modern German. Standard German developed over centuries, through printers, chanceries, schools and many other writers. But his Bible was one of the most important single influences on it.

The Bible also spread fast. Contemporary claims and modern estimates vary, but historians agree that hundreds of thousands of copies of Luther's Bible and New Testament, in whole or in part, were printed during his lifetime, making them among the bestsellers of the age.

## Other German Bibles

Luther's was not the only Reformation Bible in German. In Zurich, a complete Bible was published by the printer Christoph Froschauer in 1531, three years before Luther's, translated by Huldrych Zwingli's colleagues and drawing in part on Luther's work. Catholic scholars produced their own versions: **Hieronymus Emser** published a New Testament in 1527 that corrected Luther's text but kept much of its wording, and complete Catholic Bibles by Johann Dietenberger (1534) and Johann Eck (1537) followed.

Luther's Bible, revised many times, remains in use in German Protestant churches today. Its latest official revision, the Lutherbibel 2017, appeared in October 2016 for the Reformation's five hundredth anniversary. About seventy theologians spent more than five years on it, and by one count changed nearly twelve thousand of its roughly thirty-one thousand verses. Some changes made the text more exact; others went back to Luther's own words where earlier revisers had modernised them, such as *selig* ("saved, blessed") at Romans 10:10. It also influenced other translations, including **William Tyndale's** English New Testament (see [William Tyndale and the English New Testament](/articles/william-tyndale/)). Tyndale's "mercy seat" for the lid of the ark, for example, was probably modelled on Luther's *Gnadenstuhl*, "throne of grace."

## Showing the reasoning

The *Open Letter on Translating* is valuable precisely because Luther explained himself. Readers who disagreed could see his reasons and argue with them. Rörer's minutes of the revision sessions preserve some of the committee's reasoning too. Most translations since have offered much less.

Open Word Bible tries to make that kind of explanation routine. Every sentence carries a record of the decisions made in translating it, with the alternatives considered and the reasons for the choice. A disputed rendering, such as whether to add a word like "alone," is the kind of decision such a record is meant to bring into the open.
