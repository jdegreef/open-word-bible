By one common estimate, in 1800 the Bible, in whole or in part, had been printed in about seventy languages, most of them European. By 1900 the number was several hundred. The change was driven by two new things: societies that existed only to print and distribute Bibles, and a wave of Protestant missionaries who learned languages that had rarely or never been written down.

It was an age of great effort and real achievement. It was also an age of empire, and much of the work credited to famous missionaries was done with, and sometimes largely by, people whose names were never printed on the title page.

## Mary Jones and the Bible Society

The best-known story about the beginning of the Bible societies is about a Welsh girl, **Mary Jones** (1784–1864). As it is usually told, Mary saved for six years to buy a Welsh Bible, and in 1800, aged fifteen, walked some twenty-five miles barefoot to Bala to buy one from the Methodist minister **Thomas Charles**, who was so moved that he called for a society to supply Bibles to Wales and the world.

Mary Jones was real, and her Bible survives in the Bible Society's collections. But the familiar version was written down decades later, in Victorian books for children, and historians treat the link between her and the founding of the society as tradition rather than established fact.

What is certain is this. Welsh Bibles were scarce and expensive. In December 1802 Charles raised the need at a meeting of the Religious Tract Society in London, and the Baptist minister **Joseph Hughes** is said to have asked: if a society for Wales, why not for the world? On 7 March 1804 the **British and Foreign Bible Society** was founded.

## "Without note or comment"

The society had one simple rule: it would print the Bible "without note or comment." Anglicans, Methodists, Baptists and Congregationalists disagreed about much, but they could all agree to print the text alone. The rule had limits: in 1826, after a bitter dispute, the society refused to fund Bibles that included the Apocrypha, which strained its ties with churches in Europe.

Similar societies sprang up quickly, among them the **American Bible Society**, founded in New York in 1816. They funded translations, printed them cheaply in enormous numbers and sent colporteurs to sell them on foot.

## William Carey and Serampore

The first great name among the missionary translators is **William Carey** (1761–1834), an English shoemaker and Baptist pastor who taught himself languages. He sailed to India in 1793 and in 1800 settled at **Serampore**, a Danish post near Calcutta, with **Joshua Marshman** and the printer **William Ward**.

Carey's Bengali New Testament appeared in 1801. Over three decades the Serampore press produced Bibles or parts of the Bible in about forty languages and dialects, along with grammars and dictionaries. The quality was uneven. Many translations were made quickly, often by Indian scholars called pandits working under missionary supervision, and several were later judged barely understandable and replaced. Carey revised his own Bengali New Testament repeatedly. His chief early helper, **Ram Ram Basu**, is one of the few Indian collaborators whose name is often remembered.

## Martyn, Morrison and Judson

**Henry Martyn** (1781–1812), a Cambridge scholar serving as a chaplain in India, translated the New Testament into Hindustani, now known as Urdu. He then went to Shiraz to revise a Persian New Testament with local scholars, chief among them Mirza Sayyid Ali, and died on the way home at thirty-one.

**Robert Morrison** (1782–1834) reached Canton in 1807, when teaching Chinese to foreigners was forbidden and his teachers worked at personal risk. He built on a manuscript translation by the earlier Catholic missionary Jean Basset, which he had copied in the British Museum. With **William Milne** he finished a Chinese Bible in 1819; it was printed in 1823. It was pioneering and stylistically awkward, and many versions followed before the **Chinese Union Version** of 1919 became the standard Protestant Bible, as it largely remains.

The American Baptist **Adoniram Judson** (1788–1850) arrived in Burma in 1813 and was imprisoned for nearly two years during the first Anglo-Burmese war. Early biographies tell how his wife **Ann Hasseltine Judson** sewed his manuscript into a pillow to keep it safe. He completed the Bible in 1834, and his revised translation remains the basis of the most widely used Burmese Protestant Bible.

In southern Africa, **Robert Moffat** (1795–1883) spent most of his life at Kuruman translating into Setswana with Batswana speakers. His complete Bible appeared in 1857 and shaped written Setswana.

## The unnamed translators

Each of these famous names hides a team. Missionaries depended on teachers, language helpers, scribes, printers and early converts who knew the language from birth. Missionaries usually supervised and published; native speakers often supplied the language itself. Many of their names are lost, and where they were recorded, as with Ram Ram Basu or Mirza Sayyid Ali, they were often treated as assistants rather than fellow translators.

A few did become well known in their own right. **Samuel Ajayi Crowther** (about 1809–1891), freed from a slave ship as a boy and later the first African Anglican bishop, led work on the Yoruba Bible, completed in the 1880s.

## What did translation do?

For many years mission history was told as a story of heroic individuals. Later critics saw it as part of European colonialism: missionaries chose which dialect to write, invented spellings, and brought foreign ideas with the books.

The Gambian-born historian **Lamin Sanneh** offered an influential third view. Christianity, he argued, is unusually "translatable": it has no single sacred language, so translating the Bible into a local language gave that language new dignity, whatever the missionaries intended. Translations created written literatures, preserved vocabularies and let readers interpret the Bible for themselves, sometimes against the missionaries. Andrew Walls made similar arguments. Critics reply that Sanneh underplayed the power of missions and colonial governments over which languages were written and taught. Both points are true in different places.

## A movement that continues

The Bible societies still exist, and since 1946 they have cooperated as the United Bible Societies. Their work now goes on largely through translators working in their own languages (see [Translating for every language today](/articles/translating-for-every-language/)).

Open Word Bible shares the conviction that scripture belongs in every language, and its CC0 dedication follows the same instinct that led the early societies to print Bibles as cheaply as possible. It also takes a lesson from the unnamed translators: in its records, every reviewer will be named.
