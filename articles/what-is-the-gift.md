Ephesians 2:8–9 is one of the clearest statements in the New Testament of how a person is saved:

> {{OWB:EPH.2.8.s1:L}}

The Literal level keeps one small word that has been argued over for centuries: "this". This is not from you. But what is not from you? Grace? Faith? Or something larger?

## Where the verse stands

Verse 8 ends an argument that begins in verse 1. Paul tells his readers what they once were: dead in their trespasses and sins, following the ways of the world (verses 1–3). Then come two words that turn the whole passage: "But God" (verse 4). God, rich in mercy, made us alive together with Christ while we were still dead (verse 5). Halfway through that sentence Paul puts in a short cry: "by grace you have been saved".

Verse 8 takes up that cry and explains it. The Greek has "the grace", with the article, and the record reads it as pointing back to the grace of verses 5 and 7. So Balanced says "by this grace".

## Three candidates

The word is {{GREEK:EPH.2.8:9}}. In the sentence before it there are two obvious things it might point back to, {{GREEK:EPH.2.8:3}} ("grace") and {{GREEK:EPH.2.8:7}} ("faith"). Some readers have taken it to mean that faith itself is God's gift; others, that grace is.

The record for the verse decides that "this" is the whole of what Paul has just said: being saved, by grace, through faith. Three reasons are given:

- **Gender.** Greek nouns are masculine, feminine or neuter, and a word that points back to a noun normally matches it. "Grace" and "faith" are both feminine. "This" is neuter, which is how Paul often points back to a whole statement rather than one word (compare Philippians 1:28).
- **What follows.** The thing in question is "not from you", it is "God's gift", and in verse 9 it is "not from works". That last phrase does not fit faith on its own. It fits salvation as a whole.
- **The point of the passage.** From verse 1 Paul has been saying that the readers were dead and God made them alive. The emphasis is on God: the Greek puts {{GREEK:EPH.2.8:13}} ("God's") first in its clause, against {{GREEK:EPH.2.8:11-12}} ("from you").

Faith is part of what is given. But Paul's point is that salvation, from first to last, comes from God. The decision is not marked uncertain, and a footnote mentions the other readings.

## How it has been read

Both readings have long histories, and careful readers have held each. John Chrysostom, preaching on Ephesians in the late fourth century, took the gift to be faith. In his fourth homily on the letter he sums up Paul's point: "Neither is faith, he means, of ourselves."

John Calvin, in his commentary on Ephesians, went the other way. Paul's meaning, he wrote, is "not that faith is the gift of God, but that salvation is given to us by God". Calvin did not doubt that faith comes from God. He thought this verse was making a larger point.

On either reading, nothing in salvation is the reader's own achievement. The question is only what this one sentence points to.

## Saying it in English

Here the English wording has real work to do. Put "this is not from you" directly after "through faith", and most readers will hear "this" as "faith". The English is less clear than the Greek, which has the gender to guide the reader.

So the Balanced level says "None of this", which points to the whole statement:

> {{OWB:EPH.2.8.s1:B}}

Readable names it outright:

> {{OWB:EPH.2.8.s1:R}}

Notice the division of labor. The *meaning* decision, what "this" refers to, is made once and shared by every language. The *rendering* decisions, "None of this" and "Being saved", are English choices about how to make that meaning clear.

Other languages meet the same problem in their own way. Spanish has a neuter word for "this", *esto*, as Greek does, so it can point to the whole statement without extra words; the feminine *esta* would point to faith or grace alone. Swahili uses *hili*, the form used for a matter or affair, where *hii* would agree with the words for grace and faith. See [Showing our work](/articles/showing-the-work/) for how the two layers fit together.

## Verses 9 and 10

Verse 9 finishes the thought:

> {{OWB:EPH.2.9.s1:B}}

"Works" here has no qualifier, unlike "works of the law" in Galatians, and Paul is writing to Gentiles as well as Jews. So the record takes it to mean anything people do that might be thought to earn God's favor. And God saves this way on purpose, so that no one can claim the credit before him.

Verse 10 gives the reason, with a play on the same idea. We are not saved by our works, because we are ourselves God's work:

> {{OWB:EPH.2.10.s1:B}}

The word for "work" here, {{GREEK:EPH.2.10:4}}, is the source of the English "poem", and it is sometimes said that Paul calls believers God's poem or masterpiece. The record does not follow that. In the Greek of the time the word meant simply a thing made, and the next word, "created", shows what kind of making Paul means.

Good works still matter. They are the result of salvation, not its cause. The verb "walk" at the end of verse 10 answers the "walked" of verse 2: the life once lived in sins is now to be lived in the good works God prepared.
