Readers who grew up with older English Bibles know John 1:14 and 3:16 with the words "only begotten". Most modern translations say "one and only" or "only". Open Word Bible does too. The reasons are in the records for John 1:14 and 1:18, and they rest on a single Greek word.

## Where the word comes

John's Gospel opens with a prologue, eighteen verses that move from the Word with God "in the beginning" to the Word made flesh. Verse 14 is the turning point. The Word became flesh and lived among us, and the writer says what he and others saw:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.14.s1:B}}

The verb translated "made his home" means to pitch a tent. The record notes that it recalls the tabernacle, where God's glory lived among Israel (Exodus 25:8; 40:34). The glory John's witnesses saw is God's own glory, now seen in a person. Then comes the word this article is about.

## What the word means

The word is {{GREEK:JHN.1.14:17}}, a form of {{LEMMA:JHN.1.14:17}} (*monogenēs*). It is built from the words for "only" and "kind", not from the verb "to beget". It means unique, one of a kind, the only one in the family.

The New Testament uses it nine times, and the other uses make the sense plain. Three are in Luke, and none of them is about Jesus:

- the young man carried out for burial at Nain was his widowed mother's only son (Luke 7:12);
- Jairus's dying daughter was his only child (8:42);
- the father of the boy with a spirit begged Jesus to help his only child (9:38).

No one translates these "only begotten".

The clearest evidence is Hebrews 11:17, which uses the same word, {{GREEK:HEB.11.17:9}}, of Isaac. Isaac was not Abraham's only son; Ishmael was born first. But Isaac was unique, the son of the promise. The meaning decision for John 1:14 cites this verse.

The other five are John's (John 1:14, 18; 3:16, 18; 1 John 4:9), and each describes the Son's relation to the Father.

## How "only begotten" came into English

"Only begotten" came into English from Latin. Old Latin manuscripts, such as Codex Vercellensis, render *monogenēs* with *unicus*, "only", even where it refers to Christ. When Jerome revised the Latin Gospels in the 380s, he kept *unicus* for the children in Luke but used *unigenitus*, "only begotten", in the verses about Christ. Scholars usually connect this to the Arian controversy of his century. The Nicene Creed had confessed that the Son is "begotten, not made", against the teaching that he was a created being, and *unigenitus* carried that confession into the Bible's wording.

English followed the Latin. The Wycliffe Bible said God gave "his oon bigetun sone". William Tyndale, translating from the Greek, wrote "his only sonne" in John 3:16. The King James Version has "only begotten" in John and in Hebrews 11:17, but "only son", "only daughter" and "only child" in Luke. The Revised Standard Version's New Testament of 1946 said "only Son", and most English versions since have said "only" or "one and only".

## The objection

Some readers worry that dropping "begotten" weakens the doctrine that the Son is eternally begotten of the Father. But the doctrine does not stand or fall with this word. The creeds draw it from the whole witness of Scripture to the Father and the Son. Some scholars do defend "only begotten". Charles Lee Irons, in a 2017 essay in *Retrieving Eternal Generation*, argues that the word carries the idea of birth, so that "only-begotten" is closer than "unique". Most New Testament scholars still find the uses in Luke and Hebrews decisive.

Open Word Bible's meaning decision gives "unique, one of a kind" and lists "only begotten" as the alternative. A footnote gives the older wording, so a reader who wants it can see it.

## What each level does with John 1:14

The Greek does not actually say "Son" in this verse. Word for word it says "glory as of an only one from a father". The translation supplies "Son" because "from the Father", and John 3:16 and 18, make it clear. The levels show this in different ways:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.14.s1:L}}

Literal keeps the Greek's "as of" and puts "the", "Son" and "the" in brackets, because the Greek has none of them. Balanced, quoted above, reads "the glory of the one and only Son from the Father". Readable says it most simply:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.14.s1:R}}

## John 1:18: "Son" or "God"?

The same word appears again in verse 18, and here there is a question about the text itself. Some manuscripts read "the one and only Son"; others read "the one and only God": {{GREEK:JHN.1.18:5-6}}.

The papyri P66 and P75, both copied around AD 200, read "God", and so do Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus from the fourth century. Codex Alexandrinus from the fifth century and most later manuscripts read "Son". The King James Version follows them: "the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father".

Open Word Bible follows "God". It has the oldest witnesses, and it is the harder reading. Scribes knew "the only Son" from John 3:16 and 18 and 1 John 4:9, so they were more likely to change an unusual "God" to the familiar "Son" than the other way round. The committee behind the standard Greek text reached the same conclusion, though not unanimously. One member, Allen Wikgren, dissented, noting that scribes wrote "Son" and "God" as two-letter abbreviations that differ by a single letter, so an accidental slip was easy. The record's meaning decision gives the reasons, and a footnote gives "Son".

## A translation problem

Choosing "God" creates a problem for English. "The only God", standing alone, would suggest that the Father is not God. The Literal level shows the Greek as it stands:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.18.s1:L}}

The Balanced and Readable levels supply "Son", as verse 14 did, and a rendering decision explains why. This keeps both truths John holds together: the one who makes God known is himself God, and he is the Son, distinct from the Father.

> {{OWB:JHN.1.18.s1:B}}

## Close beside the Father

Two more decisions finish the verse. The phrase older Bibles render "in the bosom of the Father" comes from the way people ate. Diners reclined, so one could rest against another's chest, as the disciple Jesus loved did at the last supper (13:23). The image is intimacy, not place, so Balanced says "close beside the Father" and Readable "closest to the Father".

The last verb, {{GREEK:JHN.1.18:15}}, is the word from which "exegesis" comes. It means to lead out, explain or narrate. The prologue ends where it began. No one has ever seen God, and the one and only Son, who is himself God, has made him known.
