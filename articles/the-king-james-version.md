For about three hundred years, the English Bible for most English speakers was the **King James Version**, published in 1611. In Britain it is usually called the **Authorized Version**. Its phrases are woven into the English language, and many churches and readers still use it today.

It is easy to imagine it as a single inspired act of genius. The real story is more ordinary and more interesting: a committee of about fifty scholars, a set of rules, a long line of earlier translations, the best printed Hebrew and Greek texts of the day, and printers who made mistakes.

## The Hampton Court Conference

When **James VI of Scotland** became James I of England in 1603, the English church was divided. Puritans wanted further reform; the bishops wanted to keep the settlement made under Elizabeth I. In January 1604 James called a conference at **Hampton Court Palace** to hear both sides.

On the second day, **John Rainolds** (or Reynolds), President of Corpus Christi College, Oxford, and a leading Puritan scholar, proposed a new translation of the Bible, saying that the existing ones did not answer to the original. Most of his other requests were refused, but James seized on this one. He disliked the Geneva Bible's marginal notes, some of which he thought dangerous to royal authority (see [From Coverdale to Geneva: the English Bibles before 1611](/articles/english-bibles-before-1611/)), and a new Bible for the whole church, without such notes, suited him well.

## Six companies

The work was organised by **Richard Bancroft**, Bishop of London and soon Archbishop of Canterbury. The translators were divided into six "companies," two each meeting at Westminster, Oxford and Cambridge:

- First Westminster: Genesis to 2 Kings, led by **Lancelot Andrewes**.
- First Cambridge: 1 Chronicles to the Song of Songs.
- First Oxford: Isaiah to Malachi.
- Second Oxford: the Gospels, Acts and Revelation.
- Second Westminster: the New Testament letters.
- Second Cambridge: the Apocrypha.

A list of fifty-four names survives from 1604, but some died or were replaced, and lists of the translators who actually served usually give about forty-seven. They were among the best scholars of Hebrew and Greek in England. Andrewes, later Bishop of Winchester, was said to know fifteen languages. **John Bois** of Cambridge had read Hebrew as a child. Rainolds himself worked with the Oxford prophets company until he died in 1607, before the work was finished. The companies held Puritans and conservative churchmen alike, and the result belonged to neither party.

## The rules

Bancroft issued a set of fifteen rules. Among them:

- The **Bishops' Bible** was to be followed, and "as little altered as the truth of the original will permit."
- The old **ecclesiastical words** were to be kept, such as "church" rather than "congregation," a deliberate turn away from Tyndale and the Geneva Bible.
- When a word had several possible meanings, the one "most commonly used by the most of the ancient fathers" was to be kept, if it suited the passage.
- There were to be **no marginal notes**, except to explain Hebrew or Greek words that could not be expressed briefly in the text.
- Each translator in a company was to work on the same chapters, the company was to agree on a text, and each company's work was to be sent to the others for review.
- Where they agreed better with the text, the translations of Tyndale, Matthew, Coverdale, Whitchurch (the Great Bible) and Geneva were to be used.

In practice the translators went well beyond the Bishops' Bible. Scholars who have compared the texts find that much of the King James New Testament still goes back, through the Bishops' and Geneva Bibles, to **William Tyndale** (see [William Tyndale and the English New Testament](/articles/william-tyndale/)). They also drew on the Catholic Rheims New Testament, especially for its Latin-derived vocabulary.

## What they translated from

For the Old Testament the translators worked from the Hebrew of the **Rabbinic Bible** printed in Venice by Daniel Bomberg, with the Masoretic text the Jewish scribes had handed down (see [The Masoretes](/articles/the-masoretes/)). For the New Testament they used printed Greek editions in the tradition begun by Erasmus in 1516, above all those of Robert Estienne and Theodore Beza, a text later called the **Received Text** (see [From manuscripts to printed Bibles](/articles/from-manuscripts-to-print/)). They consulted the Latin Vulgate, the Aramaic Targums, the Syriac, and modern Latin, French, Italian, Spanish and German versions.

They never said exactly which Greek edition they followed, because they did not follow one. In 1881 the Cambridge scholar **F. H. A. Scrivener** reconstructed the Greek text behind their New Testament. It is mostly Beza's edition of 1598, with about 190 readings taken from other printed editions.

A final review committee met in London around 1610. Notes taken there by John Bois were rediscovered in the twentieth century and published by Ward Allen in 1969. They show the committee arguing over single words, rhythm and sense, and over how much to explain. The final text was seen through the press by **Miles Smith** and **Thomas Bilson**.

## 1611: the first printing

The new Bible was printed in London by **Robert Barker**, the King's Printer, in 1611. It was a large folio for reading in church. The title page says it was "appointed to be read in Churches"; despite the name "Authorized Version," no record survives of a formal act authorising it.

The first edition shows how the translators wanted the text to look. Words supplied in English that had no direct equivalent in the original were set in roman type to stand out from the black-letter text; later editions, printed in roman, marked them with **italics** instead. The margins gave alternative renderings and literal meanings, more than 8,000 notes in all by one count. Chapter summaries were printed at the head of each chapter.

It also included the **Apocrypha**, the books found in the Greek and Latin Old Testament but not in the Hebrew, printed between the Testaments as the Church of England expected. From the nineteenth century most printings, especially those of the Bible societies, left them out.

## Printers' errors

Printing a book of this size by hand was difficult, and errors crept in. Two printings in 1611 differ at **Ruth 3:15**: one says that "he went into the city," the other that "she went." Collectors call them the "He" Bible and the "She" Bible. The World English Bible reads "he," which agrees with the standard Hebrew text, though some manuscripts and versions read "she."

The most notorious error came in 1631, when an edition printed by Barker and Martin Lucas left out the word "not" from the seventh commandment, so that it read "Thou shalt commit adultery." This **Wicked Bible** was suppressed, the printers were fined heavily, and only a handful of copies survive.

## The translators speak

The translators prefaced the Bible with a long essay, **"The Translators to the Reader,"** written by Miles Smith. Modern editions usually leave it out, which is a pity, because it shows the translators' own view of their work. They defended translation itself:

> Translation it is that openeth the window, to let in the light.

They wanted scripture in language that "may be understood even of the very vulgar," that is, by ordinary people. They insisted that they had not set out to replace the earlier English Bibles but "to make a good one better." They defended their marginal alternatives by citing Augustine, "that variety of Translations is profitable for the finding out of the sense of the Scriptures," and argued that where a word is uncertain it is more honest to show the doubt than to hide it. And they explained that they had not tied themselves "to an uniformity of phrasing, or to an identity of words," preferring variety where the sense allowed.

## Slow acceptance

The King James Version did not win immediately. The scholar Hugh Broughton, who had been left off the committee, attacked it bitterly. The Geneva Bible remained popular for decades; it continued to be printed until about 1644, and some editions put King James text alongside Geneva notes. Only after the Restoration of 1660 did the King James Version become unquestionably the English Bible.

## Correcting the text: 1629 to 1769

Meanwhile its text was quietly corrected. The King James Bible most people read today is not quite the text of 1611.

The university press at **Cambridge** produced the first careful revisions. Its edition of **1629** corrected many misprints. The edition of **1638**, associated with the scholars Thomas Goad, Samuel Ward, Joseph Mede and John Bois, went further, correcting errors, making the use of italics more consistent and, in places, bringing the English closer to the original. It remained the standard text for more than a century.

By the eighteenth century, printers had piled up their own small changes, and English spelling had settled. **Francis Sawyer Parris** of Cambridge prepared a revised text, published in 1760 and reprinted in a fine folio in 1762. Building on his work, **Benjamin Blayney** of Oxford produced the edition of **1769**, standardising spelling, punctuation and italics and adding many marginal references. Most King James Bibles printed today follow Blayney's text. The changes from 1611 are mostly in spelling and small details, but there are thousands of them. The 1611 text of Leviticus 25:5, for example, speaks of what grows "of it owne accord"; the modern text reads "of its own accord." Scrivener in 1873 and David Norton in 2005 have produced critical editions that try to recover what the translators intended.

## Its language

The English of the King James Version was slightly old-fashioned even in 1611. Its "thee" and "thou," and verbs ending in "-eth," belonged to the older Bibles it revised as much as to the speech of the day. In Matthew 5:13 the salt that has lost its taste is still "his," in the old way:

> Ye are the salt of the earth: but if the salt have lost his savour, wherewith shall it be salted?

The translators also kept close to the Hebrew and Greek, more than many readers realise. Phrases such as "and it came to pass," "the face of the earth" and "the fruit of thy body" are Hebrew idioms carried into English word for word. Over time they stopped sounding foreign and started sounding biblical.

## What it got right

The King James Version was made to be **read aloud**, in church, to people many of whom could not read. Its rhythm carries meaning through the ear, and that is one reason its phrases stuck. Isaiah 40:1 shows the effect:

> Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God.

It was also a careful translation for its time. The translators worked from the Hebrew and Greek, consulted everything available, and checked each other's work. Their italics showed the reader what had been supplied. Their margins showed where they were unsure. Those are habits of honesty that many later Bibles dropped.

## Where modern translations differ, and why

Modern translations differ from the King James Version for three main reasons.

The first is **the text**. Since 1611, scholars have found thousands of Greek manuscripts, many far older than those behind the printed editions of Erasmus and Beza (see [How textual criticism works](/articles/how-textual-criticism-works/)). A few verses in the King James New Testament are now judged later additions. The best known is 1 John 5:7:

> For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one.

The words about the Father, the Word and the Spirit are found in no Greek manuscript before the late Middle Ages, and Erasmus added them to his Greek text only in 1522. Others include the closing words of praise in the Lord's Prayer at Matthew 6:13 and Philip's reply in Acts 8:37. Open Word Bible follows the SBL Greek New Testament in each case and records the longer reading and the reasons in the sentence's record (see [When manuscripts disagree](/articles/when-manuscripts-disagree/)).

The second is **knowledge of the languages**. Hebrew and Greek are better understood now, especially rare words in the Old Testament. Some changes are simple. In Hebrews 4:8 the King James Version says "if Jesus had given them rest," because the Greek form of Joshua's name is the same as Jesus. The verse is about Joshua, and Open Word Bible's Balanced level says so:

> {{OWB:HEB.4.8.s1:B}}

In Acts 12:4 the King James Version says Herod meant to bring Peter out "after Easter," keeping a word from Tyndale; the Greek is the word it translates "Passover" everywhere else.

The third is **English itself**. Words have shifted since 1611, so that the same sentence can now mean something else to a modern reader (see [English translations since the King James](/articles/modern-english-translations/)).

## Its influence

Few books have shaped English as much. The linguist David Crystal counted 257 idioms in modern English that come from or were popularised by the King James Version, while noting that most of them were already in earlier translations, especially Tyndale's. Phrases such as "the powers that be" (Romans 13:1), "a law unto themselves" (Romans 2:14) and "the skin of my teeth" (Job 19:20) passed into everyday speech. Charles Jennens drew the words of Handel's *Messiah* (1742) from it and from the Prayer Book psalms.

For a long time the King James Version was also the text against which all newer translations were measured. A small number of churches still hold that it alone should be used. For most readers, it is the Bible whose words they know even if they have never opened it.

## A good one better

The King James translators would have recognised some of Open Word Bible's aims. They worked from the Hebrew and Greek, built on earlier translations, and showed alternatives in the margin rather than pretending to certainty. Open Word Bible takes that last practice further: every sentence has a reasoning record listing the decisions behind it, with the options considered. Its New Testament is translated from the SBL Greek New Testament and its Old Testament from the Westminster Leningrad Codex, editions based on older and better evidence than the translators of 1611 could use. Unlike them, it is a young, AI-assisted draft, still waiting for named human reviewers. And like the King James Version in most of the world today, it is free of copyright, though by deliberate choice rather than by age.
