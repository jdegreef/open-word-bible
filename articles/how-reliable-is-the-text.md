This series has followed the Bible from its first writing, through generations of scribes, to the manuscripts and printed editions behind modern translations. This last article asks the question that lies behind all the others: how confident can we be that the Bible we read today says what its authors wrote?

There are two easy answers, and both are wrong. One says that copyists corrupted the text so badly that we cannot know what the originals said. The other says that the text has come down without any change at all. The evidence supports neither. The honest answer has more detail in it, and it is also more reassuring than many people expect.

## The size of the evidence

Start with the New Testament. The Institute for New Testament Textual Research in Münster keeps the official list of Greek manuscripts, and it now counts more than 5,800: about 140 papyri, some 320 majuscules (parchment manuscripts in capital letters), nearly 2,900 minuscules (in a small cursive hand) and around 2,500 lectionaries, books of readings for worship. Many are fragments; some are complete New Testaments. To these we add thousands of manuscripts of early translations, especially Latin, and an enormous number of quotations in early Christian writers.

No other work of ancient Greek or Latin literature comes close. The first six books of Tacitus's *Annals*, our main source for the reign of Tiberius, survive in a single medieval manuscript now in Florence. Historians still use Tacitus with confidence. Popular books sometimes push this comparison too far, with outdated figures for classical works, and the volume *Myths and Mistakes in New Testament Textual Criticism* (2019) corrects several of them. But the basic point stands: no other ancient book is attested by so many manuscripts, so early.

For the Old Testament the evidence is different. In 1776–80 Benjamin Kennicott published the readings of 615 medieval Hebrew manuscripts, and many more are known today. Behind them stand the great Masoretic codices of around 1000 CE (see [The Aleppo and Leningrad codices](/articles/aleppo-and-leningrad-codices/)), more than two hundred biblical manuscripts among the Dead Sea Scrolls from a thousand years earlier, and ancient translations, above all the Greek [Septuagint](/articles/the-septuagint/).

## Half a million variants?

With so many manuscripts, it is no surprise that they differ. A **variant** is any place where manuscripts do not agree. How many are there in the New Testament?

Peter Gurry, in a careful study published in 2016, estimated that the Greek manuscripts contain around **500,000** variants, not counting differences in spelling. The New Testament has about 138,000 words, so there are more variants than words. Bart Ehrman made this point memorably in *Misquoting Jesus* (2005), and it is true.

But the number needs to be understood. It is large *because* the number of manuscripts is large. Every copy adds a few slips of its own. If we had only ten manuscripts, we would have far fewer variants and far less certainty. The more copies we have, the more variants we count, and the easier it is to see which reading came first. How scholars do that is the subject of [How textual criticism recovers the text](/articles/how-textual-criticism-works/).

## What kinds of variants?

Daniel Wallace sorts variants into four groups.

- **Spelling and nonsense errors.** By far the largest group. A word spelled differently, a letter dropped, a slip that produces no real word. These are easy to recognise and do not affect meaning.
- **Changes that do not affect translation.** Greek can vary word order without changing the meaning, so "Christ Jesus" and "Jesus Christ," or a verb before or after its subject, often make no difference in English. Greek also sometimes puts the article before a name ("the Jesus"), which is not translated.
- **Meaningful but not viable.** A difference that would change the meaning, but is found only in one late manuscript or a small group, with no real chance of being original.
- **Meaningful and viable.** A difference that affects the meaning and has a real claim to be original. Wallace puts this group at less than one percent of the total.

One percent of half a million is still several thousand places. Most of them change a nuance, not a teaching. But it is this last group that matters, and that good translations mark in their footnotes.

## The passages that really are uncertain

Some variants concern whole passages that appear in the King James Version but are absent from the earliest manuscripts.

- **Mark 16:9–20.** Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, the two oldest complete manuscripts of the New Testament, end Mark at 16:8: "for they were afraid." Most later manuscripts go on with the longer ending; some have a short ending instead, and a few have both. Most scholars think the longer ending was added in the second century. Open Word Bible, like its Greek edition, prints both endings after 16:8, with notes saying that neither is in the earliest manuscripts and that whether Mark meant to stop at 16:8 is an open question.
- **John 7:53–8:11**, the woman caught in adultery, and **John 5:4**, the angel stirring the water. Both are absent from the earliest manuscripts of John. How Open Word Bible handles them is told in [When the manuscripts disagree](/articles/when-manuscripts-disagree/).
- **1 John 5:7–8**, the *Comma Johanneum*. The words about the Father, the Word and the Spirit bearing witness in heaven are found in no Greek manuscript before the late medieval period. They entered printed Greek texts through Erasmus's third edition (see [From manuscripts to printed Bibles](/articles/from-manuscripts-to-print/)). No level of Open Word Bible includes them, and the footnote explains why.

Here is the shorter text as Open Word Bible gives it:

> {{OWB:1JN.5.7.s1:B}}

The reasoning record for that sentence adds a telling detail. No Greek writer of the early church quotes the longer words, even in the fourth-century debates about the Trinity, where they would have been the most useful verse to cite.

Not every case is so clear. Luke 22:43–44, where an angel strengthens Jesus and his sweat falls like drops of blood, is missing from several early manuscripts, including Papyrus 75 and Codex Vaticanus, but present in others, including the first hand of Codex Sinaiticus, and known to writers of the second century. Scholars still argue about whether the verses were added or removed. Open Word Bible prints them, gives a footnote, and marks the decision as uncertain. Luke 23:34, "Father, forgive them," is a similar case.

## Smaller words, real differences

Many important variants are a single word. In Mark 1:41 a man with a skin disease asks Jesus to make him clean. Most manuscripts say Jesus was "moved with compassion." A few, among them Codex Bezae and some Old Latin copies, say he was angry: {{GREEK:MRK.1.41:2}}. In Hebrews 2:9, almost all manuscripts say Jesus tasted death for everyone "by the grace of God," but a few, and several early writers including Origen, say "apart from God": {{GREEK:HEB.2.9:20-21}}.

In both places the rarer reading is also the harder one. A scribe was more likely to soften anger into compassion than the reverse, and more likely to turn a startling phrase into a familiar one. The SBL Greek New Testament, which Open Word Bible translates, prints the harder reading both times:

> {{OWB:MRK.1.41.s1:B}}

> {{OWB:HEB.2.9.s1:B}}

Each sentence has a footnote with the other reading and is marked uncertain. A reader who prefers the majority reading can see exactly what it is and why the edition chose otherwise.

## What the skeptics get right

Bart Ehrman argued for both of those harder readings, in *Misquoting Jesus* and in his earlier *The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture* (1993). He is the best-known skeptic in this field, and it is worth hearing his case fairly, because much of it is true.

We do not have the originals of any New Testament book. We have copies of copies, and the earliest substantial manuscripts come from around 200 CE or later, more than a century after most of the books were written. Even those dates are less precise than older books claimed (see [The earliest New Testament papyri](/articles/the-earliest-new-testament-papyri/)). Scribes in the earliest period were often not professionals, and some of them changed the text on purpose, to make it clearer or to make it say more plainly what they believed. Ehrman's point is that some of these changes affect how a passage should be read, and Mark 1:41 and Hebrews 2:9 show that he is right. The fact that editors have since printed readings he argued for is a sign that his arguments were taken seriously.

Other scholars question whether "the original text" is even the right goal. David Parker has argued that the Gospels were a living text, shaped by the communities that copied them. The Münster editors now speak of the **initial text**: the earliest form that can be reconstructed from the surviving evidence. That is a careful claim. It is not the same as saying we hold the author's own page.

## Does any teaching depend on them?

On the central question, though, skeptics and believers end up close together. In an appendix to the paperback edition of *Misquoting Jesus*, Ehrman agreed with his teacher Bruce Metzger that essential Christian beliefs are not affected by textual variants in the manuscript tradition. Ehrman argues that variants matter for how particular passages are read, and they do. But the major teachings of the Christian faith, such as the deity of Christ, his death and resurrection, salvation by grace and the Trinity, are taught in many passages whose text is not in doubt. The doctrine of the Trinity was formulated in the fourth century by theologians who did not have the *Comma Johanneum* in their Bibles.

Wallace and Ehrman debated these questions in public in 2008, and the book that came out of it (*The Reliability of the New Testament*, 2011) shows two scholars who disagree on much and agree on this. The places of real uncertainty are known, and they are limited.

## The Old Testament

The Old Testament presents a different picture. Its transmission within Judaism, especially from the first century CE onwards, was extraordinarily stable, as the comparison of the Dead Sea Scrolls with the medieval manuscripts shows (see [The Great Isaiah Scroll](/articles/the-great-isaiah-scroll/)). But before that period some books existed in more than one edition, and the Masoretic text, for all its care, preserves some early errors (see [What the Dead Sea Scrolls show](/articles/what-the-dead-sea-scrolls-show/)).

Three places in Open Word Bible show what this looks like.

- **Genesis 4:8.** The Hebrew says "Cain said to Abel his brother," and then gives no words. The Samaritan Pentateuch, the Septuagint, the Syriac and the Latin add "Let us go out to the field." Open Word Bible follows the Hebrew, with a footnote.
- **Isaiah 53:11.** The Hebrew says the servant "will see." The Great Isaiah Scroll, another Isaiah scroll from the same cave and the Septuagint say he "will see light." Open Word Bible translates the Hebrew and gives "light" in a footnote; the meaning is close either way.
- **Psalm 22:17** (22:16 in most English Bibles). The Hebrew as handed down reads "like the lion" at my hands and my feet, which leaves the line without a verb. A scroll from Nahal Hever in the Judean desert has a slightly different form that looks like a verb. The Septuagint has "they dug" or "pierced," the reading in the King James Version. Open Word Bible follows the Hebrew, explains the alternatives in a footnote, and marks the decision uncertain.

So Old Testament translators face hard textual decisions more often than New Testament translators do. The drafts show it: Old Testament sentences are about twice as likely as New Testament ones to carry a text decision marked uncertain. But here too, the great majority of the text is not in doubt, and the alternatives that are can be named and weighed.

## How Open Word Bible marks the doubts

Every sentence in Open Word Bible has a reasoning record. Where the manuscripts differ in a way a reader might notice, the record has a decision in the category *text variant*: the reading followed, the alternatives, the evidence in plain language, and whether the question is open. In the drafts so far, about one sentence in ten has such a decision, most of them small. About one in sixty-five is marked genuinely uncertain. Where a variant would change what a reader understands, there is a footnote.

These records are AI-assisted drafts. Most of them end by asking a reviewer to check the summary against the scholarly apparatus, and until a named human reviewer does, they stay drafts (see [AI-assisted translation and human review](/articles/ai-assisted-translation-and-human-review/)).

## Confidence, not certainty

What, then, should we say?

We do not have the original manuscripts of any biblical book, and we will not find them. We cannot claim that every letter in a printed Bible is exactly what the author wrote. In a small number of places scholars are genuinely uncertain, and in some of those the decision affects the meaning of a sentence.

But we can say this. The Bible's text is attested by more manuscripts, closer in time to the originals, than any other ancient writing. The work of scribes, from the Masoretes to the copyists of the early church, was in the main careful and devoted. Their mistakes follow patterns that can be recognised. Over three centuries of study, scholars of every background have reconstructed a text that, in the great majority of its words, is not in dispute. And where it is uncertain, we know where the uncertainty lies.

That is not blind trust. It is confidence based on evidence. A translation should honour that by being open about the evidence, and that is what Open Word Bible tries to do: every sentence carries its reasoning, including any textual decision behind it, so that readers can see not only what the text says, but how we know.
