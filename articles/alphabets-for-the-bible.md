Most languages in history have never been written down. To translate the Bible into such a language, a translator first has to decide how to write it, and sometimes that means inventing an alphabet.

In late antiquity this happened for three peoples on the edges of the Roman world: the **Goths**, the **Armenians** and the **Georgians**. Two of their alphabets are still in daily use.

## Wulfila and the Goths

The Goths were a Germanic people who in the fourth century lived north of the Danube. Among them lived descendants of Christian captives taken in earlier raids on Roman territory. One of these was **Wulfila**, or **Ulfilas**, born around 311; his name means "little wolf." According to the church historian **Philostorgius**, he was consecrated bishop for the Christians among the Goths around 341. Persecution later drove him and his followers across the Danube into what is now northern Bulgaria. He died around 383.

Philostorgius says that Wulfila invented an alphabet for the Goths and translated the scriptures into their language (*Church History* 2.5). The alphabet has around twenty-seven letters, based mainly on Greek capitals, with some from Latin and perhaps from the Germanic runes. The translation was made from Greek and follows it closely.

Philostorgius adds one memorable detail. Wulfila translated everything, he says, except the books of **Kings**, because they were full of wars and the Goths were already too fond of fighting. Philostorgius wrote decades after Wulfila's death, and almost none of the Gothic Old Testament survives, so the story cannot be checked. But it is an early picture of a translator weighing what scripture might do to its new readers.

## The Silver Bible

Most of what survives of the Gothic Bible is New Testament. The most famous manuscript is the **Codex Argenteus**, the "Silver Book," copied in the early sixth century, probably in Italy for the court of the Ostrogothic king Theoderic, in silver and gold ink on purple-dyed parchment. Of its original 336 or so leaves, 188 survive. Swedish troops took it from Prague in 1648, and 187 leaves have been at **Uppsala University** since 1669. The other one turned up in Speyer Cathedral in 1970.

The Lord's Prayer in Gothic begins *Atta unsar þu in himinam*, "Our Father, you in the heavens." It is among the oldest substantial texts in any Germanic language, English included.

## Mesrop Mashtots and the Armenian alphabet

Armenia was one of the first states to adopt Christianity, traditionally in 301, though many historians prefer a date around 314. For about a century its church read the Bible in Greek or Syriac and explained it orally.

Around 405 a former royal secretary turned monk, **Mesrop Mashtots**, set out to change this, backed by the Catholicos **Sahak** and King **Vramshapuh**. His pupil **Koriun** tells the story in his *Life* of Mashtots. A first alphabet, attributed to a Syrian bishop named Daniel, could not represent Armenian sounds. Mashtots travelled with students to Syrian cities such as Edessa and Samosata, and there devised a new alphabet of thirty-six letters, matched carefully to the sounds of Armenian. Koriun records the first sentence translated into the new script, from the opening of Proverbs:

> to know wisdom and instruction; to discern the words of understanding; (Proverbs 1:2)

It was a fitting choice for a people about to read in their own language for the first time.

The Armenian Bible was first translated largely from Syriac. After the Council of Ephesus in 431, Mashtots's pupils brought Greek manuscripts from Constantinople and the translation was revised against them. Its accuracy and elegance led the eighteenth-century scholar **Mathurin Veyssière de La Croze** to call it, by the usual account, the "queen of versions." Mashtots's alphabet is still the alphabet of Armenian.

## Georgian and a lost language

The kingdom of **Kartli**, in what is now eastern Georgia, became Christian in the fourth century through the work of a woman remembered as **St Nino**, and a script for Georgian soon followed. Its origin is disputed: Koriun credits Mashtots, while many scholars think Georgians created it themselves in the fourth or fifth century. The earliest dated Georgian inscriptions are from the fifth. The Georgian Bible was translated in that century, probably at first from Armenian or Syriac, and later revised against Greek.

Koriun also says Mashtots made an alphabet for the Caucasian Albanians, in what is now Azerbaijan. That script was almost lost until 1975, when a cache of manuscripts was found at St Catherine's Monastery on Sinai. Beneath later Georgian writing on some reused pages, scholars identified Caucasian Albanian texts, including Gospel readings. Published in 2008, they are almost the only substantial texts in that language. A people's Gospel readings survived for more than a thousand years, scraped off and written over.

## Scripts and scripture

The pattern repeats. A people becomes Christian; the church reads scripture in a foreign language; someone creates a way to write the local language; the first books in the new script are biblical, and a written literature grows from them. In the ninth century Cyril and Methodius did the same for the Slavs (see [Cyril and Methodius and the Slavonic Bible](/articles/cyril-and-methodius/)), and in modern times missionaries and local Christians devised scripts for many more, such as Cree syllabics in Canada (on that era, see [Bible societies and missionary translators](/articles/bible-societies-and-missionary-translators/)).

Open Word Bible works in languages that already have long written traditions, so it needs no new alphabets. But it shares these scribes' concern for getting each letter of the source right. On this site, Hebrew and Greek are never typed by hand but copied from pinned source data (see [Why we never type Greek or Hebrew by hand](/articles/never-typed-by-hand/)).
