Visit a synagogue today and you may see the Torah scroll taken from the ark, unrolled and read aloud. That scroll was written by hand by a trained scribe, following rules more than a thousand years old. A single missing letter would make it unfit for public reading.

## Where the rules come from

The rules are set out in the Babylonian Talmud (completed around 500–600 CE), in a later tractate called *Soferim* ("Scribes"), and in the law code of **Maimonides** (1138–1204). These are rabbinic rules from late antiquity and after. They do not describe how every scribe worked in the time of the prophets, and the Dead Sea Scrolls show that earlier practice was more varied (Tov 2004). What they show is how the text was guarded in the centuries when the Masoretic text, the basis of our Old Testament, was copied.

## The rules themselves

**Copy from a written text, never from memory.** The Talmud forbids writing even one letter of scripture except from a written copy (*Megillah* 18b). However well the scribe knows the text, they look at the exemplar for every word. This guards against the most natural error of all: writing what you *expect* the text to say. By custom the scribe also says each word aloud before writing it.

**Every letter whole and separate.** Each letter must be correctly formed, must not touch its neighbour, and must be surrounded by blank parchment. A broken letter, or one that could be taken for another, makes the scroll unfit until it is fixed. Hebrew has several pairs of look-alike letters, and this rule keeps them apart.

**The layout is part of the text.** The Torah is divided by "open" gaps, which start a new line, and "closed" gaps within a line. A scroll that places them wrongly is unfit. The Song of the Sea (Exodus 15) is laid out like bricks in a wall.

**The divine name.** Before writing the name of God, the scribe pauses to intend the holy name. Once written, it may not be erased (Maimonides, *Mishneh Torah*, Foundations of the Torah 6:1–2).

**One letter matters, and correction has a limit.** A scroll lacking one letter, or with one extra, is unfit until corrected (*Mishneh Torah*, Laws of Tefillin, Mezuzah and the Torah Scroll 7:11). A column with too many errors may not be patched at all (*Menahot* 29b). The scroll is put away in a *genizah*, a storeroom for worn-out sacred texts, or buried. The genizah of a Cairo synagogue, brought to scholars' attention in the 1890s, held hundreds of thousands of such fragments.

## Counting letters

The Talmud says the early scribes were called *soferim* because they *counted* (the same root) the letters of the Torah. It names the middle letter of the Torah, in the word for "belly" in Leviticus 11:42 (*Qiddushin* 30a). The [Masoretes](/articles/the-masoretes/) took counting much further, with notes on verse totals and unusual spellings. A copy whose totals do not match has gone wrong somewhere.

## Did it work?

Hebrew manuscripts of the Middle Ages, copied in different countries, agree to a remarkable degree. When Benjamin Kennicott collated hundreds of them in the eighteenth century, most differences were spellings that did not change the meaning. The Dead Sea Scrolls let us look a thousand years further back. The biblical scrolls from Masada and Wadi Murabba'at, from the first and second centuries CE, are almost identical in their consonants to the medieval text (see [What the Dead Sea Scrolls show](/articles/what-the-dead-sea-scrolls-show/)). The rules did not create the Masoretic text. They preserved a text that already existed.

## What the rules cannot do

Careful copying keeps a text from changing. It cannot correct a mistake made before the rules took hold. In 1 Samuel 13:1, the Hebrew gives Saul's age and length of reign in a form that seems to have lost a number, and it has been copied that way ever since.

So the rules are no guarantee that every letter is as the author wrote it. They are something more modest and more real: evidence that for over a thousand years, scribe after scribe treated each letter as important, and passed on the text they received.
