Philippians 4:6–7 is one of the most loved passages in Paul's letters, and one of the most quoted to anxious people. Read closely, it says more than "don't worry". Here is the Balanced level:

> {{OWB:PHP.4.6.s1:B}}

## Where the words come

Paul wrote this letter in chains (1:7, 13), not knowing whether he would live or die (1:20). The church at Philippi was dear to him, but it had trouble of its own. Just before these verses he pleads with two women, Euodia and Syntyche, to agree in the Lord (4:2). Then come short commands: rejoice in the Lord always; let your gentleness be known to everyone; "the Lord is near" (4:4–5).

That last phrase is the hinge. The record marks it uncertain, because "near" can mean the Lord's return is close, or that he is close to his people now. Paul may mean both. Either way, the command not to worry follows from it.

In Greek, verses 6 and 7 are one sentence, so they are one record. The Literal level keeps it as one, joined by a semicolon:

> {{OWB:PHP.4.6.s1:L}}

Balanced breaks it into three English sentences, and Readable into four, but all three levels say the same thing.

## "Do not worry"

The verb {{GREEK:PHP.4.6:2}} is anxious care, the same word Jesus uses in Matthew 6:25:

> {{OWB:MAT.6.25.s1:B}}

Paul can use the word in a good sense. In 2:20 it describes Timothy's genuine concern for the church. Here the context makes it worry, the kind that crowds God out. Peter uses the related noun when he tells his readers to throw every worry onto God, because God cares for them (1 Peter 5:7). The Greek puts "nothing" first for weight; Literal keeps that order: "Be anxious about nothing".

## Prayer, and specific requests

Paul sets "in everything" against the "nothing" he began with: worry about nothing, pray about everything. Then he uses two words for prayer. The first, {{GREEK:PHP.4.6:7}}, is prayer of every kind. The second, {{GREEK:PHP.4.6:10}}, is asking for something particular. Together they mean: bring everything to God, including the specifics. The verb at the end asks that your requests "be made known" to God. Jesus said the Father knows what we need before we ask (Matthew 6:8). Paul still tells the Philippians to bring it to him, every part of it, in words. Readable says it plainly:

> {{OWB:PHP.4.6.s1:R}}

## Thanks with the asking

The Greek says the requests are made "with thanksgiving". The thanks goes with the asking. It is not something to offer later, once the answer has come. The record's decision states this, and it is a small grammatical point with a large consequence for how we pray.

## A promise, not a payment

Verse 7 begins with "and". What follows is the result of bringing everything to God, not a reward for praying well. God's peace is promised, not earned. Two verses later Paul says something larger still. Here it is the peace of God that guards; there, the God of peace himself will be with you (4:9). The record for verse 9 reads that phrase as the God who is the source of peace, and Readable says "the God who gives peace".

## Beyond understanding

The phrase about understanding can mean that God's peace is beyond what the mind can grasp, or that it achieves more than any human planning could. Both fit. The translation follows the more common reading, marks the decision uncertain, and notes the other in a footnote.

## A garrison

The verb in verse 7, {{GREEK:PHP.4.7:10}}, is what a garrison does. Paul uses it of the guard set on Damascus to catch him (2 Corinthians 11:32), and of the law holding God's people in custody (Galatians 3:23).

Philippi knew garrisons. After the battle of Philippi in 42 BC, Antony and Octavian made it a Roman colony and settled veteran soldiers there. After his victory at Actium in 31 BC, Octavian refounded the colony with more settlers. Acts 16:12 calls it a colony. Paul's readers would have known what it looked like when soldiers stood guard over a city.

So the Balanced level says God's peace will "stand guard over" your hearts and minds, and the Readable level that it will "keep watch over" them. Both keep the military picture without the older, vaguer "keep" of the King James Version. Literal says simply "will guard".

## Hearts and minds, in Christ

In biblical language the heart is the centre of the whole person, and the second word is a thought or plan. Heart and mind together mean the whole inner life.

The sentence ends "in Christ Jesus". That is where the guarding happens: in union with him. Readable spells this out: "because you are joined to" him. That adds a "because" the Greek leaves implicit, so the rendering decision is marked uncertain and flagged for reviewers to check.
