Imagine a synagogue in Galilee in the third century. A reader stands and reads a verse of the Torah in Hebrew, from a scroll. Beside him stands a second man, with no scroll, who repeats the verse aloud in Aramaic, the language of the street and the market. Then the reader reads the next verse, and the translator renders it in turn. Verse by verse, the congregation hears the scripture twice: once in the holy language, once in their own.

The Aramaic renderings that grew out of this practice are called **Targums**, from an Aramaic word meaning "translation" or "interpretation." Together with the Greek [Septuagint](/articles/the-septuagint/), they are the oldest translations of the Hebrew Bible, and they are unusual because they were never meant to replace the original. The Hebrew was always read first.

## Why Aramaic?

Aramaic is a close relative of Hebrew. It became the common language of the Assyrian and Persian empires, and after the Babylonian exile many Jews spoke it in daily life. Parts of the Bible itself are in Aramaic, most of Daniel 2–7 and sections of Ezra. A congregation that understood Aramaic better than Hebrew needed help to follow the reading.

Jewish tradition traced the practice back to Ezra, who read the Law to the people while the Levites helped them understand:

> They read in the book, in the law of God, distinctly; and they gave the sense, so that they understood the reading. (Nehemiah 8:8)

The Babylonian Talmud reads "distinctly" as a reference to Targum (*Megillah* 3a). Most modern scholars think this reads a later custom back into the text. But it says something true about what a Targum was for: giving the sense, so that the people understood.

## The translator in the synagogue

The person who gave the Aramaic was the **meturgeman**, the "translator." The Mishnah, compiled around 200 CE, lays down rules for him (*Megillah* 4). The Torah reader gives him one verse at a time; from the Prophets, up to three. Some passages were read in Hebrew but not translated aloud, among them Reuben's sin with his father's concubine (Genesis 35:22).

One saying, attributed to Rabbi Judah in the second century, captures the translator's dilemma: whoever translates a verse strictly literally is a liar, and whoever adds to it is a blasphemer (Babylonian Talmud, *Qiddushin* 49a). A translation must carry the meaning, not just the words, without slipping in ideas of its own. Every translator since has faced the same tension (see [Word for word or meaning for meaning?](/articles/word-for-word-or-meaning-for-meaning/)).

Written Targums existed early. Among the [Dead Sea Scrolls](/articles/what-the-dead-sea-scrolls-show/) are a substantial Aramaic translation of **Job** from Cave 11, copied around the turn of the era, and fragments of Targums of Job and Leviticus from Cave 4.

## Onqelos and Jonathan

Two Targums came to have special authority. **Targum Onqelos** covers the Torah. Most scholars think it took shape in the land of Israel in the first or second century CE and was standardised in Babylonia. It is fairly literal, but its small changes are telling. It softens descriptions of God in human terms: where the Hebrew says God "came down" or "saw," Onqelos may say he "revealed himself." It often speaks of "the *Memra*," the "word," of the Lord acting. And in Genesis 49:10, where the sceptre will not depart from Judah "until he comes to whom it belongs," Onqelos names the Messiah.

**Targum Jonathan** covers the Prophets, from Joshua to the Twelve. It is freer than Onqelos. Its Isaiah names the Messiah at the start of the servant passage: in Bruce Chilton's English rendering of Isaiah 52:13, "Behold, my servant, the Messiah, shall prosper," though the rest of the chapter is read very differently from Christian readings.

Both were copied beside the Hebrew in medieval manuscripts and printed in the Rabbinic Bibles of the sixteenth century. Yemenite Jews still read Onqelos aloud, verse by verse, in the synagogue.

## The Palestinian Targums

A second family of Torah Targums, from the land of Israel, is far more expansive, adding stories, sermons and explanations. In 1956 the Spanish scholar **Alejandro Díez Macho** identified a manuscript in the Vatican Library, *Neofiti 1*, as a nearly complete Palestinian Targum of the whole Torah. The copy is from the sixteenth century; the translation is older, by how much is debated.

[Genesis 4:8](/gen/4/) shows how far such a Targum can go. The Hebrew says that "Cain said to Abel his brother," and then reports no words before the murder. The Samaritan Pentateuch, the Septuagint and other ancient versions supply a short line: "Let us go out to the field." Neofiti and its relatives supply a whole argument between the brothers, about whether the world was created in love, whether there is judgment, and whether the righteous are rewarded. The silence of the Hebrew has become a debate about divine justice.

## What the Targums teach a translator

A translation that adds nothing may leave readers puzzled. One that explains everything may bury the text under the translator's interpretation, and readers cannot tell which is which. The synagogue's answer was to keep the two side by side: the Hebrew read from the scroll, the Aramaic spoken beside it, never mistaken for each other.

Open Word Bible keeps a similar line visible in a different way. In Genesis 4:8 it translates the Hebrew as it stands, with Cain's words missing, and a footnote gives the short line the other versions add.
