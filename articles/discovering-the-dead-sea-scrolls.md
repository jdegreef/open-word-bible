In the winter of 1946–47, a young Bedouin shepherd named **Muhammed edh-Dhib**, of the Ta'amireh tribe, was looking for a stray goat among the cliffs near the north-western shore of the Dead Sea. He threw a stone into an opening in the rock and heard something break. When he and his companions later climbed in, they found tall clay jars, and inside some of them, bundles of old leather wrapped in linen.

Those bundles were the first of the **Dead Sea Scrolls**. Over the next decade, eleven caves near the ruins of Khirbet Qumran would yield the remains of roughly nine hundred manuscripts, written between about the third century BCE and the first century CE. Among them are the oldest copies of the books of the Hebrew Bible ever found.

## Not the first find

The caves had given up books before. The church historian Eusebius reports that Origen, the great Christian scholar of the third century, used a Greek translation of the Old Testament that had been found in a jar near Jericho (*Church History* 6.16). Around the year 800, Timothy I, the patriarch of the Church of the East in Baghdad, wrote to a fellow bishop named Sergius about a story he had heard from Jewish converts. About ten years earlier, they said, a hunter's dog had gone into a cleft in the rocks near Jericho. Its owner followed and found a chamber full of books, among them books of the Old Testament and other writings in Hebrew. One of them, he was told, was a book of psalms of David with more than two hundred psalms. The Bible has 150. When the large Psalms scroll was found in Cave 11 in 1956, it too contained psalms that are not in the Bible, some of them previously known only in Syriac.

The dry desert air explains both stories. Leather and papyrus that would rot anywhere else could survive for two thousand years in a sealed jar in a cave above the Dead Sea.

## The first seven scrolls

The Bedouin took the scrolls to Bethlehem, where they eventually came to a cobbler and antiquities dealer, **Khalil Iskander Shahin**, known as Kando. From there the first seven scrolls were divided between two buyers.

- Four went to **Mar Athanasius Yeshue Samuel**, the Syrian Orthodox Metropolitan of Jerusalem, at St Mark's Monastery. They were the **Great Isaiah Scroll** (1QIsa-a), the **Community Rule**, a **commentary on Habakkuk**, and the badly damaged **Genesis Apocryphon**.
- Three were bought by **Eleazar Sukenik**, an archaeologist at the Hebrew University. They were a second, fragmentary **Isaiah scroll** (1QIsa-b), the **War Scroll**, and the **Thanksgiving Hymns**. Sukenik made his first purchase on 29 November 1947, the same day the United Nations voted to partition Palestine.

Scholars at the American Schools of Oriental Research in Jerusalem, including John Trever and Millar Burrows, photographed Mar Samuel's scrolls in early 1948. Trever compared the script of the Isaiah scroll with the Nash Papyrus, a small Hebrew text then thought to be the oldest known, and sent photographs to the archaeologist **William F. Albright**, who replied that it was "the greatest manuscript discovery of modern times." The announcement was made in April 1948.

Trever's photographs turned out to be more important than anyone expected. When he saw the scrolls again in 1966, the leather had darkened and the text was harder to read than he remembered. His 1948 pictures are still used as a record of what the scrolls looked like when they first came out of the caves.

Mar Samuel took his four scrolls to the United States. Unable to sell them for years, he placed a small advertisement in the *Wall Street Journal* on 1 June 1954 offering "The Four Dead Sea Scrolls." It was answered, through intermediaries, on behalf of **Yigael Yadin**, Sukenik's son, who bought them for the State of Israel for $250,000. All seven of the first scrolls are now displayed in the **Shrine of the Book** at the Israel Museum, Jerusalem, a building designed to look like the lid of one of the jars in which they were found.

## Eleven caves

Once it was known that the scrolls were valuable, the Bedouin searched the area thoroughly, and often found caves before the archaeologists. The official excavations were led by **Roland de Vaux** of the École Biblique in Jerusalem and **Gerald Lankester Harding** of the Jordanian Department of Antiquities, who explored Cave 1 in 1949 and then dug the nearby settlement of Khirbet Qumran from 1951 to 1956.

- **Cave 1** gave the first seven scrolls and fragments of dozens more.
- **Cave 3** (1952) held the **Copper Scroll**, a list of hidden treasure engraved on metal, still not explained. It was too corroded to unroll, so in 1955 and 1956 it was taken to Manchester, where Professor H. Wright Baker cut it into twenty-three strips with a fine saw so that it could be read.
- **Cave 4** (1952), found by the Bedouin within sight of the settlement, was the richest of all. It held some 15,000 fragments from more than 500 different manuscripts, many no bigger than a postage stamp.
- **Cave 11** (1956) gave several well-preserved scrolls, including a large Psalms scroll and the **Temple Scroll**, which Yadin acquired in 1967.

Cave 4 also showed the cost of the Bedouin's head start. From September 1952 its fragments began to appear on the antiquities market, and the Palestine Archaeological Museum in East Jerusalem, then under Jordanian rule, bought them to keep the collection together. When its money ran short, foreign institutions put up funds between 1954 and 1959, among them McGill University in Montreal, the universities of Manchester, Heidelberg and Oxford, and McCormick Theological Seminary in Chicago. They expected to receive fragments in return. In 1961 the Jordanian government nationalised the scrolls, and the fragments stayed in Jerusalem. The pieces were laid out under glass in a long room at the museum, which the team called the "scrollery."

In June 1967, during the Six-Day War, the museum (now called the Rockefeller Museum) came under Israeli control, and with it the Cave 4 fragments. In the same weeks Yadin obtained the Temple Scroll from Kando, who had kept it hidden for years; the dealer was later paid for it.

In 2017, archaeologists from the Hebrew University announced a twelfth cave near Qumran, containing broken jars and a blank scroll but no texts: probably looted long ago.

Other sites in the Judean desert produced related finds: biblical scrolls at **Masada**, the fortress where Jewish rebels held out against Rome until 73 or 74 CE; documents and biblical texts at **Wadi Murabba'at** and **Nahal Hever** from the time of the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 CE); and a Greek scroll of the Minor Prophets from Nahal Hever. As recently as 2021, the Israel Antiquities Authority announced new fragments of that Greek scroll, from Zechariah and Nahum, found in the "Cave of Horror" during a survey to stay ahead of looters. They were the first biblical scroll fragments found in an excavation in some sixty years. Like the rest of the scroll, they write the Greek text but give the name of God in Hebrew letters. The archaeologists think the fragments were carried into the cave by refugees at the end of the Bar Kokhba revolt, around 135 CE.

## What is in the collection?

The roughly nine hundred manuscripts fall into three broad groups.

**Biblical manuscripts.** More than two hundred of the Qumran manuscripts are copies of books of the Hebrew Bible. Every book is represented except Esther (and Nehemiah, which ancient Jews counted as one book with Ezra). The most frequently copied are Psalms, Deuteronomy, Genesis and Isaiah. Most are fragmentary; the Great Isaiah Scroll is the only one that is complete.

**Other Jewish writings known before 1947.** These include books such as Tobit, Sirach and Jubilees, and parts of 1 Enoch, some found for the first time in their original languages.

**Writings previously unknown.** Rules for a community, hymns, prayers, calendars, commentaries on prophetic books, legal texts and visions of the end of the age. These are the texts that sparked most of the public debate.

## Who wrote them?

De Vaux concluded that the scrolls belonged to a community living at Qumran, and identified it with the **Essenes**, a Jewish group described by Josephus, Philo and the Roman writer Pliny the Elder, who places Essenes on the western shore of the Dead Sea (*Natural History* 5.73). This remains the majority view, though in modified forms. Some scholars, such as Norman Golb, argued that the scrolls came from libraries in Jerusalem, hidden in the caves before the Romans destroyed the city in 70 CE. Others think Qumran was one of several Essene settlements, and that the scrolls were collected over a long period.

For the history of the Bible, the question matters less than it might seem. Whoever owned them, the biblical scrolls were copied by Jewish scribes over several centuries, and many were clearly brought to Qumran from elsewhere. They show what the text of the Hebrew Bible looked like in the last centuries before Jesus.

## Dating the scrolls

How do we know how old they are? Three independent methods agree.

- **Archaeology.** Pottery and coins at Qumran show that the settlement was occupied from the late second or early first century BCE and destroyed by the Romans around 68 CE.
- **Palaeography.** The study of handwriting allows scholars to place scripts in a sequence. Frank Moore Cross's classic study of Jewish scripts dated most of the scrolls between about 250 BCE and 68 CE.
- **Radiocarbon dating.** Tests carried out in Zurich in 1990–91 and in Tucson in 1994, on samples from a range of scrolls, gave dates broadly in agreement with the palaeographic estimates.

## Fakes on the market

The high prices brought forgers. After 2002, about seventy new "Dead Sea Scroll" fragments appeared on the antiquities market, many of them bought by collectors and Christian institutions in the United States. Scholars grew suspicious of them. In 2020 an investigation commissioned by the Museum of the Bible in Washington concluded that all sixteen of its fragments were modern forgeries, probably written on old leather and then treated to look ancient. These findings do not touch the genuine scrolls, whose history from the caves to the museums is well documented. They do show why that history matters.

## The long road to publication

The scrolls from Cave 1 were published quickly. Cave 4 was a different matter. Its tens of thousands of fragments were entrusted to a small international team, who spent years sorting, joining and identifying them. Publication in the official series, *Discoveries in the Judaean Desert* (DJD), was slow, and by the 1980s many texts were still unpublished, more than thirty years after their discovery.

Critics also pointed out that the team formed in 1953 had included no Jewish or Israeli scholar, although the scrolls are Jewish texts. Israelis such as Emanuel Tov and Elisha Qimron joined the work only in the 1980s. Change began in 1990, when the chief editor, John Strugnell, was removed after an interview in which he made offensive remarks about Judaism. Frustration boiled over in 1991. Ben Zion Wacholder and Martin Abegg used a concordance prepared by the team to reconstruct unpublished texts by computer; the Biblical Archaeology Society published a facsimile edition of photographs; and the Huntington Library in California announced that it would allow access to its set of photographs. The monopoly was broken. Meanwhile, under a new editor-in-chief, **Emanuel Tov**, appointed in 1990, the official team was greatly enlarged, and the DJD series was essentially completed in the 2000s, with some forty volumes.

Today anyone can view high-resolution images of the scrolls online. The Israel Museum's **Digital Dead Sea Scrolls** project, built with Google, went live in September 2011 with five of the great scrolls from Cave 1, including the Great Isaiah Scroll. The Israel Antiquities Authority's **Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library** followed in December 2012. It photographs every fragment in several wavelengths of light, including infrared, which can bring out letters that have faded beyond reading to the naked eye.

## Why it matters

Before 1947, the oldest substantial Hebrew manuscripts of the Old Testament dated from around 900–1000 CE, among them the great codices described in [The Aleppo and Leningrad codices](/articles/aleppo-and-leningrad-codices/). Scholars could compare those books with each other and with the ancient translations, but they could not see the Hebrew text itself as it stood before the time of Jesus. Overnight, scholars had copies roughly a thousand years older. The next two articles look at what those copies show: first [the Great Isaiah Scroll](/articles/the-great-isaiah-scroll/), and then [what the scrolls show about the Bible's text](/articles/what-the-dead-sea-scrolls-show/) as a whole.
