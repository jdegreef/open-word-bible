Before printing, every copy of the New Testament was made by hand. More than five thousand Greek manuscripts survive, from scraps of papyrus to complete Bibles, and no two of them are exactly alike. Most of the differences are small: a spelling, a word order, a "the" added or left out. A few are larger. Scholars call these differences *variants*, and the work of weighing them *textual criticism*. See [How textual criticism works](/articles/how-textual-criticism-works/) for the method itself.

A translation has to decide which words it is translating. This article explains how Open Word Bible does it, and what a reader sees when the manuscripts part ways.

## One fixed text

Open Word Bible translates a single published edition of the Greek: the SBL Greek New Testament, edited by Michael Holmes and published in 2010, pinned to an exact version. Its editor weighed the manuscripts and printed the text he judged most likely to be original. For the Old Testament the base is the Westminster Leningrad Codex, a digital edition of the oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Bible. Starting from fixed editions means every word of the translation can be traced to a known source text, and every result can be checked again later. See [Why we never type Greek or Hebrew by hand](/articles/never-typed-by-hand/).

Following an edition is not the same as hiding its choices. Where the manuscripts differ in a way a reader might notice, the record for the sentence has a meaning decision in the category *text variant*. It names the reading followed, gives the alternatives, and explains the evidence in plain language. There are already more than a thousand such decisions across the books translated so far.

## When there is a footnote

Not every variant needs a footnote. In John 3:16 many manuscripts read "his Son" where some of the oldest have "the Son". English needs "his" anyway, the meaning is the same, and the record notes the variant without a footnote.

When a variant changes what a reader would understand, there is a footnote. Ephesians 1:1 is an example. Almost all later manuscripts address the letter to the believers {{GREEK:EPH.1.1:12-13}}, "in Ephesus". Some of the earliest, among them the papyrus P46 and the first hands of Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, do not have the words. The translation follows the SBL edition and keeps "in Ephesus", and a footnote gives the shorter reading. The record adds that the letter has no personal greetings and speaks of the readers' faith as something Paul has heard about, so it may have been written for several churches in the province of Asia, with Ephesus as the chief city.

## How the evidence is weighed

The records use the same few questions again and again. They are the ordinary tools of textual criticism, put in plain words:

- **How old are the witnesses?** A reading found in the papyri of the second and third centuries and in the great fourth-century codices, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, starts with a strong claim.
- **How widely is it spread?** A reading found in different regions and in early translations into Latin, Syriac and Coptic is harder to explain as one scribe's slip.
- **Which reading explains the others?** Scribes tended to smooth out what was hard, explain what was strange and add to what seemed incomplete. So the harder or shorter reading often turns out to be the earlier one, though not always.
- **Does it fit the author?** Style, vocabulary and the author's habits elsewhere count as evidence.

John 1:18 shows these questions at work. Most later manuscripts call Jesus "the one and only Son"; the oldest, the papyri P66 and P75 with Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, read "the one and only God". The record follows "God": it has the oldest witnesses, and it is the harder reading, which scribes were more likely to change to the familiar "Son" than the other way round. A footnote gives "Son". See ["One and only" or "only begotten"?](/articles/one-and-only-son/).

## John 5:4: a verse that is not there

Readers of older Bibles sometimes find that John 5 skips from verse 3 to verse 5. The missing verse tells of an angel who stirred the water of the pool at Bethesda, so that the first sick person into the water was healed.

> {{OWB:JHN.5.3.s1:B}}

The record explains why the verse is not in the text. The oldest manuscripts, among them two early papyri and the codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, do not have it. Some later ones have only part of it, others mark it as doubtful, and its wording varies a good deal from copy to copy. It seems to have been added to explain what the sick man says in verse 7. The verse number stays empty, so that references still match other Bibles, and a footnote gives the added words in full.

Several other verse numbers are empty for the same reason, among them Mark 9:44 and 9:46, Luke 17:36 and Acts 8:37. Each record says why.

## 1 John 5:7: three that testify

The King James Version reads, in 1 John 5:7, "For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one." Modern translations do not have these words. Open Word Bible reads:

> {{OWB:1JN.5.7.s1:B}}

The longer wording is known as the *Johannine Comma*. The record sets out why it is left out. It is found in no Greek manuscript before the late Middle Ages, and then only in a handful of late copies, some with the words added in the margin by a later hand. No Greek writer of the early church quotes it, even in the fourth-century debates about the Trinity, where it would have been the most useful verse to cite. It first appears in Latin writers and then in later copies of the Latin Bible, probably as a comment on the three witnesses that was copied into the text. From there it entered the printed Greek New Testament in the sixteenth century, and so the King James Version.

The record also says something a worried reader needs to hear: the doctrine of the Trinity does not rest on this passage. A footnote gives the longer words.

## John 7:53–8:11: a story set apart

The story of the woman caught in adultery is the largest case in John. It is missing from the earliest manuscripts of John, among them P66 and P75, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, and from the early translations into other languages. The first manuscript known to have it is Codex Bezae, from the fifth century. In later copies it turns up in different places: here, after John 7:36 or 7:44, at the end of John, or in Luke after 21:38. Its style and vocabulary differ from the rest of John. It names the scribes and the Mount of Olives, which John does not otherwise mention.

Many scholars hold that it is an old and true account of Jesus that was handed down outside John and later written into the Gospel. Open Word Bible prints it in its usual place, set off in square brackets that open at 7:53 and close at the end of 8:11:

> {{OWB:JHN.7.53.s1:B}}

The record considered two other options: leaving the passage out and giving it only in a note, or printing it as plain John. It chose the middle course the SBL edition also takes. It is careful about what the evidence shows. The evidence shows that the passage was not part of the earliest copies of John. It does not decide whether the events took place.

## The end of Mark

Mark's Gospel raises the largest question of all. The two oldest complete manuscripts of the New Testament, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, both from the fourth century, end Mark at 16:8, with the women running from the empty tomb: {{GREEK:MRK.16.8:17-18}}, "for they were afraid".

Most manuscripts, nearly all of them later, go on with the twelve verses known as the longer ending (16:9–20). The record notes that its style and vocabulary differ from the rest of Mark, that it introduces Mary Magdalene as if she had not just been named in 16:1, and that its appearances read like a summary of those in Luke, John and Acts. Some manuscripts have instead a short ending of a sentence or two, and a few have both.

Open Word Bible follows the SBL edition, which prints both endings after 16:8 and marks them as old and widely read but not part of the original text. The translation gives both, with footnotes. A second decision is marked uncertain, because the manuscripts cannot answer it: did Mark mean to end at "for they were afraid", or was his ending lost? A book ending on the Greek word for "for" is very unusual. Some think the last page of the earliest copy was lost. Others think Mark ended there on purpose, leaving the reader at the empty tomb with the promise of 16:7. The record gives both views and does not choose.

## When the evidence is evenly balanced

Some variants are not settled even among careful scholars, and the records say so. Luke 22:43–44, where an angel strengthens Jesus in Gethsemane and his sweat falls like drops of blood, is missing from several important early witnesses, among them P75 and Vaticanus, but present in others, among them the first hand of Sinaiticus and Codex Bezae, and known to writers of the second century. Jesus's prayer from the cross in Luke 23:34 has a similar spread of evidence:

> {{OWB:LUK.23.34.s1:B}}

In both places the translation follows the SBL edition and keeps the words. It marks the decision uncertain, gives the other reading in a footnote, and records the arguments on both sides: copyists may have added the words, or left them out because they found them troubling.

## The Old Testament too

Variants are not only a New Testament matter. The Dead Sea Scrolls include a nearly complete copy of Isaiah, the Great Isaiah Scroll, copied more than a thousand years before the Leningrad Codex. The two agree closely, but not in every word. In Isaiah, Open Word Bible's records weigh dozens of the scroll's readings, usually keeping the Hebrew of the Leningrad Codex and noting the scroll's wording.

Occasionally the record follows the scroll instead. In Isaiah 14:4 the traditional Hebrew text has a word found nowhere else, of unknown meaning. The scroll has a word one letter different that means "onslaught" or "raging", and the Greek Septuagint points the same way. The translation follows the scroll, marks the decision uncertain and gives a footnote. See [The Great Isaiah Scroll](/articles/the-great-isaiah-scroll/).

## Reviewed against the evidence

Most text variant decisions end with a request: a reviewer should check this against the apparatus, the scholarly list of manuscript readings printed with an edition. Some records go further and say that a detail was given from memory and must be checked. That honesty matters. The first drafts are AI-assisted, and summaries of manuscript evidence are exactly the kind of thing a human scholar should verify, witness by witness. Until a named reviewer does, the record stays a draft. See [AI-assisted translation and human review](/articles/ai-assisted-translation-and-human-review/).

None of this should alarm a reader. The large variants are few, they are well known, and every modern translation deals with the same ones. What Open Word Bible adds is that the reasons are there to read, verse by verse. [Open Mark 16](/mrk/16/) or [John 8](/jhn/8/) to see them.
