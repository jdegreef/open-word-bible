Before we ask how accurately the Bible was copied, it helps to ask what it was copied *onto*. The materials ancient scribes used explain which manuscripts survived and where. The form of their books explains something more surprising: the early Christians were among the first people to adopt the codex, the ancestor of the modern book.

## Papyrus and parchment

For most of antiquity the standard writing material of the Mediterranean world was **papyrus**, made from a reed that grew along the Nile. Pliny the Elder describes the process (*Natural History* 13.68–83): strips of pith were laid in two layers at right angles, pressed and beaten together, dried and polished. Sheets were glued edge to edge to make a roll. Papyrus was cheap and light, but it rots in damp climates. That is why almost all surviving ancient papyri, including the earliest copies of the New Testament, come from the dry sands of Egypt.

**Parchment** is animal skin, soaked in lime, scraped, stretched and dried. It is far stronger than papyrus, takes writing on both sides, and lasts even in a damp climate. Most of the Dead Sea Scrolls are on skin, and from the fourth century the great Bibles were written on parchment. The New Testament mentions both: 2 John 12 speaks of "paper and ink," and in 2 Timothy 4:13 Paul asks for "the books, especially the parchments."

## The scroll

For most of antiquity a "book" meant a **scroll**: a long roll written in columns on one side, which the reader unrolled with one hand and rolled up with the other. A long book like Luke or Acts filled a whole roll. Finding a passage meant winding through it. When Jesus reads in the synagogue at Nazareth, he is handed "the book of the prophet Isaiah," unrolls it to the place, and rolls it up again (Luke 4:17–20). Jewish scripture is still read from scrolls in the synagogue.

## The codex

The **codex** is a stack of sheets folded and fastened at one edge: a book with pages. Its ancestor was the set of wax tablets Romans tied together for notes and school exercises. By the first century, Romans also used small parchment notebooks. The poet Martial, around 85 CE, recommends pocket parchment editions of Homer and Virgil for travellers (*Epigrams* 14.184–192), which shows that the format was known for literature but still a novelty. Among Greeks and Romans, the scroll remained the normal form for literature until the fourth century.

## The Christian preference

Christians were different. Colin Roberts and T. C. Skeat, in *The Birth of the Codex*, gathered the evidence from the papyri. Among surviving Greek literary texts from the second century, only a small percentage are codices. Among Christian copies of the scriptures from the same period, almost all are. Larry Hurtado, revisiting the evidence in *The Earliest Christian Artifacts*, confirmed the pattern: early Christians overwhelmingly chose the codex for the books they treated as scripture, even when they used scrolls for other texts.

Why? Scholars have offered several explanations, none of them certain.

- **Practicality.** A codex is compact, uses both sides of the page, and makes it easy to find a passage. But these advantages were open to everyone, and non-Christians were much slower to adopt it.
- **Collections.** A codex holds far more than a scroll. Harry Gamble argues that an early collection of Paul's letters, too long for one roll, was published as a codex and set the pattern. Others point to the four Gospels.
- **Distinctiveness.** A codex looked different from both the Torah scroll and the pagan literary roll. It may have marked the Christian scriptures as something new.
- **Working books.** Codices were associated with practical texts rather than prestige editions. Christian scriptures were books to be read in worship, studied, carried by travelling teachers and consulted.

Whatever the reason, the choice mattered. A codex could bind the four Gospels together, or all of Paul's letters, or eventually the whole Bible, in one volume. The single-volume Bibles of the fourth century, [Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus](/articles/sinaiticus-and-vaticanus/), would have been impossible as scrolls. The way we read the Bible today, as one book with pages, began with the choices of the first Christian copyists, who are the subject of the [next article](/articles/early-christian-copyists/).
