Few translation choices carry as much history as this one. The Gospel of John uses the Greek word {{LEMMA:JHN.1.19:11}} (*Ioudaios*, plural *Ioudaioi*) more than seventy times, far more than Matthew, Mark and Luke together. Often it simply names a people: "salvation comes from the Jews." Often it names the men in Jerusalem who question Jesus, try to stone him and hand him over to Pilate. Read without care, the second group can swallow the first, as if "the Jews" as a whole were Jesus's enemies. That reading has been used against Jewish people for centuries, by Christians who forgot that Jesus, his mother, his disciples and almost everyone else in the Gospel were Jews.

English Bibles have answered in three main ways: "the Jews" everywhere, "the Judeans", or "the Jewish leaders" where the context shows the authorities. This article sets out the options and what Open Word Bible does.

## One word, several senses

In the drafts of John the word occurs 71 times, and each occurrence has its own meaning decision saying which sense it has there. The senses fall into a few groups.

**The Jewish people.** At the well in Samaria the woman calls Jesus "a Jew", and Jesus answers her as one of his people:

> {{OWB:JHN.4.22.s1:B}}

The meaning decision says that here the word "clearly means the Jewish people, and Jesus speaks as one of them." The same sense is used when John explains customs for readers who did not keep them: the stone jars "kept for Jewish ritual washing" (2:6), "the Jewish festival of Passover" (2:13), the way "the Jews prepare a body for burial" (19:40). It is also in the title on the cross:

> {{OWB:JHN.19.19.s1:B}}

There, the record notes, the word names the nation, as in any royal title, and every level keeps it.

**People in a particular scene.** Many of the word's uses name a group who are present at one moment. When Lazarus dies, "many Jews" come from Jerusalem to comfort Martha and Mary:

> {{OWB:JHN.11.19.s1:B}}

The decision calls them "the friends and neighbors who came to comfort the sisters", and adds that afterwards many of them believe in Jesus while some report to the Pharisees (11:45–46). Others are the hearers in the temple who argue with Jesus in chapters 8 and 10, the crowd in the synagogue at Capernaum who grumble in chapter 6, and the people of Jerusalem who read the notice on the cross (19:20).

**The authorities.** Very often, though, the word means the religious leaders in Jerusalem. The first time John uses it, they send priests and Levites to question John the Baptist:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.19.s1:B}}

The meaning decision gives the reasons: these men send an official delegation from Jerusalem, the envoys must report back to them (1:22), and verse 24 links them with the Pharisees. "That is the leadership, not the whole people." The same group persecutes Jesus over the Sabbath (5:16–18), questions the man born blind and his parents (9:18–22), and brings Jesus to Pilate (18:31; 19:7, 12). The clearest case is the fear that keeps people quiet:

> {{OWB:JHN.9.22.s1:B}}

As the record points out, the parents who are afraid are Jews themselves, and so are their son and Jesus. "The Jews" they fear cannot mean the Jewish people; it must mean the men with power over the synagogue.

## Option one: "the Jews" everywhere

The King James Version, and most word-for-word translations since, write "the Jews" in every place. This has a real strength. It shows the reader the word John actually wrote, and it does not hide the fact that John uses one word for all these groups. Some scholars argue that the choice belongs to John and should be left for readers and teachers to understand, and that a translation should not tidy up an ancient author.

Its weakness is just as real. In modern English "the Jews" means the Jewish people as a whole, in every age. A reader who meets "the Jews were seeking to kill him" (5:18) with no further help will hear something John did not mean, since everyone in the scene, Jesus included, was Jewish.

## Option two: "the Judeans"

Some scholars argue that in ancient Greek the word first meant a people tied to a place, Judea, the way "Egyptians" or "Galileans" did, and that "Jew" in the modern religious sense is a later idea. On that view "Judeans" would be more accurate in all ancient texts, and in John it would also explain why the opponents are so often in Jerusalem and why Jesus, a Galilean, is treated as an outsider there. Some social-science commentaries on John render the word "Judeans" throughout.

Others answer that the change has its own cost. Adele Reinhartz and other scholars have warned that "Judeans" can make Jews disappear from ancient history, and that in John it lets the Gospel's sharpest words pass by modern Jewish readers as if they were about someone else. It also fits some verses badly. In 6:41 the people who grumble are Galileans in the synagogue at Capernaum, people who know Jesus's family. And when the Samaritan woman says "a Jew like you", she is not asking where he lives.

Open Word Bible considers "the people of Judea" as an alternative in its meaning decisions, but does not use "Judean" for the people in John. It does use it for the land: Jesus goes "into the Judean countryside" (3:22). In Jeremiah and 2 Kings, where the Hebrew word names the people of the kingdom of Judah in the sixth century BC, the Literal and Balanced levels say "Judeans", and the record explains: "The later word 'Jew' comes from it, but here it names the people of the kingdom that had just fallen." That is a judgment about each book, not a rule for every ancient text.

## Option three: name the group the context shows

The third approach translates the sense in each place. Where the word means the authorities, the translation says so; where it means the people, it says "Jews" or "Jewish". The New International Version and the New Living Translation, among others, do this in many verses.

The objection is that a translator now decides, verse by verse, who John meant, and the reader no longer sees that John used one word. In some scenes the line between leaders and crowd is not clear.

## What Open Word Bible does

The book's term list for John calls this "the most sensitive decision in the book", and the records combine the first and third approaches by level.

**Literal says "the Jews" every time,** so a reader can always see the word John wrote. Compare John 1:19 at the Literal level with the Balanced level quoted above:

> {{OWB:JHN.1.19.s1:L}}

**Balanced and Readable name the authorities where the context shows them.** In 27 of the 71 places they say "the Jewish leaders". Each of these has a footnote giving the word John wrote, so nothing is hidden: a reader of the Balanced text can see that John says "the Jews" and that the translation has made a judgment. About thirty decisions in John carry such a footnote.

**Where the word means the people, every level keeps it.** Balanced and Readable say "the Jews", "Jews" or "Jewish": "a Jew like you" (4:9), "salvation comes from the Jews" (4:22), "the King of the Jews" (18:33; 19:19), "where all the Jews come together" (18:20).

**Where it means a group in one scene, Balanced and Readable keep the word and tie it to the scene.** This is the step that matters most for the passages where the line is blurred. The hearers in the temple are "the Jews who were arguing with him" (8:48) and "the Jews who had gathered around him" (10:31). The disciples warn Jesus about "the Jews at the temple" (11:8). Jesus leaves "the Jews of Judea" for a while (11:54). Each rendering decision gives the same reason: to keep the word John wrote and tie it to the people present, "so that no reader takes it to mean every Jew."

Here the records do not say "the Jewish leaders", because John does not single them out. At 10:24 the meaning decision says that leaders "may well have been among them", and marks the decision uncertain. It does not settle the question the text leaves open.

## Some verses that test the rule

**John 8:31.** Jesus speaks to Jews who have just come to believe him:

> {{OWB:JHN.8.31.s1:B}}

By verse 40 he tells the same hearers that they are seeking to kill him, and at 8:48 they call him a Samaritan with a demon. The meaning decisions follow the scene: these are hearers in the temple, first believing and then hostile, "not the people as a whole: Jesus is himself a Jew (4:9, 22), and so are his disciples." The records keep "Jews" here, since nothing marks them as the authorities.

**John 6:41 and 6:52.** In Capernaum the crowd that ate the loaves grumbles and argues. Literal and Balanced say "the Jews"; Readable says "the Jewish people there", and its decision explains that it does not say "leaders", "because nothing in the scene points to the authorities."

**John 19:12.** At the trial the accusers shout "If you let this man go, you are no friend of Caesar." John names the chief priests as their spokesmen in verse 15, and Balanced says "the Jewish leaders"; the decision allows that others may have stood with them, "but John does not describe a crowd."

## Why John writes this way

Scholars have long asked why John uses the word so often and so sharply. One widely held view links it to the experience of John's first readers. Three times the Gospel speaks of being put out of the synagogue for confessing Jesus (9:22; 12:42; 16:2). J. Louis Martyn argued in 1968 that this reflects a painful split between Jewish followers of Jesus and their synagogue late in the first century, which colours how the story is told. Not every scholar accepts his reconstruction, but most agree that the Gospel was written by people who were themselves Jewish, in an argument within a community, not by outsiders about a foreign people.

None of that changes the words of the text, and Open Word Bible does not change them either. What a translation can do is make sure that the reader is not misled by a shift in English that John never intended. The Literal level shows the word; the Balanced and Readable levels show the sense; the footnotes and decisions show the reasons.
