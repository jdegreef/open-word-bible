The **Christian Standard Bible** (CSB) is one of the younger major English translations, and one of the fastest growing. It is a thorough revision of the **Holman Christian Standard Bible** (HCSB), first published in full in 2004. Both are published by Holman Bible Publishers, part of **LifeWay Christian Resources**, the publishing arm of the Southern Baptist Convention, though their translators came from many denominations.

This article traces the history of the two, explains the approach their makers call **optimal equivalence**, and looks at some of their distinctive choices, including the HCSB's use of the name "Yahweh" and the CSB's return to "LORD." Both translations are under copyright, so their wording is described rather than quoted, apart from a few words.

## Arthur Farstad's project

The story begins with **Arthur L. Farstad** (1935–1998), a New Testament scholar who had been general editor of the **New King James Version**, whose New Testament appeared in 1979 (see [The King James Version and the New King James Version](/articles/kjv-and-nkjv/)). With Zane Hodges he also edited an edition of the Greek New Testament according to the **Majority Text**, the form of the text found in most surviving manuscripts (see [The Greek texts behind New Testaments](/articles/greek-texts-behind-new-testaments/)).

In 1984 Farstad began work on a new English translation, independent of the King James tradition. For more than a decade the project moved slowly. Then in 1998 LifeWay agreed to fund and publish it. Farstad died later that year, and **Edwin A. Blum**, already a leading member of the team, became general editor.

Under Blum the project took its final shape. The New Testament was translated from the standard critical editions of the Greek, the Nestle-Aland 27th edition and the United Bible Societies' fourth edition, not from the Majority Text, and the Old Testament from *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*, the standard printed edition of the Masoretic Text. The translation team grew to about a hundred scholars from seventeen denominations. All affirmed the inerrancy of Scripture, a conviction stated in the translation's introduction.

## The Holman Christian Standard Bible

The HCSB New Testament appeared in 1999 and the whole Bible in March 2004. A revised edition followed in 2009.

LifeWay's investment came at a time of tension over Bible translation in the Southern Baptist Convention. In 1997, amid controversy over plans for a gender-inclusive NIV, the convention passed a resolution urging publishers to resist gender-neutral translation, and in 2002 it passed another against the TNIV, a revision of the NIV with more inclusive language, which LifeWay declined to sell (see [The New International Version](/articles/the-niv/)). The HCSB gave the denomination's publisher a modern translation of its own, in the hands of scholars it trusted. Like any publisher's own translation, it also had commercial advantages, a subject discussed in [Why Bible translations are copyrighted](/articles/why-bible-translations-are-copyrighted/).

The HCSB had several distinctive features:

- **"Yahweh."** In places where the Old Testament stresses the personal name of God, the HCSB used **Yahweh** rather than the traditional "LORD." The first edition did so in fewer than a hundred places; the 2009 revision used it several hundred times, though still in only a small fraction of the more than six thousand occurrences of the name.
- **"Messiah."** Where the Greek *christos* functions as a title rather than a personal name, especially in the Gospels and Acts, the HCSB often translated it "Messiah," to bring out its Jewish meaning.
- **"Slave."** The HCSB often rendered the Greek *doulos* as "slave" rather than "servant," arguing that "servant" softens the total belonging the word implies.
- **Bullet notes.** Key theological terms were marked with a bullet, referring readers to short explanations at the back of the Bible.

## Why "Yahweh"?

The Hebrew Bible refers to God by a personal name, written with four consonants, YHWH. Out of reverence, Jewish readers from ancient times have avoided pronouncing it, reading *Adonai*, "my Lord," in its place. Most English Bibles follow that custom by printing "LORD" in small capitals (see [The divine name in translation](/articles/the-divine-name-in-translation/)). Psalm 23:1 shows the difference. The King James Version reads:

> The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want. (Psalm 23:1, KJV)

The World English Bible, which uses the name throughout, reads:

> Yahweh is my shepherd: I shall lack nothing. (Psalm 23:1, WEB)

The HCSB's makers argued that in some passages the name itself is the point, as when God declares "I am Yahweh," and that a title such as "the LORD" hides this from readers. Critics found the HCSB's practice inconsistent. If the name matters, why use it in some verses and not others? Others felt that "Yahweh" was unfamiliar in worship, or uncomfortable for readers who share the Jewish reluctance to pronounce it.

## From HCSB to CSB

In June 2016 LifeWay announced that the HCSB would be revised and renamed. The **Christian Standard Bible** was published in 2017. The revision was overseen by a **Translation Oversight Committee** co-chaired by **Thomas R. Schreiner**, a New Testament scholar at the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, and **David L. Allen**, then dean of theology at Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary. The committee and its translators again came from a range of conservative evangelical traditions, not only Southern Baptist.

Schreiner has described the CSB as a revision of the HCSB, not a new translation. But the changes were extensive, and many went in the same direction: toward wording closer to other modern English Bibles and toward smoother English. The New Testament now followed the newer Nestle-Aland 28th edition and the United Bible Societies' fifth edition.

Among the most visible changes:

- **"LORD" restored.** The CSB returned to rendering YHWH as "LORD" in small capitals, in line with most English translations, setting aside the HCSB's "Yahweh."
- **"Brothers and sisters."** Where Paul addresses a whole church as *adelphoi*, the CSB often has "brothers and sisters," with a note giving the literal word. The committee described this as accuracy, since the letters were addressed to men and women, while keeping masculine language where the original refers to men. See [Gender language in Bible translation](/articles/gender-language-in-translation/).
- **Smoother style.** Many sentences were recast for easier reading aloud.

A further small update, the **CSB 2020** text, changed less than one per cent of the wording, mostly footnotes, punctuation and individual word choices. One change shows how a single word can carry a long theological debate. In Romans 3:25 Paul uses the Greek *hilasterion* of Christ. The King James Version reads:

> Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God; (Romans 3:25, KJV)

The World English Bible has "an atoning sacrifice," as did the CSB of 2017. The 2020 text changed it to "mercy seat," the name of the lid of the ark of the covenant where blood was sprinkled on the Day of Atonement, the sense the same Greek word has in the Septuagint and in Hebrews 9:5. The committee published an explanation of its choice.

## Optimal equivalence

The HCSB introduced, and the CSB kept, the phrase **optimal equivalence** for its method. The translators describe it as a balance between formal equivalence, which follows the words and structure of the original, and functional equivalence, which renders the sense in natural English (see [Word for word or meaning for meaning?](/articles/word-for-word-or-meaning-for-meaning/)). Their approach, in summary:

- Where a literal rendering is clear and natural English, use it.
- Where a literal rendering would be unclear or misleading, translate the meaning, with a footnote giving the literal wording where useful.
- Give priority to accuracy, but treat readability as a requirement, not a luxury.

Critics have pointed out that nearly every modern translation claims something similar. The NIV calls its aim a balance of accuracy and clarity; the ESV calls itself "essentially literal" but also renders many idioms by their sense. In a review in the journal *Themelios*, the New Testament scholar Mark Strauss, himself a member of the NIV's translation committee, described optimal equivalence as a middle path between formal and functional equivalence, and placed the CSB in the same middle zone as translations such as the NIV, the NET Bible and the Common English Bible. On that view the label describes where the CSB sits on the scale more than a new method. Its makers would answer that the label is meant to describe a practice of choosing, verse by verse, whichever approach best serves the reader, and that the term is useful precisely because it refuses to be either extreme.

## Reception

The CSB has been adopted by many Southern Baptist churches and by others beyond the denomination, and it is used in a wide range of study Bibles and church resources. Supporters praise its readability and its scholarly team, and appreciate that its footnotes often show the literal sense.

Criticisms have come from several directions:

- Readers who valued the HCSB's distinctive features regretted the loss of "Yahweh" and some of its more literal renderings.
- Some complementarian commentators examined the CSB's gender language closely, though it drew far less criticism than the TNIV or the NIV of 2011. Some egalitarian readers thought it did not go far enough.
- Others questioned whether yet another translation in the middle of the scale was needed at all, given the number already available.

All of these are matters on which careful readers differ.

## What the CSB shows

The HCSB and CSB illustrate several realities of modern Bible publishing: that translations are often sponsored by publishers with particular communities in view, that a translation's character can change markedly between editions, and that small decisions about a single word, such as "Yahweh" or "mercy seat," can carry both theological and practical weight.

Open Word Bible treats such choices as decisions to be stated and explained. For each sentence its reasoning record lists the options considered, and its three levels, Literal, Balanced and Readable, share one set of meaning decisions, so that a balance like "optimal equivalence" is not a single compromise but a choice the reader can see from both sides (see [Literal, Balanced, Readable](/articles/three-levels-one-meaning/)). Its renderings of the divine name are handled in the same open way. It translates from the SBL Greek New Testament and the Westminster Leningrad Codex, is released under CC0, and uses the CSB and other translations only for comparison, never as the basis for its wording. Its drafts are AI-assisted and remain unreviewed until named human reviewers approve them.
