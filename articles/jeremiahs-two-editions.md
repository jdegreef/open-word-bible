Every book of the Bible has places where the manuscripts disagree. In most books they are few and small. Jeremiah is different. In Open Word Bible's records it has 176 text-variant decisions, more than any other book: more than John (139), the Psalms (134) or Isaiah (122). Ninety-six of them have a footnote, and fifty are marked uncertain.

The main reason is that Jeremiah has come down to us in two editions. This article explains what they are, why scholars think the book exists in two forms, and what the translation does.

## The book tells us it grew

Jeremiah is the one prophet whose book describes how it was written. In the fourth year of King Jehoiakim, Jeremiah dictated his words to the scribe Baruch, who wrote them on a scroll and read them in the temple. The king had the scroll read to him, and as each few columns were read he cut them off with a knife and threw them into the fire (36:23). Then:

> {{OWB:JER.36.32.s1:B}}

The meaning decision draws out what this means: "Burning the scroll did not silence the word; the new scroll was fuller." Another decision notes that the verse "shows part of the way the book itself took shape, though it does not say which chapters the scroll held." A book that was dictated, burned, dictated again and added to is a book that could exist in more than one form.

## Two forms of Jeremiah

The Hebrew text that Jewish and Christian Bibles follow, the Masoretic text, is the longer form. The ancient Greek translation, the Septuagint, made by Jewish translators before the time of Jesus, gives a form of the book that is noticeably shorter. Open Word Bible's opening note at Jeremiah 1:1 sets out the differences:

- The Greek is "about one-eighth shorter". Estimates vary a little, and some put it nearer one-seventh.
- It lacks "many words and phrases and some whole passages".
- It places the oracles against the nations, chapters 46–51 in the Hebrew, in the middle of chapter 25, right after 25:13, and in a different order.

Some of the larger passages the Greek does not have:

- **33:14–26**, the promise of a righteous branch for David and of priests who will never lack a man, "the longest passage of the Hebrew book missing from the Greek".
- **39:4–13**, the flight and capture of Zedekiah and the burning of Jerusalem, told also in 2 Kings 25 and Jeremiah 52.
- **10:6–8 and 10**, lines of praise set between the descriptions of idol making.
- **17:1–4**, **29:16–20**, **51:44–49** in part, and **52:2–3** and **52:28–30**.
- In **27:16–22** the Greek is much shorter and ends with the temple vessels going to Babylon, without the promise that they will be brought back.

## Abridged, or an earlier edition?

For centuries the usual explanation was that the Greek translator had shortened the book, dropping repetitions and passages he found difficult. Jeremiah does repeat itself a good deal, and some of the missing lines appear elsewhere in the book. Verses 30:10–11, for example, are in the Greek only in their place in chapter 46.

The Dead Sea Scrolls changed the discussion. Among the fragments of Jeremiah found in Cave 4 at Qumran, some (known as 4QJer-b and 4QJer-d) have a Hebrew text of the shorter kind, close to the Greek, while others have the longer form. So the shorter form was not invented by a translator: it existed in Hebrew. Both forms were being copied in the same period. The note at 1:1 says this: "both forms of the book were copied in ancient times."

Many scholars, among them Emanuel Tov, now think the Greek translates an earlier Hebrew edition, and that the longer Masoretic text is a second, expanded edition, with headings, repetitions, fuller titles and some new passages added, perhaps by Jeremiah's followers. Others still think the translator abridged in places, or that both forms changed over time. The question is not settled, and the records do not try to settle it. For the scrolls themselves, see [What the Dead Sea Scrolls show about the Bible's text](/articles/what-the-dead-sea-scrolls-show/).

## Which edition does the translation follow?

Open Word Bible follows the Hebrew text of Jeremiah throughout. It translates the Westminster Leningrad Codex, the digital edition of the oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Bible, as it does for every Old Testament book. The Hebrew is the form the synagogue and the church have read for two thousand years, and it is the form every major English translation follows.

Following one edition is not the same as hiding the other. The records mention the Greek in three kinds of places.

**Where a whole passage is missing.** Each has a footnoted decision. At 10:6, for instance, the record says the old Greek "does not have verses 6-8 and 10, and it places verse 9 inside verse 5, so the description of the idols runs on without a break. A fragment of Jeremiah found at Qumran seems to share the shorter form." The decision is marked uncertain, because it is not clear which form is earlier here.

**Where the order differs.** Decisions at 25:13 and 46:1 explain the different place of the oracles against the nations. Chapters that stand later in the Greek get a short note: chapter 26 of the Hebrew "is chapter 33 of the Greek translation", chapter 29 is 36, chapter 35 is 42.

**Where a Greek reading changes the sense.** In these places the Hebrew is followed and the footnote gives the other reading. Two examples:

- **23:33.** The LORD tells Jeremiah how to answer people who mockingly ask for "the LORD's burden", the prophet's oracle. The Hebrew reads "What burden?" The same consonants, divided differently, read "You are the burden", and the Septuagint and the Latin Vulgate have that. The Hebrew is kept, and the decision notes that both readings say "that the people, not God's word, are the weight God will throw down."
- **46:15.** The Hebrew asks why Egypt's "mighty one" has been swept away. The Greek divides the letters differently and reads "Why has Apis fled?", Apis being the sacred bull of Memphis. If that is right, the verse mocks Egypt's god. The decision gives both and is marked uncertain.

Many of these notes say something unusual: "This description of the Greek is from general knowledge." The project's pinned sources are the Hebrew and Greek texts it translates, with their word data; they do not include the Septuagint or a critical apparatus, so claims about what the Greek reads are written from general knowledge and flagged for a reviewer to check against the published editions. That is part of why fifty of Jeremiah's decisions are marked uncertain.

## The other half: what the scribes wrote and what they read

About 84 of Jeremiah's 176 decisions concern the Greek. Nearly as many, about 78, concern something else: places where the Hebrew manuscripts themselves give two forms of a word.

The Masoretes, the scribes who copied and vocalized the Hebrew text in the early Middle Ages, would not change the letters they had received. Where they thought a word should be read differently, they left the written consonants alone and noted in the margin the form to be read aloud. The written form is called the *ketiv* ("what is written"), and the form read aloud the *qere* ("what is read"). Jeremiah has an unusually large number of them. See [The Masoretes: guardians of the Hebrew text](/articles/the-masoretes/).

Most are tiny: an older spelling, a singular for a plural, two letters in a different order. The records follow the form read aloud in the great majority and note that "the meaning is the same either way." Only a few change the sense enough for a footnote. One of them is in the picture of the tree by the water:

> {{OWB:JER.17.8.s1:B}}

The written consonants say the tree "will not see" when heat comes, the same words used of the desert shrub in verse 6. The scribes marked "will not fear" to be read, which fits the parallel line about the drought, "it has no worries". The record follows the reading and gives the written form in a footnote: "The meaning hardly changes: the tree is untroubled when the heat comes."

The remaining fourteen or so decisions concern places where some Hebrew manuscripts differ, or where the standard text is hard to make sense of. At 2:11, for example, Jewish tradition lists the verse among a few places where scribes are said to have changed a word out of reverence: "their glory" may once have read "my glory". The record keeps the text as written, with a footnote.

## Why so many notes?

Put together, the count is not a sign that Jeremiah's text is unreliable. Most of the 176 decisions record either a difference in spelling that the Hebrew scribes themselves marked, or a place where the Greek edition is shorter. The first kind affects almost nothing. The second is real, but it is well understood and well documented: two editions of one prophet's book, both ancient, both copied by faithful communities.

A reader of Open Word Bible can see both, without being made to choose. The text is the Hebrew edition that the church and synagogue have read. The footnotes show where the other edition differs, and the decisions explain why, and how sure the translation is.
