The first complete English Bible was made by John Wycliffe's followers in the fourteenth century. But English speakers had been putting parts of the Bible into their language for some seven hundred years before that, in **Old English**.

## A cowherd's song

The earliest story comes from **Bede**, who finished his *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* in 731 (book 4, chapter 24). **Caedmon**, a cowherd at the monastery of **Whitby** under the abbess **Hild**, used to slip away from feasts when the harp came round, because he knew no songs. One night a figure in a dream told him to sing about the creation, and he did. The monks then taught him the stories of scripture, and he turned them into English verse. Bede's account includes a miracle we cannot check, but historians accept that Caedmon was real and pioneered Christian poetry in English. A copy of Bede made around 737 preserves his first song in Northumbrian English. It begins *Nu scylun hergan hefaenricaes uard*: "Now we must praise the guardian of heaven's kingdom."

## Bede's last sentence

Bede also translated. His pupil Cuthbert describes his last days in May 735: though ill, he had been dictating an English version of John's Gospel, as far as the words:

> There is a boy here who has five barley loaves and two fish, but what are these among so many? (John 6:9)

Cuthbert says that on his last day, when the scribe told him one sentence remained, Bede finished it and died singing "Glory be to the Father." He does not say which work that sentence belonged to, and devotion may have shaped the scene. The translation itself is lost.

## Laws, glosses and prose

**King Alfred** of Wessex (reigned 871–899) opened his law code with the Ten Commandments and much of Exodus 21–23 in English, presenting his laws as heirs of the law of Moses.

In the mid-tenth century a priest named **Aldred** wrote English words between the lines of the Latin **Lindisfarne Gospels**, made some two and a half centuries earlier. His gloss is the oldest surviving English version of the Gospels. Late in the tenth century an unknown translator produced the **West Saxon Gospels**, the first to read as continuous English. Its Lord's Prayer begins *Fæder ure þu þe eart on heofonum, si þin nama gehalgod*: "Father our, you who are in heavens, be your name hallowed."

The monk **Ælfric**, who translated part of Genesis, worried in his preface that simple readers would copy the patriarchs' several wives without grasping the spiritual sense. Translators still weigh that risk (see [Word for word or meaning for meaning?](/articles/word-for-word-or-meaning-for-meaning/)). After 1066, French and Latin dominated, and a complete English Bible waited for [the Wycliffites](/articles/the-wycliffe-bible/).

The Anglo-Saxons worked from Latin, in forms that ranged from glosses to free verse.
