Hundreds of millions of people write Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Serbian and other languages in the **Cyrillic** alphabet. It is named after a ninth-century Byzantine scholar, **Constantine**, better known by the monastic name he took just before his death: **Cyril**. Yet Cyril almost certainly did not invent Cyrillic. He invented a different alphabet, **Glagolitic**, so that the Slavs could hear the Bible in their own language.

## Two brothers and a request

Constantine (about 827–869) and his brother **Methodius** (about 815–885) came from **Thessalonica**, a Byzantine city with many Slavic settlers around it, so they probably grew up hearing Slavic spoken. Constantine, known as "the Philosopher," was a brilliant scholar in Constantinople. Most of what we know comes from two *Lives* written by their disciples soon after their deaths, to honour them as saints.

Around 862, **Rastislav**, ruler of **Great Moravia** (roughly today's Czech Republic and Slovakia), asked the Byzantine emperor for a teacher who could explain the faith to his people in their own language. Frankish missionaries were already there, using Latin, and Rastislav also wanted a counterweight to Frankish power. The emperor sent the two brothers. According to the *Life of Constantine*, Constantine first composed letters for the Slavic language and began translating with the opening of John's Gospel: "In the beginning was the Word." Scholars think the brothers began with the Gospel readings used in church services rather than a continuous Bible.

## An alphabet made for scripture

**Glagolitic** is a carefully designed script, with letters for Slavic sounds that Greek could not represent. Its language, now called **Old Church Slavonic**, was based on the dialect around Thessalonica but was understood widely, since the Slavic languages were still close. It became the first literary language of the Slavs. Other peoples had received alphabets made for scripture before (see [Alphabets invented for the Bible](/articles/alphabets-for-the-bible/)).

## "Three languages" and Rome

The mission reached Moravia in 863 and soon met opposition from Frankish clergy. Some held what the *Life* calls the "three-language" view: that God should be praised only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, the languages of the inscription on the cross. Constantine argued against it, citing Paul's words about speaking with understanding (1 Corinthians 14).

Around 867 the brothers went to **Rome**, where Pope **Adrian II** approved the Slavonic books. Constantine fell ill there, became a monk under the name **Cyril**, and died in February 869. Methodius returned north as archbishop, but Bavarian bishops tried him and held him prisoner for more than two years until Pope **John VIII** secured his release.

## The disciples and Cyrillic

When Methodius died in 885, his opponents prevailed, and his disciples were expelled from Moravia. Several, including **Clement** and **Naum**, found refuge in **Bulgaria**, whose ruler Boris I wanted a church that did not depend on Greek clergy. There, probably around 900, a new alphabet was developed from Greek capital letters, with extra letters for Slavic sounds. Easier for anyone who knew Greek, it gradually replaced Glagolitic and came to be called **Cyrillic** in honour of Cyril. From Bulgaria, Slavonic scripture spread to the Serbs and, after 988, to Kyiv and the Eastern Slavs.

For centuries, Slavs had scripture mainly in service books. The first complete Slavonic Bible, compiled in Novgorod under Archbishop **Gennadius** in 1499, had to fill gaps by translating some books from the Latin Vulgate. The first printed one was the **Ostrog Bible** of 1581.

## Why it matters

Cyril and Methodius are honoured as saints by Orthodox and Catholic Christians alike. On one point their position is clear: they rejected the idea that only a few sacred languages are fit for scripture.

Open Word Bible stands in that tradition in a modest way. It translates into Spanish, French and Swahili as well as English, each from the Hebrew and Greek, so that no language receives a copy of another translation, as Gennadius's team had to accept.
