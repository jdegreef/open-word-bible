Every translator faces the same choice many times on every page. Should I follow the words and structure of the original as closely as my language allows, so that readers can see what the author actually wrote? Or should I say what the author meant in the way a native speaker of my language would say it, so that readers understand without effort?

Both answers have strong arguments behind them, and the debate between them is more than two thousand years old. This article traces it, from ancient Rome to modern translation theory, and explains how Open Word Bible approaches it.

## An example

Take 1 Peter 1:13. A word-for-word rendering of the Greek begins, "having girded up the loins of your mind." The King James Version kept the picture: "gird up the loins of your mind." The image is of a man tucking his long robe into his belt so that he can work or run. To an ancient reader it was vivid. To most modern readers it is puzzling. The World English Bible gives the sense instead:

> Therefore prepare your minds for action, be sober, and set your hope fully on the grace that will be brought to you at the revelation of Jesus Christ— (1 Peter 1:13)

The first approach keeps the image and asks the reader to understand it. The second gives the meaning and loses the image. Neither is simply right.

## Cicero, Horace and Jerome

The question was already familiar in ancient Rome, where educated people translated Greek literature into Latin. Around 46 BCE the orator **Cicero** wrote that, in translating two Greek speeches, he had worked "as an orator," not as a mere interpreter, keeping the force and style of the words rather than counting them out one by one (*On the Best Kind of Orator* 14). The poet **Horace**, in his *Art of Poetry*, advised writers not to render word for word like a slavish "faithful interpreter."

Four centuries later, **Jerome**, the translator of the Latin Vulgate (see [Jerome and the Latin Vulgate](/articles/jerome-and-the-vulgate/)), quoted both. In his *Letter* 57, defending a translation he had made, he declared that he rendered "not word for word, but sense for sense" (*non verbum e verbo, sed sensum exprimere de sensu*). But he made one exception: Holy Scripture, he said, "where even the order of the words is a mystery." So the most famous early statement of meaning-for-meaning translation also contains the most famous argument for word-for-word translation of the Bible. In practice, scholars note, Jerome's own Bible translation was often freer than that exception suggests.

## Luther and Tyndale

At the Reformation, the question became urgent, because translations were now meant for ordinary people. **Martin Luther**, in his *Open Letter on Translating* (1530), said that to learn how to speak German one must not ask the Latin letters but the mother in the house, the children in the street and the common man in the market, and watch how they speak. He defended his addition of "alone" in Romans 3:28 partly on the grounds that natural German required it, although his critics saw the addition as theological (see [Luther and the German Bible](/articles/luther-and-the-german-bible/)). Yet Luther also said, in a defence of his translation of the Psalms, that sometimes he had kept the original wording exactly, even against natural German, where much depended on it.

**William Tyndale** wanted a ploughboy to understand the Bible, and his New Testament was written in plain, strong English (see [William Tyndale and the English New Testament](/articles/william-tyndale/)). The translators of the King James Version, in their preface "The Translators to the Reader," explained that they had deliberately not tied themselves "to an uniformity of phrasing, or to an identity of words," using different English words for the same Greek or Hebrew word where the sense allowed. Many later translators, including the revisers of 1881, thought this a mistake; the debate over consistency of terms continues today.

## Schleiermacher's two paths

In 1813 the German theologian **Friedrich Schleiermacher** gave a lecture in Berlin on the different methods of translating. He argued that there are really only two. Either the translator leaves the author in peace as much as possible and moves the reader towards the author, or leaves the reader in peace and moves the author towards the reader. Schleiermacher preferred the first: a translation should let readers feel that they are reading something foreign.

His distinction has been revived in modern theory, for example by **Lawrence Venuti**, who calls the two paths "foreignising" and "domesticating" and argues that domesticating translation hides the strangeness of other cultures.

## Eugene Nida and dynamic equivalence

The most influential modern statement of the meaning-based approach came from **Eugene Nida**, a linguist with the American Bible Society (see [Translating for every language today](/articles/translating-for-every-language/)). In *Toward a Science of Translating* (1964) he distinguished **formal equivalence**, which focuses on the form and content of the message itself, from **dynamic equivalence**, which aims at the closest natural equivalent, so that readers today respond to the message in substantially the same way as the original readers did. In *The Theory and Practice of Translation* (1969), written with Charles Taber, he argued that a translation should be judged by whether ordinary readers understand it correctly.

Nida's ideas shaped hundreds of translations in languages around the world, and in English the Good News Bible. Because "dynamic" was often misunderstood as permission to paraphrase, he and Jan de Waard later preferred the term **functional equivalence** (*From One Language to Another*, 1986).

## Purposes and contexts

Later theorists widened the question. **Skopos theory**, developed by Katharina Reiss and Hans Vermeer in the 1980s, argues that the right method depends on the purpose of the translation: a text for public reading, a study edition and a children's Bible may rightly differ. Christiane Nord added the principle of loyalty: whatever its purpose, a translation must not misrepresent the author's intentions.

**Ernst-August Gutt**, a translation consultant who applied relevance theory to translation, argued that meaning always depends on context, which readers of an ancient text do not share. A translation can either try to give the meaning in terms of the reader's own context, or try to preserve as much of the original as possible and expect readers to learn the context, perhaps with the help of notes. Gutt called the second "direct translation." On his account, the choice is not between accuracy and freedom but between two different ways of communicating.

## The critics

Meaning-based translation has had strong critics. **Leland Ryken**, a literary scholar, argued in *The Word of God in English* (2002) for what he called "essentially literal" translation. He contended that dynamic equivalence interprets for the reader, removes ambiguities that the original leaves open, flattens literary features such as metaphor and repetition, and makes it impossible to tell where the text ends and the translator begins.

Defenders of meaning-based translation, such as Gordon Fee and Mark Strauss, replied that every translation interprets, that literal translations can mislead by using English words in ways no English speaker would understand, and that a reader who misunderstands an accurate-looking sentence has not received the original. Each side has a point. Much depends on the reader and the purpose, as skopos theory says.

## One meaning, three levels

Open Word Bible does not try to settle this argument by choosing a side. It gives every sentence at three levels: **Literal**, which follows the original's wording and order wherever English allows; **Balanced**, which reads as ordinary modern English while staying close to the original; and **Readable**, which uses plain language and explains idioms (see [Literal, Balanced, Readable: three levels, one meaning](/articles/three-levels-one-meaning/)). Here is 1 Peter 1:13 in the current drafts:

> {{OWB:1PE.1.13.s1:B}}

> {{OWB:1PE.1.13.s1:R}}

What holds the levels together is the rule that they never differ in meaning. Decisions about what the Greek or Hebrew means are made once, recorded with their alternatives and reasons, and followed by every level. Only wording changes. That meets one of Ryken's chief concerns: a reader can always see where interpretation has happened, because each decision is listed in the sentence's reasoning record (see [Showing our work: what a reasoning record is](/articles/showing-the-work/)). And it meets Nida's: a reader who needs plain language can have it without a different meaning being smuggled in.

## Inside one sentence

The reasoning record for 1 Peter 1:13 shows how the work is divided. The picture of girding the loins gets one **meaning decision**: to gird up one's loins was to tuck a long robe into the belt so as to work, run or travel. Israel ate the first Passover dressed that way, ready to leave Egypt (Exodus 12:11), and Peter applies the picture to the mind. That decision says what the phrase means, for every level and every language.

A separate **English wording decision** then says how each level shows it. Literal keeps the image, because a reader studying the text wants to see it. Balanced says "brace your minds for action" and Readable "get your minds ready to act," because readers today do not wear robes tied with a belt. The Literal reader is not left with a puzzle, since the meaning decision sits beside the sentence. The Readable reader is not given a guess, since the plain wording comes from the same decision.

The same verse holds a harder problem. The Greek adverb {{GREEK:1PE.1.13:9}} (*teleiōs*, "completely") stands between "being sober" and "hope," and could belong to either: "be completely sober" or "hope completely." Greek can leave it where it is. English has to put it somewhere, and so does every translation, literal or free. The King James Version attached it to hope ("hope to the end"), as does the World English Bible ("set your hope fully"). Open Word Bible does too, but its record marks the decision as uncertain, says the evidence is close to even, and gives the other reading in a footnote. This is the honest answer to Ryken's worry about lost ambiguity. Where a language forces a choice, the translator makes it, and then tells the reader that a choice was made.

Wording problems belong to each language. In Spanish the natural verb for "hope," *esperar*, also means "wait," and a plural command would most often be heard as "wait!" So the Spanish drafts say it with the noun *esperanza*, the key term, at every level. The meaning decision did not change. Only the Spanish words did.

## Long of anger

The Hebrew Bible is full of pictures like this. When God passes before Moses in Exodus 34:6, he is called {{HEBREW:EXO.34.6:11-12}}, literally "long of nostrils." Hebrew pictured anger as a nose that burns hot, so a "long" nose is slow to flare. The King James Version said "longsuffering," a word that keeps the length but drops the nose. The World English Bible says "slow to anger." Open Word Bible's Literal level keeps "long of anger," and Balanced reads:

> {{OWB:EXO.34.6.s1:B}}

Here the levels show their purpose plainly. A reader who meets "long of anger" learns how Hebrew thinks about anger and can trace the phrase through Proverbs 14:29 and 16:32. A reader who meets "slow to grow angry" understands at once. Both are reading the same decision: patient, slow to punish. And both can follow the record's note that these words became Israel's confession of who God is, echoed in Numbers 14:18, Psalm 103:8, Joel 2:13 and Jonah 4:2.

## An objection

A thoughtful skeptic might say that three levels are simply three translations with a promise attached. The promise matters, though, because it can be checked. Every level is drafted against the same written list of meaning decisions, and anyone who finds a level saying something the decisions do not say has found an error, not a difference of style. That is harder to do across three separate translations, where each team's decisions are mostly unwritten.

The approach has limits. Three levels cannot capture every possible purpose, and deciding what counts as meaning and what counts as wording is itself a judgement that reviewers may dispute. The drafts are AI-assisted and none has yet been reviewed. But the project's hope is that, by showing its reasoning, it lets readers take part in the oldest argument in translation rather than simply receive its results.
