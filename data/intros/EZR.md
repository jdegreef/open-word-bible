## What the book is

Ezra begins where 2 Chronicles ends. In 539 BC Cyrus of Persia conquered Babylon, and in his first year he let the exiles go home:

> {{OWB:EZR.1.1.s1:B}}

The first half of the book (1–6) tells of the first return, under Sheshbazzar and then Zerubbabel and the priest Joshua, with the list of the families who came back. They build the altar and lay the foundation of the temple, and the old men who remember Solomon's temple weep aloud while others shout for joy. The neighbouring peoples oppose the work and write to the Persian kings, and it stops, until the prophets Haggai and Zechariah stir the people up, King Darius confirms Cyrus's decree, and the temple is finished in 515 BC.

The second half (7–10) moves on some sixty years. Ezra, a priest and a scribe, comes from Babylon with a letter from King Artaxerxes and a second company of exiles, for he "had set his heart to study the law of the LORD, to practice it, and to teach its statutes and rulings in Israel". When he learns that many of the people, priests and leaders among them, have married women of the surrounding peoples, he tears his clothes and prays a long confession, and the people make a covenant to send those wives away.

## Who wrote it, and when

Ezra and Nehemiah were one book in the Hebrew Bible, and many scholars think the same writers who composed 1 and 2 Chronicles shaped it, since the end of Chronicles and the start of Ezra repeat each other; others see Ezra–Nehemiah as a separate work. Parts of Ezra are in Ezra's own voice (7:27–9:15), and the book quotes lists and official letters. It was probably put together in the fourth century BC. Ezra came to Jerusalem in "the seventh year of King Artaxerxes" (7:7), most likely Artaxerxes I, which would be 458 BC; some date his coming to the reign of Artaxerxes II, in 398 BC, after Nehemiah.

## How the book is laid out

- **The return under Cyrus** (1–2): the decree, the temple vessels, and the list of those who came back.
- **The altar and the foundation** (3).
- **Opposition** (4): letters to the Persian kings, and the work stopped.
- **The temple finished** (5–6): Haggai and Zechariah, Darius's decree, and the Passover.
- **Ezra comes to Jerusalem** (7–8): Artaxerxes' letter, and the journey.
- **Ezra's prayer and the mixed marriages** (9–10).

## How Open Word Bible translates Ezra

**Two languages.** From 4:8 to 6:18, and again in 7:12–26, the book is written in Aramaic, the language of the Persian empire's letters and records, which the Jews of the exile also spoke. These are the official letters and the story around them. Notes mark where the language changes; the translation reads straight through.

**The kings.** The Persian kings are named in their Hebrew forms: Ahasuerus is the king known in Greek as Xerxes (4:6), and Artaxerxes, named for his support of the temple in 6:14, reigned later than Darius. Decisions give the dates. Chapter 4 gathers opposition from different reigns, and 4:24 returns to the time of verse 5; the decision is marked uncertain.

**Numbers and lists (1–2, 8, 10).** The totals in the lists sometimes differ from the sums of their parts, as in 1:11 and 2:64, and some names in the lists of chapters 8 and 10 seem to have dropped out or been run together. The draft translates the text as it stands, marks those decisions uncertain and footnotes the readings of the parallel lists in Nehemiah 7 and in 1 Esdras, a Greek retelling of these events.

**The counsel of the Lord (10:3).** The vowels of the Hebrew read "the Lord", meaning God; the same letters can be read "my lord", addressing Ezra. The draft follows the vowels, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other.

**Wives and children sent away (10).** The draft translates the story as it stands. The decisions explain the words, and note where the Hebrew is hard, as in the last verse of the book.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. In the Aramaic chapters God is "the God of heaven". See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
