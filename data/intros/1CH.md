## What the book is

1 Chronicles begins with a name, "Adam", and for nine chapters it is almost all names: the line from Adam to Abraham and Israel, and then the tribes of Israel, with Judah and David's family first and Levi at the centre. Here and there the lists pause for a story, as when Jabez prays and God gives him what he asked. The lists end with the people who came back to live in Jerusalem after the exile, which shows who the book was written for: a people rebuilding their life around the temple, asking who they were and whether God's promises still stood.

The rest of the book is the reign of David. Saul's death is told in a single chapter, with its reason: he "did not keep the word of the LORD". Then David is made king by all Israel, takes Jerusalem, and brings the ark of God there with music and joy. The heart of the book is God's promise to him through the prophet Nathan:

David is not allowed to build the temple himself, because he has been a man of war, but he gathers the materials, organizes the priests, Levites, singers and gatekeepers, and hands everything over to Solomon. The book ends with David's prayer of praise, "everything in heaven and on earth is yours", and his death.

## Who wrote it, and when

1 and 2 Chronicles were one book in the Hebrew Bible, and in Jewish Bibles it stands last of all. Its writer, often called the Chronicler, drew on Genesis, Samuel and Kings, which he follows closely in many places, and on other sources he names, among them the records of Samuel, Nathan and Gad (29:29). The lists run several generations after the return from exile (3:19–24), so most scholars date the book to the fourth century BC. Jewish tradition credited it to Ezra, and the end of 2 Chronicles is repeated at the start of Ezra.

The Chronicler tells the story to make its meaning plain. He leaves out much that Samuel tells of David's failings, among them Bathsheba and Absalom's revolt, and puts in much about the temple and its worship. The Greek translation called the book "the things left out", as if it only filled gaps in Kings; its Hebrew name means "the events of the days".

## How the book is laid out

- **From Adam to the tribes of Israel** (1–9): the family lists, ending with those who lived in Jerusalem.
- **Saul's death** (10).
- **David becomes king and brings the ark to Jerusalem** (11–16): his mighty men, and his psalm of thanks.
- **God's promise to David** (17).
- **David's wars, and the census** (18–21).
- **Preparing for the temple** (22–29): the materials, the priests and Levites, the singers and gatekeepers, and David's last words.

## How Open Word Bible translates 1 Chronicles

**Comparing with Samuel and Kings.** Where Chronicles retells Samuel or Kings, the decisions note where its words differ, as names, numbers or whole phrases. Often the difference is the Chronicler's own emphasis; sometimes it is a copying slip in one book or the other. The decisions say which is more likely, and mark the matter uncertain when it is not clear.

**Satan (21:1).** Where 2 Samuel 24:1 says that the LORD's anger flared up and "he stirred David up" to count the people, Chronicles says that "Satan rose up against Israel and stirred David up". The Hebrew word has no article here, so it may be a name, Satan, or mean simply "an adversary", as it does in 1 Kings 11:14. The draft reads it as the name, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other. A decision explains how the two accounts look at one event from two sides.

**Names in the lists.** The same person often appears under two names, such as Bathshua for Bathsheba (3:5) or Azariah for Uzziah (3:12). Decisions note where the lists differ from other books, and the draft keeps the form each book gives.

**Verse numbers.** The verses are numbered as in the Hebrew text. Hebrew 5:27–41, the line of the high priests, is 6:1–15 in most English Bibles, so the rest of chapter 6 is fifteen verses lower here; and the Hebrew divides 12:4 in two. Notes explain.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
