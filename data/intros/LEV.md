## What the book is

Exodus ends with the glory of the LORD filling the newly built tent of meeting, so that even Moses cannot go in. Leviticus begins with God calling to Moses from that tent, and the whole book answers the question it raises: how can a holy God live among an unholy people?

It begins with sacrifices: the burnt offering, the grain offering, the peace offering, the sin offering and the guilt offering, and how each is to be made. It tells how Aaron and his sons were ordained as priests, and how two of them died when they offered fire God had not commanded. It sets out what is clean and unclean: food, childbirth, skin disease, mould in a house, and bodily discharges. At its centre is the Day of Atonement, the one day each year when the high priest entered the inner room of the tent and the sins of the people were placed on a goat sent away into the wilderness:

> {{OWB:LEV.16.30.s1:B}}

The second half of the book, often called the Holiness Code, turns to the life of the whole people: sexual relations, justice, the festivals, the sabbath year and the Jubilee, when land returns to its families and slaves go free. Here is the command Jesus called the second greatest:

> {{OWB:LEV.19.18.s1:B}}

and, a few verses later, the same love commanded for the foreigner. The book closes with blessings for obedience and warnings for disobedience, and the promise that even in exile God will remember his covenant.

## Who wrote it, and when

Leviticus is the third of the five books of the Law. Almost all of it is presented as words God spoke to Moses, and Jewish and Christian tradition has held that Moses wrote it. Many scholars think instead that it gathers priestly teaching and practice from many generations, reaching its final form during or after the exile. The draft takes no side on these questions; they do not change how a sentence is translated.

## How the book is laid out

- **The offerings** (1–7): burnt, grain, peace, sin and guilt offerings.
- **The priests ordained** (8–10): Aaron and his sons, and the fire of Nadab and Abihu.
- **Clean and unclean** (11–15): food, childbirth, skin disease and discharges.
- **The Day of Atonement** (16).
- **Holy living** (17–22): the blood, sexual relations, love of neighbour, and the holiness of the priests.
- **The holy times** (23–25): the festivals, the sabbath year and the Jubilee.
- **Blessings and warnings** (26).
- **Vows and dedications** (27).

## How Open Word Bible translates Leviticus

**Sin offering and atonement.** The Hebrew word for the sin offering is the same as the word for "sin". A decision at 4:3 explains that the offering deals not only with sin but with impurity, as after childbirth, and that some translations call it the "purification offering". "Make atonement" translates a verb that a decision at 1:4 explains as putting right what stands between the worshiper and God, and the draft uses one wording for it through the book.

**Azazel (16:8).** One goat is chosen by lot "for the LORD" and one "for Azazel". The word is found only in this chapter, and its meaning is unknown. It reads most naturally as a name, set opposite God's, perhaps of a being thought to live in the wilderness. Older Bibles read "the scapegoat". The draft keeps the name, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other readings.

**Life in the blood (17:11).** "The blood makes atonement by means of the life that is in it." The small Hebrew word can also mean "in exchange for", the blood as a life given for a life. The draft takes the first, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the second.

**Serious skin disease (13–14).** The word older Bibles translate "leprosy" names skin conditions with visible signs, and is used of growths in cloth, leather and the walls of a house; leprosy as known today does not fit the signs. The draft says "serious skin disease", a decision explains, and the mark in a house is "mold".

**Animals and birds (11).** Many of the creatures in the food laws cannot be identified for certain. The draft gives the probable identifications, marks the decisions uncertain and notes other suggestions.

**Strange fire (10:1).** Nadab and Abihu offered "an improper fire, one he had not commanded them to bring". What exactly was wrong with it the text does not say; the decision gives the readings and is marked uncertain.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
