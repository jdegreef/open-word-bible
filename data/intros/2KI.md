## What the book is

2 Kings begins with the end of Elijah's life, carried up to heaven in a whirlwind:

> {{OWB:2KI.2.11.s1:B}}

Elisha takes up his cloak and his work, and the first chapters are full of his deeds: oil that does not run out, a dead boy raised, poisoned stew made good, an axe head that floats, and Naaman, the commander of the Aramean army, healed of his skin disease by washing seven times in the Jordan. Then Jehu, anointed at Elisha's word, wipes out the house of Ahab and the worship of Baal in Israel, and the boy Joash is hidden in the temple and made king of Judah.

The rest of the book follows the two kingdoms, reign by reign. The northern kingdom falls to Assyria in 722 BC, and the writer stops to explain why: Israel sinned against the LORD who had brought them out of Egypt, worshipped other gods and would not listen to the prophets. Judah survives a century and a half longer. Hezekiah trusts the LORD, and Jerusalem is delivered from the Assyrian army; Manasseh fills the city with idols; Josiah finds the book of the law in the temple and leads the greatest reform of all:

> {{OWB:2KI.23.25.s1:B}}

But it is too late. Babylon besieges Jerusalem, burns the temple and carries the people into exile. The book ends with a small sign of hope: in Babylon, the captive king Jehoiachin is released from prison and given a place at the king's table.

## Who wrote it, and when

1 and 2 Kings were one book in the Hebrew Bible, divided later, and the break falls in the middle of the reign of Ahaziah of Israel. They name no author, and they cite older sources, the chronicles of the kings of Israel and of Judah. The last event is Jehoiachin's release, around 561 BC, so the history was completed during the exile. Many scholars see it as the last part of a longer history running from Deuteronomy through Kings, which judges each king by Deuteronomy's standard. Jewish tradition credited Kings to Jeremiah.

## How the book is laid out

- **Elijah taken up, and Elisha** (1–8): the chariot of fire, Elisha's miracles, Naaman, and the siege of Samaria.
- **Jehu and Joash** (9–12): the end of Ahab's house, Athaliah, and the temple repaired.
- **The two kingdoms to the fall of Samaria** (13–17): why Israel went into exile.
- **Hezekiah** (18–20): Sennacherib's siege, Isaiah, and Hezekiah's illness.
- **Manasseh to Josiah** (21–23): the book of the law found, and Josiah's reform.
- **The fall of Jerusalem** (24–25): the last kings, the exile, and Jehoiachin released.

## How Open Word Bible translates 2 Kings

**Dates that do not match.** The book dates each reign by the reign of the king in the other kingdom, and the figures do not always agree, as between 1:17 and 3:1, or 8:25 and 9:29. Decisions explain that they probably come from different ways of counting a king's years and from times when a father and son reigned together, and they are marked uncertain.

**Young lads (2:23).** The boys who mock Elisha are "young lads", in Hebrew "small lads", a phrase that can mean children or young men. The decision explains the range of the word and is marked uncertain.

**Naaman's request (5:18).** Naaman asks pardon for bowing in the temple of Rimmon when he must attend his king. The draft reads this as a request for what he will have to do in the future, not for what he has done, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other.

**The book of the law (22:8).** The book found in the temple is not named further, but what it leads Josiah to do matches Deuteronomy closely, and it is usually identified with Deuteronomy or its core. The decision says so and is marked uncertain.

**Written and read.** In several places the Hebrew text as written differs from the form the scribes marked to be read aloud. At 8:10, Elisha tells Hazael either "Go and tell him, 'You will certainly recover'", or, as the letters are written, "Go and say, 'You will certainly not recover'". The draft follows the scribes' reading, as is usual, marks such decisions uncertain and footnotes the written form.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
