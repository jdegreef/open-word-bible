## What the book is

In 586 BC the Babylonian army broke into Jerusalem after a long siege, burned the temple, tore down the walls and carried the people away. Lamentations is the cry that rose from the ruins. It begins:

> {{OWB:LAM.1.1.s1:B}}

The five poems do not soften what happened. Children faint in the streets from hunger; mothers do worse; priests and elders die looking for food; the enemy laughs. Jerusalem herself speaks, asking those who pass by whether any pain is like her pain. The poets say plainly that the city sinned and that the LORD has done what he planned, and they also cry out to him to look and see.

At the centre of the book, in the third poem, one man who has lived through affliction turns from despair to hope:

> {{OWB:LAM.3.23.s1:B}}

But the book does not end in triumph. Its last poem is a prayer of the whole people, and its last words leave the question open: turn us back to yourself, "unless you have rejected us completely".

## Who wrote it, and when

The book names no author. The Greek translation and Jewish tradition credit it to Jeremiah, who saw the fall of the city and was known for his laments (2 Chronicles 35:25), and in many Bibles it follows the book of Jeremiah. Most scholars think instead that the poems were written by one or more unnamed poets who lived through the disaster, soon after 586 BC, and that they were sung at the fasts held to mourn the fall of Jerusalem (Zechariah 7:3–5). In the Hebrew Bible it stands among the Writings, and Jews still read it on the ninth of Av, the fast that remembers the destruction of the temple.

## How the book is laid out

- **The city sits alone** (1): Jerusalem a widow, with no one to comfort her.
- **The Lord's anger** (2): he has destroyed without pity.
- **The man who has lived through affliction** (3): despair, and hope in the LORD's loyal love.
- **Gold grown dim** (4): the horrors of the siege.
- **Remember, LORD** (5): the people's prayer.

## How Open Word Bible translates Lamentations

**Alphabet poems.** The first four poems are acrostics: each verse, or in chapter 3 each of three verses in turn, begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, twenty-two in all. The fifth has twenty-two lines but no alphabet. In chapters 2, 3 and 4 two letters come in the opposite order to the usual one. The pattern cannot be kept in translation. See [Hebrew poetry and parallelism](/articles/hebrew-poetry-and-parallelism/).

**The man (3:1).** "I am the man who has lived through affliction": the speaker is never named. He may be Jeremiah, the city spoken of as a man, or one sufferer who speaks for all. The draft does not name him, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the readings.

**Not wiped out (3:22).** The standard Hebrew text says "we have not been wiped out"; with a small change, other manuscripts and the ancient translations read "the LORD's loyal love has not ceased". The draft follows the Hebrew, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other. On "loyal love", see [Hesed: steadfast love](/articles/hesed-steadfast-love/).

**The last verse (5:22).** The book's last line can be read "unless you have rejected us completely", "but instead you have rejected us", or as a question. The draft reads the first, marks the decision uncertain, and footnotes the others. A footnote also notes that when the book is read aloud in the synagogue, verse 21 is repeated after it, so as not to end on rejection.

**Disaster and good (3:38).** "Do not disaster and good alike come out of the mouth of the Most High?" A decision explains that the Hebrew word for "disaster" is the common word for "evil", here meaning calamity, not moral wrong.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
