## What the book is

Titus is one of the three Pastoral Letters, with 1 and 2 Timothy, written to Paul's co-workers about leading churches. Paul has left Titus in Crete to put in order what was left unfinished and to appoint elders in every town. The churches there are troubled by rebellious people, "full of empty talk and deception", who are ruining whole households. Paul tells Titus what kind of people the elders must be, how to teach older and younger men and women and slaves, and how believers are to live among their neighbours.

Again and again the letter says that right teaching shows itself in good works, and twice it gives the reason in a short summary of the gospel:

## Who wrote it, and when

The letter is from Paul (1:1). Titus was a Gentile believer who had worked with Paul for years: he went with him to Jerusalem (Galatians 2:1–3) and carried his letters and messages to Corinth (2 Corinthians 7–8). As with 1 and 2 Timothy, the church accepted the letter as Paul's from the second century, and many scholars today think instead that a follower wrote it in Paul's name after his death, around AD 80–100. Others hold that Paul wrote it late in his life, after a release from his first imprisonment in Rome. Acts does not mention a mission to Crete, which fits either view.

## How the book is laid out

- **Greeting** (1:1–4).
- **Elders for Crete** (1:5–9): blameless, hospitable, holding firmly to the trustworthy message.
- **False teachers** (1:10–16): they claim to know God, but deny him by what they do.
- **Teaching for the household of faith** (2:1–10): older men and women, younger men, Titus himself, and slaves.
- **The grace of God has appeared** (2:11–15).
- **Saved by his mercy** (3:1–8): ready for every good work, because God saved us not for what we had done.
- **Final instructions and greetings** (3:9–15).

## How Open Word Bible translates Titus

**Our great God and Savior (2:13).** Paul speaks of "the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ". With one article governing both titles, the phrase most naturally names one person, as in 2 Peter 1:1. Others read it as two persons, "the great God and our Savior Jesus Christ". The draft takes the first, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the second.

**The husband of one wife (1:6).** As in 1 Timothy 3:2, the Greek says "a one-woman man". It may mean faithful to one wife, not a polygamist, or married only once. Most now take it as faithfulness in marriage. The decision is marked uncertain and footnoted, and the draft keeps the wording open.

**Cretans are always liars (1:12).** Paul quotes "one of their own prophets", whom early writers identify as Epimenides of Crete, a poet of the sixth century BC honoured as a seer. A decision explains that Paul calls him a prophet as the Cretans did.

**The washing of new birth (3:5).** God "saved us through the washing of new birth and renewal by the Holy Spirit". One preposition governs both, so most take it as one washing that brings both new birth and the Spirit's renewal, and many see a reference to baptism. The draft follows that reading, marks it uncertain, and footnotes the reading of two separate acts.

**The household instructions (2:1–10).** Paul's teaching for women, slaves and others assumes the household order of his day and gives it a Christian reason: "so that no one will speak evil of the word of God". A decision says so, and a footnote notes that how such instructions apply in other times is debated. On the word "slave", see [Slave or servant?](/articles/slave-or-servant/).

**Doing good.** The letter returns to doing good again and again (1:16; 2:7, 14; 3:1, 8, 14). In 3:8 and 3:14 the verb can also mean to take up an honest trade, and the decisions there note that reading.
