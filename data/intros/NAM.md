## What the book is

Nahum is "an oracle about Nineveh". For more than a century Assyria had been the terror of the ancient world. It had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BC, laid waste the towns of Judah and besieged Jerusalem, and its kings boasted in their inscriptions of the cities they burned and the prisoners they flayed. Nahum announces that its capital will fall.

The book opens with a hymn to God as judge of his enemies, coming in the whirlwind and the storm. It is also a God who is patient and who protects his own:

> {{OWB:NAM.1.7.s1:B}}

Then the poem moves to the city itself, in some of the most vivid battle poetry in the Bible: chariots flashing in the streets, the river gates opened, the palace melting away, plunder carried off. It ends with a lament that is no lament, since everyone who hears the news claps their hands: "who has not felt your endless cruelty?"

## Who wrote it, and when

Nothing is known of Nahum beyond the book. His name means "comfort", and his town, Elkosh, has not been located for certain. He speaks of the fall of Thebes in Egypt, which Assyria sacked in 663 BC, as already past (3:8–10), and of the fall of Nineveh, which came in 612 BC at the hands of the Babylonians and Medes, as still to come. So he spoke at some time between those dates.

Nahum is often read beside Jonah, the other book about Nineveh. In Jonah, God spares the city when it repents; in Nahum, it has returned to its cruelty, and judgment comes. Both use the words God spoke to Moses: "slow to grow angry" (Nahum 1:3; Jonah 4:2; Exodus 34:6).

## How the book is laid out

- **The LORD, the avenging God** (1:1–11): a hymn, in part an alphabet poem.
- **Good news for Judah, doom for Nineveh** (1:12–2:1): the feet of one who brings good news.
- **The fall of Nineveh** (2:2–14): the attack, the river gates, and the lions' den.
- **Woe to the city of bloodshed** (3): like Thebes, Nineveh will fall, and its wound is fatal.

## How Open Word Bible translates Nahum

**Verse numbers.** The verses are numbered as in the Hebrew text. "Look! On the mountains, the feet of one who brings good news!" is 2:1 here and 1:15 in most English Bibles, so the rest of chapter 2 is one verse higher than in those Bibles. Notes at 2:1 and 2:2 explain.

**A broken alphabet poem (1:2–8).** Lines in these verses begin, more or less in order, with the letters of the Hebrew alphabet, from the first letter to the eleventh. The order is incomplete, and some doubt that it was intended. A decision describes it and marks it uncertain. An alphabet pattern cannot be kept in translation. See [Hebrew poetry and parallelism](/articles/hebrew-poetry-and-parallelism/).

**Hard words in the battle poem (1:10; 2:4, 8).** Several words in these verses are used only here, and some lines are hard to follow. Verse 1:10 is one of the hardest verses in the book; in 2:4 the word rendered "steel" is uncertain; and the first word of 2:8 may be a verb, "it is decreed", or the name or title of Nineveh's queen. In each place the draft gives the most likely sense, marks the decision uncertain and notes the others.

**Who is "he"? (2:6).** The verse does not say who calls up his officers. Because the "siege shelter" at the end is a device used by attackers, the draft takes him as the commander of the attack, not the king of Nineveh, and marks the decision uncertain.

**Vengeance.** Nahum speaks of God as "jealous and avenging" (1:2). The decisions explain that God's jealousy is his zeal for the loyalty that belongs to him alone, and that he avenges as a ruler and judge punishes wrong, set beside his patience (1:3) and his goodness (1:7).

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
