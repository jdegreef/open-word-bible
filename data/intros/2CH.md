## What the book is

2 Chronicles continues the history begun in 1 Chronicles. Its first part is Solomon's reign, and above all the building of the temple. When the ark is brought in and the singers praise God, "For he is good; his loyal love lasts forever", the house is filled with a cloud. Solomon prays at its dedication, knowing that "heaven itself, even the highest heaven, cannot contain you", and God answers him at night:

> {{OWB:2CH.7.14.s1:B}}

That promise sets the pattern for the rest of the book. Chronicles follows only the kings of Judah, from Rehoboam to Zedekiah, and mentions the northern kingdom only where it touches Judah. Kings who seek the LORD are given peace and victory; kings who forsake him meet disaster; and again and again a prophet comes to warn. Jehoshaphat sends singers ahead of his army. Hezekiah cleanses the temple and invites the north to keep the Passover. Even Manasseh, the worst of the kings, is taken captive to Babylon, humbles himself, and is brought back: "Then Manasseh knew that the LORD is God."

But the people mock God's messengers until there is no remedy. Jerusalem falls, the temple is burned, and the land lies desolate for seventy years. The book ends with the decree of Cyrus, and its last words are an open door: "let them go up".

## Who wrote it, and when

1 and 2 Chronicles were one book in the Hebrew Bible, and in Jewish Bibles it stands last of all, so that the Hebrew Scriptures end with the call to go up to Jerusalem. The writer, often called the Chronicler, follows Kings closely in many places and names other sources, many of them the records of prophets. Most scholars date the book to the fourth century BC. Its last two verses are repeated at the start of Ezra (Ezra 1:1–3).

## How the book is laid out

- **Solomon** (1–9): wisdom, the temple built and dedicated, and the visit of the queen of Sheba.
- **The kingdom divided** (10–12): Rehoboam and the revolt of the north.
- **The kings of Judah** (13–28): from Abijah to Ahaz, among them Asa, Jehoshaphat, Joash and Uzziah.
- **Hezekiah** (29–32): the temple cleansed, the Passover, and Jerusalem delivered from Assyria.
- **Manasseh to Josiah** (33–35): Manasseh's repentance, and Josiah's reform and death.
- **The fall of Jerusalem and the decree of Cyrus** (36).

## How Open Word Bible translates 2 Chronicles

**Comparing with Kings.** Where Chronicles retells Kings, the decisions note where its words differ. Some differences are numbers, as when the bronze Sea holds three thousand baths here and two thousand in 1 Kings 7:26, or the porch of the temple is 120 cubits high (3:4), four times the height of the house. The draft keeps the numbers as the Hebrew text gives them, marks the decisions uncertain and footnotes other readings. Measures are given in modern units, and a reader setting shows the cubits and baths of the Hebrew. See [Weights, measures and money](/articles/weights-measures-and-money/). Others are the Chronicler's own emphasis, as in his account of Manasseh's repentance, which Kings does not tell.

**If my people (7:14).** This verse is often quoted on its own. A decision explains that in its setting it is God's answer to Solomon at the dedication of the temple: "my people" are Israel, "their land" is the land God gave them, and healing it means ending the drought, locusts and plague of verse 13. It also notes how the promise has been applied more widely.

**Neco's warning (35:21–22).** Pharaoh Neco warns Josiah not to fight him, and the narrator says that his words came "from the mouth of God". Josiah disguises himself, like Ahab before him, and is killed. A decision explains.

**Seventy years and the sabbaths (36:21).** The land lay desolate "until the land had enjoyed its Sabbaths", words taken from Leviticus 26:34–35 and joined to Jeremiah's seventy years. A decision explains.

**Verse numbers.** The verses are numbered as in the Hebrew text, which ends chapter 1 a verse later and chapter 13 a verse later than most English Bibles, so chapters 2 and 14 are one verse lower here. Notes explain.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
