## What the book is

Most prophets speak to the people for God. Habakkuk speaks to God for himself, and the book begins with a complaint:

> {{OWB:HAB.1.2.s1:B}}

God's answer is worse than the question: he is raising up the Babylonians, a fierce and swift nation, to punish Judah. So Habakkuk complains again. How can a God whose eyes are too pure to look on evil use a people more wicked than those they punish? He climbs to his watchpost to wait for an answer, and it comes as a vision to be written down plainly on tablets. It will come at its appointed time, and in the meantime:

> {{OWB:HAB.2.4.s1:B}}

Five woes follow against the proud oppressor, with the promise that "the earth will be as full of knowing the glory of the LORD as the sea is full of water". The book ends with a psalm: a vision of God coming in power, as at the exodus, and the prophet's resolve to rejoice in God even if the fig tree does not flower and the fields yield nothing.

## Who wrote it, and when

Nothing is known of Habakkuk but his name and his title, "the prophet". He speaks of the Babylonians as rising to power, so most date the book shortly before or after their victory at Carchemish in 605 BC, in the last years before Jerusalem fell. The psalm of chapter 3 has its own title and musical notes, like the psalms of the Psalter, and was probably used in worship.

## How the book is laid out

- **First complaint: how long?** (1:1–4).
- **God's answer: the Babylonians** (1:5–11).
- **Second complaint: why the wicked?** (1:12–2:1).
- **The vision** (2:2–5): write it down; the righteous will live by their faithfulness.
- **Five woes against the oppressor** (2:6–20): ending "the LORD is in his holy temple".
- **Habakkuk's prayer** (3): God comes in power, and "yet I will rejoice in the LORD".

## How Open Word Bible translates Habakkuk

**By their faithfulness (2:4).** This line is quoted three times in the New Testament, in its Greek form, as "The one who is righteous will live by faith" (Romans 1:17; Galatians 3:11; Hebrews 10:38). The Hebrew word means firmness, steadiness and trustworthiness, the steady loyalty to God of someone who waits for the vision to come true, and the draft says "faithfulness". Whose faithfulness is meant is uncertain: the Greek translation reads "my faithfulness", God's own. The decision is marked uncertain, and the footnotes give the readings and the New Testament quotations. See [Faith in Christ](/articles/faith-in-christ/) and [How the New Testament quotes the Old](/articles/how-the-new-testament-quotes-the-old/).

**The Dead Sea Scrolls.** One of the scrolls found at Qumran is a commentary on Habakkuk 1–2, with its own copy of the text. In several places it reads differently from the Hebrew text the draft follows, as with "wealth" for "the wine" in 2:5. The draft follows its Hebrew text and footnotes the scroll. See [What the Dead Sea Scrolls show](/articles/what-the-dead-sea-scrolls-show/).

**We will not die (1:12).** The Hebrew says "we will not die". Jewish tradition counts this among the places where scribes changed "you will not die", said of God, out of reverence. The draft keeps the Hebrew, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the tradition.

**The psalm (3).** The prayer is set "on shigionoth", a musical term whose sense is unknown, and is marked with "Selah", like the psalms. See [Selah and the psalm headings](/articles/selah-and-the-psalm-headings/). Some of its lines, such as 3:9 and 3:14, are among the most obscure in the Old Testament; the draft gives the most likely sense and footnotes the others.

**The poetry.** Most of Habakkuk is written in Hebrew verse, and the draft sets it in lines. See [Hebrew poetry and parallelism](/articles/hebrew-poetry-and-parallelism/).

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
