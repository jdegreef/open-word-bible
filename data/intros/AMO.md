## What the book is

Amos is the earliest of the prophets whose words were gathered into a book of their own. He was not a prophet by trade but "one of the sheep breeders of Tekoa", a village in the hills of Judah, and God sent him north to Israel. It was a time of peace and prosperity under Jeroboam II. The rich built summer houses and winter houses, lay on beds inlaid with ivory and drank wine by the bowlful, while they sold the poor "for a pair of sandals" and turned justice into bitter wormwood. And they crowded the shrines at Bethel and Gilgal, sure that God was on their side. Amos told them that God hated it all:

The book opens with judgments on Israel's neighbours, "for three crimes ... even for four", which close in on Judah and then on Israel itself. Because Israel alone has been known by God, it will be punished. The day of the LORD, which the people long for, will be darkness, not light. Five visions follow, among them a plumb line and a basket of summer fruit, and the book ends with a promise that God will raise up "the fallen booth of David" and restore his people to their land.

## Who wrote it, and when

Amos spoke "during the reign of Uzziah king of Judah and during the reign of Jeroboam son of Joash king of Israel, two years before the earthquake" (1:1), around 760–750 BC. The earthquake was remembered for centuries (Zechariah 14:5). Amaziah, the priest of Bethel, told him to go back to Judah and prophesy there (7:10–17). Most of the book is taken to be Amos's own words, gathered by his followers; many scholars think some passages, among them the promise at the end, were added later, while others hold that they are his.

## How the book is laid out

- **Judgments on the nations, and on Israel** (1–2): "for three crimes ... even for four".
- **Hear this word** (3–6): Israel's privilege and punishment, the cows of Bashan, "seek me and live", and woe to those at ease.
- **Five visions** (7:1–9:10): locusts, fire, the plumb line, the summer fruit, and the Lord beside the altar; with Amos and Amaziah at Bethel.
- **The fallen booth of David restored** (9:11–15).

## How Open Word Bible translates Amos

**Wordplay (8:2; 5:5).** God shows Amos "a basket of summer fruit" and says, "The end has come upon my people Israel": in Hebrew the two words sound almost the same. And "Gilgal is sure to go into exile" repeats the sounds of the name. The footnotes explain what English cannot keep. See [Wordplay that cannot be translated](/articles/wordplay-that-cannot-be-translated/).

**A plumb line? (7:7–8).** The word is found only in these verses. It has long been understood as a plumb line, a weight hung on a cord to test whether a wall is straight, and the draft keeps that sense; others take it as the metal tin. The decision is marked uncertain and footnoted.

**Not a prophet (7:14).** Hebrew uses no verb here, so Amos's answer to Amaziah can be read "I am not a prophet" or "I was not a prophet", until the LORD called him. The draft reads the present, Amos denying that he belongs to a company of professional prophets, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other.

**The cows of Bashan (4:1).** The words are addressed to the wealthy women of Samaria, since the verbs are feminine. A decision says so, and is marked uncertain.

**The Greek of Amos in Acts (9:11–12; 5:25–27).** James quotes Amos 9:11–12 at the council in Jerusalem (Acts 15:16–17), and Stephen quotes Amos 5:25–27 (Acts 7:42–43), both following the Greek translation, which differs from the Hebrew. The draft follows the Hebrew, and the decisions and footnotes explain the Greek. See [How the New Testament quotes the Old](/articles/how-the-new-testament-quotes-the-old/).

**The poetry.** Most of Amos is written in Hebrew verse, and the draft sets it in lines. See [Hebrew poetry and parallelism](/articles/hebrew-poetry-and-parallelism/).

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
