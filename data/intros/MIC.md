## What the book is

Micah came from Moresheth, a small town in the low hills south-west of Jerusalem, and he spoke for the country people against the powerful of the capital. Landowners lie awake planning to seize fields and houses; rulers "tear the skin" off their people; prophets preach peace to whoever feeds them; and everyone says the LORD is among them, so no disaster can come. Micah answers that Jerusalem will become a heap of ruins.

Yet the book moves back and forth between judgment and hope. After the ruin comes a vision of the mountain of the LORD's house raised above the hills, with the nations streaming to it to learn his ways, and swords hammered into plow blades. The book ends with a psalm of trust in a God who forgives and "delights in loyal love".

## Who wrote it, and when

Micah spoke "during the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah over Judah" (1:1), between about 740 and 690 BC, the same years as Isaiah. He saw Samaria fall to Assyria in 722 BC and the Assyrian armies sweep through the towns of Judah in 701. A century later, when Jeremiah was on trial for foretelling the fall of the temple, the elders remembered that Micah had said the same in Hezekiah's day, and that Hezekiah had listened (Jeremiah 26:18–19). Many scholars think the promises of chapters 4–5 and 7 were added after the exile by those who treasured his words; others hold that they are his.

## How the book is laid out

- **Judgment on Samaria and Judah** (1–3): the lament over the towns of the lowlands, the land-grabbers, and the corrupt leaders.
- **The mountain of the LORD and the ruler from Bethlehem** (4–5): the nations come to learn, and one from Bethlehem shepherds his flock.
- **The LORD's case against his people** (6): what does the LORD ask of you?
- **Lament and trust** (7): "Who is a God like you?"

## How Open Word Bible translates Micah

**Bethlehem (5:1).** The verses are numbered as in the Hebrew text, where this verse is 5:1; in most English Bibles it is 5:2. The ruler's "origins are from of old, from ancient days": the word for "origins" means goings out or descent, and the draft reads it as a line that reaches back to long ago, to David. The King James Version reads "whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting", and many Christians read it of the eternal Son. The decision is marked uncertain and the footnote gives that reading. Matthew 2:6 quotes the verse in a different form, which a footnote explains.

**To love loyalty (6:8).** The Hebrew word is *ḥesed*, the faithful love of someone bound to another by a promise, which is also the word for God's "loyal love" in 7:18. See [Hesed: steadfast love](/articles/hesed-steadfast-love/).

**The towns of the lowlands (1:10–15).** Micah's lament plays on the names of the towns: Beth-leaphrah, "house of dust", will roll in the dust; the people of Shaphir, "beautiful", will go out naked and ashamed; Lachish sounds like the word for fast horses. Decisions explain each play on words, which English cannot keep. Some lines are hard, and those decisions are marked uncertain. See [Wordplay that cannot be translated](/articles/wordplay-that-cannot-be-translated/).

**Micah and Isaiah (4:1–3).** These verses appear almost word for word in Isaiah 2:2–4. A note says so; who first spoke them is not known.

**Who is speaking? (2:4–12).** In chapter 2 the voices change without warning, between the LORD, the prophet, his opponents and the people. The draft follows the most likely reading of who says what, marks those decisions uncertain and footnotes the others.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
