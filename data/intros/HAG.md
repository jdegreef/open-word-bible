## What the book is

Haggai is a short book of four dated messages, all given within four months of the year 520 BC. Almost twenty years earlier the first exiles had come back from Babylon and laid the foundation of a new temple, but the work had stopped. The people had built their own homes, and the house of God still lay in ruins. Haggai puts the question bluntly:

> {{OWB:HAG.1.4.s1:B}}

They sow much and harvest little, he says, because of the house that lies in ruins. And the governor Zerubbabel, the high priest Joshua and the people listen, and within weeks they are at work. When the older people, who remember Solomon's temple, see how little the new one looks, Haggai encourages them: "Be strong ... and get to work, for I am with you". God will shake the nations and fill this house with glory, and the book ends with a promise to Zerubbabel, of the line of David: God will "make you like a signet ring, for I have chosen you".

## Who wrote it, and when

Haggai is named as a prophet in Ezra 5:1 and 6:14, beside Zechariah, as one of those whose preaching led to the rebuilding of the temple, which was finished in 515 BC. The book speaks of him in the third person and gives each message a date by the reign of Darius I of Persia, so it was probably put together by someone close to him, soon after the events.

## How the book is laid out

- **Rebuild the house** (1:1–11): the people's neglect, and the drought.
- **The people obey** (1:12–15): "I am with you", and the work begins.
- **The latter glory** (2:1–9): be strong; the glory of this house will be greater.
- **Unclean, and blessed** (2:10–19): a question for the priests, and blessing from this day on.
- **Zerubbabel the signet ring** (2:20–23).

## How Open Word Bible translates Haggai

**The desire of all nations? (2:7).** Older English Bibles read "the desire of all nations shall come", and many have heard in it a promise of the Messiah. The word means what is desired or precious, and its verb is plural, "they will come", so it is usually read as the wealth of the nations, which verse 8 explains: "The silver belongs to me, and the gold belongs to me". The draft follows that reading, marks the decision uncertain, and footnotes the other.

**The latter glory (2:9).** The word "latter" can go with "glory" or with "house": "the latter glory of this house", or "the glory of this latter house". Haggai treats the temple as one house, which the old people saw in its former glory, so the draft takes the first. The decision is marked uncertain and footnoted.

**A sentence without a verb (2:5).** The verse begins with the sign of a direct object, "the word that I made with you when you came out of Egypt", but no verb comes before it. The draft reads it with "I am with you" in verse 4, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes other ways of filling the gap.

**Up to this day (2:15).** Haggai asks the people to think carefully about how things have been "up to this day", and then "from this day on" (2:18). The Hebrew phrase in 2:15 is the same as the one in 2:18 and can look forward or back. The draft reads it there as looking back to the time before the building began, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other.

**Dates.** Each message is dated by day, month and year of Darius. The last words of 1:15, "in the second year of King Darius", may close chapter 1 or open chapter 2, which otherwise has no year; the footnote explains.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. Haggai's favourite title is "the LORD of Armies". See [Names and titles of God](/articles/names-and-titles-of-god/).
