## What the book is

Obadiah is a single chapter of twenty-one verses, a vision about Edom. The Edomites, descended from Esau, were the brother people of Israel, descended from Jacob, and lived in the steep, red mountains south-east of the Dead Sea. They trusted in their heights:

> {{OWB:OBA.1.3.s1:B}}

But on the day strangers entered Jerusalem's gates, Edom stood at a distance and did nothing, and then joined in: it gloated over Judah's ruin, looted the city and handed over those who escaped. For that violence against its brother, Edom will be brought down. The day of the LORD is near for all the nations: "As you have done, so it will be done to you". The book ends with the survivors of Jacob back on Mount Zion and the whole land restored, and "the kingdom will belong to the LORD".

## Who wrote it, and when

Nothing is known of Obadiah beyond his name, which means "servant of the LORD" and was a common one. The book does not say when he spoke. Most scholars connect it with the fall of Jerusalem to Babylon in 586 BC, which Psalm 137:7, Lamentations 4:21–22 and Ezekiel 35 also remember as the day Edom turned on Judah, and date it soon after. Others link it with an earlier attack on Jerusalem. Verses 1–9 are very close to Jeremiah 49:7–22, and the two prophets may both draw on an older oracle against Edom.

## How the book is laid out

- **Edom brought down** (1–9): from its rock heights, by its own allies.
- **Edom's crime** (10–14): violence against its brother Jacob on the day of Jerusalem's disaster.
- **The day of the LORD** (15–16): as you have done, it will be done to you.
- **The restoration of Israel** (17–21): Mount Zion holy again, the land possessed, and the kingdom the LORD's.

## How Open Word Bible translates Obadiah

**The poetry.** Obadiah is written in Hebrew verse, and the draft sets it in lines. See [Hebrew poetry and parallelism](/articles/hebrew-poetry-and-parallelism/).

**The day strangers entered (11).** The verse does not name the enemy. The draft follows the usual view, that it was the Babylonian conquest of 586 BC, and marks the decision uncertain, since Jerusalem was attacked more than once.

**"You should not have" (12–14).** These verses are eight commands in the Hebrew, "do not gloat ... do not rejoice". Verse 11 has already spoken of the day as past, so the draft takes them as the prophet standing in that day and condemning each thing Edom did, and renders them "you should not have". The decision is marked uncertain and footnoted, since some read them as warnings for a disaster still to come.

**Who drank on the holy mountain? (16).** Until now Edom has been "you" in the singular; here the verb is plural. It may be Judah, who drank the cup of God's anger when Jerusalem fell, or the Edomites, who drank in celebration on the captured mountain. The decision is marked uncertain and the footnote gives both readings.

**Hard verses (7, 19–20).** The line about "those who share your bread" in verse 7 is difficult, and its word for "trap" is rare. Verse 20 is the hardest in the book: word for word it reads "and the exiles of this host of the sons of Israel which Canaanites as far as Zarephath". The draft gives the most likely sense in each place, marks the decisions uncertain and footnotes the other readings. Sepharad, the place of exile in verse 20, is not known for certain; it is often identified with Sardis in Asia Minor, and later Jewish tradition took it to mean Spain, which is why the Jews of Spain are called Sephardim.

**The Greek translation (9, 17, 21).** In three places the Greek translation reads the Hebrew differently: it joins "in the slaughter" to verse 10, reads "those who dispossessed them" in verse 17, and reads "those who have been delivered" for "deliverers" in verse 21. The draft follows the Hebrew text and footnotes the Greek. See [The Septuagint](/articles/the-septuagint/).

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
