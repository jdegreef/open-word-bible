## What the book is

Philemon is a single page, the shortest of Paul's letters and the most personal. Paul is in prison. With him is Onesimus, a slave who belongs to Philemon, a believer in whose house a church meets. Onesimus has come to faith through Paul, and Paul is sending him back. He could give orders, he says, but he would rather ask, and he asks Philemon to receive Onesimus as he would receive Paul himself:

> {{OWB:PHM.1.17.s1:B}}

The letter is full of warmth and of tact. Paul plays on Onesimus's name, which means "useful": "Once he was useless to you, but now he is useful to you and to me." He offers to pay whatever Onesimus owes, writing the promise in his own hand, and he does not fail to mention that Philemon owes Paul his own self. He ends by asking for a guest room, since he hopes to visit.

## Who wrote it, and when

The letter is from Paul and Timothy (verse 1), and almost all scholars agree that Paul wrote it. It is closely tied to Colossians: Onesimus and Archippus belong to Colossae, and the same companions, Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas and Luke, send greetings in both letters (Colossians 4:9–17), so the two were most likely written at the same time and carried together. Where Paul was held is not said. Most have thought of his imprisonment in Rome, around AD 60–62; others suggest an earlier imprisonment in Ephesus, which was much nearer to Colossae.

## How the book is laid out

- **Greeting** (1–3): to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus and the church in Philemon's house.
- **Thanks for Philemon's love and faith** (4–7): he has refreshed the hearts of God's people.
- **Paul's appeal for Onesimus** (8–16): once useless, now useful, "a dear brother".
- **Take him in as you would me** (17–22): put any debt on my account.
- **Final greetings** (23–25).

## How Open Word Bible translates Philemon

**Singular and plural.** The letter is written to Philemon, and "you" is singular almost throughout, but the greeting, the closing blessing and verse 22 ("your prayers") are plural: the whole church in his house hears it. Decisions mark each change.

**Why Onesimus was away.** The letter never says how Onesimus came to be with Paul. Many have thought he ran away, perhaps taking money with him; others think he went to Paul, his master's friend, to ask him to plead for him after a quarrel. Paul writes only "if he has wronged you at all, or owes you anything". The decision at verse 18 leaves the matter open and marks it uncertain. At verse 15 Paul says that Onesimus "was parted" from Philemon, a passive that may hint at God's purpose in what happened.

**Freedom?** Paul asks Philemon to have Onesimus back "no longer as a slave but as more than a slave" (verse 16), and says he knows Philemon "will do even more than I say" (verse 21). Whether he is asking for Onesimus's legal freedom is not said. The decisions at 15 and 21 say so, are marked uncertain, and the footnote at 15 gives the reading that Paul asks Philemon to free him. On the word "slave", see [Slave or servant?](/articles/slave-or-servant/).

**An old man, or an ambassador? (9).** Paul calls himself an old man. A word differing by one letter means "ambassador", and some read that here, since in Ephesians 6:20 Paul calls himself "an ambassador, though in a chain". No manuscript has it. The draft keeps "an old man", marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other reading.

**The sharing of your faith (6).** Paul prays that "the generosity that flows from your faith will be put to work". The Greek says "the sharing of your faith", which can mean Philemon's generosity to other believers, his telling others the faith, or his share in the faith all believers hold. The draft takes the first, since verse 7 praises his kindness to God's people and verse 17 asks for the same partnership toward Onesimus; it marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the others.

**The text (12, 25).** Some manuscripts add "receive him" in verse 12, from verse 17, and "Amen" at the end. The draft follows its Greek text, which has neither. See [When manuscripts disagree](/articles/when-manuscripts-disagree/).
