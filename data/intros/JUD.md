## What the book is

Jude meant to write about "the salvation we hold in common", but news reached him that changed his plan. Some people had slipped into the church unnoticed, turning God's grace into an excuse for shameless living and disowning Jesus Christ, "our only Master and Lord". So he writes instead to urge his readers to fight for the faith handed over to God's people once for all.

Most of the letter is a dense string of warnings, drawn from Scripture and from Jewish tradition: the people saved out of Egypt who did not believe, the angels who left their place, Sodom and Gomorrah, Cain, Balaam and Korah, and a prophecy of Enoch. The false teachers are "clouds that bring no rain", "wandering stars". Then Jude turns to his readers, telling them to build themselves up, to keep themselves in God's love, and to show mercy to those who waver, and he ends with one of the best-loved blessings in the New Testament:

## Who wrote it, and when

The letter is from "Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and a brother of James" (verse 1). Since early times this Jude, or Judas, has been identified as one of the brothers of Jesus named in Mark 6:3, and the James as the leader of the church in Jerusalem. If so, it was written in the second half of the first century, perhaps in the 50s or 60s. Some scholars think a later writer used his name, pointing to its good Greek and to its talk of "the faith" as a body of teaching handed down; others reply that neither requires a late date. Much of Jude appears again, rearranged, in 2 Peter 2, and most scholars think 2 Peter drew on Jude.

## How the book is laid out

- **Greeting** (1–2).
- **Fight for the faith** (3–4): ungodly people have slipped in among you.
- **Warnings from the past** (5–16): Israel in the wilderness, the angels, Sodom and Gomorrah, Michael and the devil, Cain, Balaam and Korah, and Enoch's prophecy.
- **Remember the apostles' words** (17–23): keep yourselves in God's love, and show mercy.
- **Doxology** (24–25): to the one who is able to guard you from stumbling.

## How Open Word Bible translates Jude

**Jesus saved a people out of Egypt (5).** The manuscripts differ here more than anywhere else in Jude: some read "Jesus", others "the Lord" or "God". The draft follows its Greek text, which reads "Jesus", with some of the most important early manuscripts, and so Jude speaks of Jesus Christ, his "only Master and Lord", as present in Israel's history, as Paul does in 1 Corinthians 10:4. The decision is marked uncertain, and the footnote gives the other readings. See [When manuscripts disagree](/articles/when-manuscripts-disagree/).

**Our only Master and Lord (4).** One article governs both titles, so the draft reads them as one person, Jesus Christ. Many later manuscripts add "God", so that the verse names two persons, and the King James Version follows them. The footnote explains.

**Books outside the Old Testament (9, 14–15).** Jude tells of the archangel Michael disputing with the devil over the body of Moses, a story not found in the Old Testament, which early Christian writers say came from a book called the Assumption of Moses. And his prophecy of Enoch is a close quotation of 1 Enoch 1:9, a Jewish writing not in the Hebrew Bible, though the Ethiopian Orthodox Church counts it as Scripture. Decisions say where each comes from, and footnotes give the sources.

**The angels and Sodom (6–7).** The angels who abandoned their own dwelling are most likely those of Genesis 6:1–4, as Jewish tradition told the story. Sodom and Gomorrah "went after flesh of another kind": the decision notes that the men of Sodom demanded the visitors, who were angels, and that others read it of same-sex relations or of immorality in general. The text says no more, and the draft keeps its words, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the readings.

**Reefs (12).** The false teachers are "reefs hidden under the water" at the love feasts. A word that sounds much the same means "stains", and 2 Peter 2:13 calls the false teachers "stains and blemishes"; the footnote gives that reading.

**Mercy for the wavering (22–23).** These two verses are among the hardest in the New Testament to establish. The manuscripts differ over whether Jude speaks of two groups or three, and over whether the first are to receive mercy or a rebuke. The draft follows its Greek text, with three groups, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the others, including the reading the King James Version follows.
