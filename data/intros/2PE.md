## What the book is

2 Peter is written as a last testament. The writer knows that he "must soon lay this tent aside", and he wants his readers to remember what they have been taught after he is gone. He begins with God's gifts: by his divine power believers have been given everything they need for life and godliness, so that they may "come to share in the divine nature". So they must add goodness to faith, and knowledge, self-control, endurance, godliness, kindness and love. He reminds them that the apostles saw Christ's greatness with their own eyes on the holy mountain, and that the prophets spoke from God.

Then he turns to false teachers, who will disown the Master who bought them, and warns of their end with the examples of the angels who sinned, the flood, Sodom and Gomorrah, and Balaam. And he answers the scoffers who ask, "Where is this promise of his coming?":

## Who wrote it, and when

The letter is from "Simeon Peter, a servant and apostle of Jesus Christ" (1:1), who says he was on the mountain when the voice came from heaven (1:16–18) and calls this his "second letter" (3:1). It was the most disputed book of the New Testament in the early church, and many scholars today think it was written in Peter's name after his death, perhaps around AD 80–120. They point to its Greek, very different from 1 Peter's, to its use of Jude, much of which reappears in chapter 2, and to its mention of Paul's letters as a collection. Others hold that Peter wrote it shortly before his death in the mid-60s, perhaps with a different helper from the one who wrote 1 Peter with him.

## How the book is laid out

- **Greeting** (1:1–2).
- **Growing in godliness** (1:3–11): sharers of the divine nature, and the qualities to add to faith.
- **Peter's reminder** (1:12–21): the apostles were eyewitnesses, and the prophets spoke from God.
- **False teachers** (2): their judgment is certain, as the angels, the flood and Sodom show.
- **The day of the Lord** (3:1–13): a thousand years like a single day, and new heavens and a new earth.
- **Closing** (3:14–18): Paul's letters, and "keep growing in the grace".

## How Open Word Bible translates 2 Peter

**Our God and Savior (1:1).** One article governs both titles, so the draft reads "our God and Savior Jesus Christ" as one person, as in Titus 2:13. A few manuscripts read "Lord" for "God". The footnotes give both the reading of two persons and the other text.

**Sharers of the divine nature (1:4).** The decision explains that believers come to share God's own life, above all his freedom from decay and death, through his promises. It does not mean that human beings become God. The decision is marked uncertain and footnoted, since the phrase has been read in different ways.

**Their own interpretation (1:20).** "No prophecy in Scripture arises from anyone's own interpretation": the draft reads this of how prophecy began, not in the prophet's own understanding but from God, as verse 21 goes on to say. Others read it of the reader: no one may interpret prophecy privately. The decision is marked uncertain and footnoted.

**Tartarus (2:4).** God threw the angels who sinned "down into Tartarus", the name Greek writers gave to the deepest part of the underworld. The draft keeps the name. See [Sheol, Hades and Gehenna](/articles/sheol-hades-and-gehenna/).

**Found out (3:10).** At the end of this verse the manuscripts differ more than anywhere else in the letter: "will be found", "will be burned up", "will not be found" and "will disappear". The draft follows the oldest manuscripts, "will be found", and renders it "found out": the earth and its deeds laid open before God for judgment. Both decisions are marked uncertain and footnoted. See [When manuscripts disagree](/articles/when-manuscripts-disagree/).

**Hurry it on (3:12).** Believers wait for the day of God "and hurry it on". The word can also mean to be eager for it. Verse 9 says the Lord waits so that people may repent, and the draft takes the first sense, marks it uncertain and footnotes the second.

**The other Scriptures (3:16).** Paul's letters are set beside "the other Scriptures", which counts them among the Scriptures. A decision says so, and a footnote gives the readings that take the word more loosely.
