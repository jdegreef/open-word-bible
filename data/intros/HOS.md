## What the book is

Hosea's message begins in his own home. God tells him to marry a woman given to unfaithfulness, Gomer, and to give their three children names that are messages: Jezreel, for the bloodshed there; Lo-ruhamah, "not shown compassion"; and Lo-ammi, "not my people". As Gomer is unfaithful to Hosea, so Israel has been unfaithful to God, running after the Baals, the gods of rain and harvest, as if they had given the grain and the wine.

Yet the marriage is not ended. God will lead his people into the wilderness and speak tenderly to her, as when she first came out of Egypt, and the children's names will be reversed: "You are not my people" will become "children of the living God". The rest of the book is a torrent of short oracles against Israel's priests and kings, its calf idols, its alliances with Egypt and Assyria, and its shallow repentance. ## Who wrote it, and when

Hosea son of Beeri spoke in the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah of Judah, and of Jeroboam son of Joash of Israel (1:1), from about 750 BC until shortly before Samaria fell to Assyria in 722. He was a younger contemporary of Amos, and the only one of the writing prophets who came from the northern kingdom itself. His words were probably gathered and kept in Judah after the north fell; the last verse speaks to any reader who is wise enough to understand.

## How the book is laid out

- **Hosea's marriage and children** (1–3): the names, the unfaithful wife, the wilderness and the new betrothal, and Hosea buying back his wife.
- **The LORD's case against Israel** (4–10): no faithfulness, no loyal love, no knowledge of God; priests, kings, calves and alliances.
- **The love that will not let go** (11): out of Egypt I called my son.
- **Jacob and Ephraim** (12–14:1): Israel's history of deceit, and its coming end.
- **Return, Israel** (14:2–10): I will heal their unfaithfulness.

## How Open Word Bible translates Hosea

**Verse numbers.** The verses are numbered as in the Hebrew text, which differs from most English Bibles in three places. Hebrew 2:1–2 is their 1:10–11, so the rest of chapter 2 is two verses higher here; Hebrew 12:1 is their 11:12; and Hebrew 14:1 is their 13:16. Notes at those verses explain.

**A promiscuous wife (1:2).** The text does not say whether Gomer was unfaithful before the marriage or became so after it. A decision says so, marks the matter uncertain and footnotes the readings. Whether the woman of chapter 3 is Gomer is also not said; the draft takes her to be, marks it uncertain and footnotes the others.

**Loyal love (6:6).** "I want loyal love, not sacrifice". The Hebrew word is *ḥesed*, faithful love within a covenant, and it is one of Hosea's key words, with faithfulness and the knowledge of God (4:1). Jesus quotes the verse twice in its Greek form, "I want mercy, not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13; 12:7). See [Hesed: steadfast love](/articles/hesed-steadfast-love/).

**On the third day (6:2).** The people's song of return says that God "will raise us up" on the third day. The draft translates it as Hosea's words about the people's recovery, and a footnote notes that many Christians have heard in it a foreshadowing of the resurrection.

**Questions or promises? (13:14).** "Shall I ransom them from the power of Sheol?" can be read as questions expecting the answer no, followed by a call to death to bring its plagues, or as promises of rescue. Paul quotes the line in its Greek form as a taunt over death (1 Corinthians 15:55). The draft reads it as questions, as the next line, "compassion is hidden from my eyes", suggests, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other reading. See [Sheol, Hades and Gehenna](/articles/sheol-hades-and-gehenna/).

**A hard text.** The Hebrew of Hosea is among the hardest in the Old Testament, and the ancient translations already differ over many lines. Where the text is uncertain, the draft follows the Hebrew as it stands where it can, gives the most likely sense, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other readings, including those of the Greek translation. See [The Septuagint](/articles/the-septuagint/).

**Names and wordplay.** Hosea plays on names: Bethel, "house of God", becomes Beth-aven, "house of wickedness"; Israel will call God "my husband", no longer "my baal", a word that means both "master" and the name of the god. Decisions explain each. See [Wordplay that cannot be translated](/articles/wordplay-that-cannot-be-translated/).

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
