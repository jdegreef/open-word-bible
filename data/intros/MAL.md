## What the book is

Malachi is written as a series of arguments. God makes a statement, the people answer with a question, and God replies. "I have loved you, says the LORD. Yet you ask, 'In what way have you loved us?'" The people have come home from exile and the temple has been rebuilt, but the great days the prophets promised have not come, and they have grown weary and careless. The priests offer blind and lame animals on the altar. Men are divorcing the wives of their youth. The people hold back their tithes, and say that it is useless to serve God, since the wicked do well.

God answers each complaint, and promises that he himself will come:

> {{OWB:MAL.3.1.s1:B}}

On that day the difference between those who serve God and those who do not will be plain, and for those who revere his name "the sun of righteousness will rise, bringing healing in its wings". The book ends by calling the people to remember the law of Moses, and with a promise that God will send Elijah the prophet before the great and terrible day of the LORD. The Gospels see that promise kept in John the Baptist (Matthew 11:10–14; Mark 9:11–13; Luke 1:17).

## Who wrote it, and when

The name Malachi means "my messenger", the word God uses in 3:1, and nothing else is known of the prophet; some have thought it a title, not a name. The book speaks of a governor, of the temple standing and its sacrifices offered, and of the same troubles that Nehemiah faced: careless priests, marriage to worshippers of other gods, and unpaid tithes (Nehemiah 13). So it is usually dated in the fifth century BC, around 460–430.

## How the book is laid out

- **I have loved you** (1:1–5): Jacob and Esau.
- **The priests despise God's name** (1:6–2:9): blemished sacrifices, and the covenant with Levi.
- **Unfaithfulness to the covenant** (2:10–16): marriage to foreign gods, and divorce.
- **The messenger and the day of judgment** (2:17–3:5): "Where is the God of justice?"
- **Return to me** (3:6–12): the tithes, and the windows of heaven.
- **Those who revere the LORD** (3:13–24): the book of remembrance, the sun of righteousness, and Elijah.

## How Open Word Bible translates Malachi

**Verse numbers.** The verses are numbered as in the Hebrew text, which ends the book with chapter 3. The last six verses, 3:19–24 here, are chapter 4 in most English Bibles. Notes at 3:19 and 3:23 explain.

**I hate divorce (2:16).** The Hebrew of this verse is hard. The draft reads it with the LORD as the one who hates divorce, as the words "says the LORD" suggest, and marks the decision uncertain. Others read it of the man who divorces, "the man who hates and divorces covers his garment with violence", and some ancient translations read it as permission to divorce. The footnotes give these readings. Verse 15, just before it, is among the hardest verses in the prophets, and is also footnoted.

**Jacob loved, Esau hated (1:2–3).** A decision explains that in Hebrew "love" and "hate" set side by side often mean choosing one and setting the other aside, not the feelings the English words suggest. The draft keeps the words, and the footnote explains. Paul quotes the verse in Romans 9:13.

**The Lord and the messenger (3:1).** God first sends "my messenger" to clear the way. Then "the Lord you are seeking", God himself, comes to his temple, and "the messenger of the covenant" is coming. Whether the messenger of the covenant is the Lord himself or a second figure is uncertain; the decision says so and the footnote gives the other reading.

**My name is great among the nations (1:11).** The Hebrew has no verb, so the verse may speak of the present or of the future: "my name is great", or "will be great". The draft takes the first, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the second.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. Malachi's favourite title is "the LORD of Armies". See [Names and titles of God](/articles/names-and-titles-of-god/).
