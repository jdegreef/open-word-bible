## What the book is

Judges covers the time between Joshua's death and the rise of the kings. The tribes have settled in Canaan, but they have not driven out its peoples, and they soon begin to worship their gods. The book sets out a pattern at the start:

> {{OWB:JDG.2.11.s1:B}}

God hands them over to their enemies; they cry out; and he raises up a leader to rescue them:

> {{OWB:JDG.2.16.s1:B}}

Then the leader dies, and the people turn away again, each time a little further. The book tells the stories of twelve of these leaders. Some are told in a line or two. Others are among the best-known stories of the Old Testament: left-handed Ehud and the fat king of Moab; Deborah and Barak, and Jael with her tent peg, with Deborah's song of victory; Gideon, who tests God with a fleece and defeats the Midianites with three hundred men; Jephthah and his terrible vow; and Samson, whose strength is in his uncut hair and whose weakness is Delilah.

The judges themselves grow more flawed as the book goes on, and its last chapters, without any judge, tell of a stolen idol and a migrating tribe, and of an outrage at Gibeah that leads to civil war. Twice the book gives its own verdict on the age:

> {{OWB:JDG.21.25.s1:B}}

## Who wrote it, and when

The book names no author. Jewish tradition credited it to Samuel. Its refrain, "in those days there was no king in Israel", looks back from a time when there was a king. Many scholars see Judges as part of a longer history running from Deuteronomy through Kings, drawn together from older stories and songs in the seventh and sixth centuries BC. The Song of Deborah (5) is thought by many to be one of the oldest poems in the Bible. The draft takes no side on these questions; they do not change how a sentence is translated.

## How the book is laid out

- **Israel in the land** (1:1–3:6): the tribes fail to drive out the Canaanites, and the pattern of the book.
- **The judges** (3:7–16:31): Othniel, Ehud, Shamgar, Deborah and Barak, Gideon, Abimelech, Tola and Jair, Jephthah, Ibzan, Elon and Abdon, and Samson.
- **No king in Israel** (17–21): Micah's idol and the tribe of Dan, the outrage at Gibeah, and the war against Benjamin.

## How Open Word Bible translates Judges

**Judges.** The leaders of this book are more than court judges: they are leaders God raised up for a crisis, who rescued Israel in battle and then led the people. A decision at 2:16 explains the word, which the draft keeps.

**Jephthah's vow (11:31, 39).** Jephthah vows that "whoever or whatever comes out through the doors of my house to meet me" will be offered as a burnt offering, and it is his daughter. The Hebrew form can mean a person or an animal, and the draft keeps it open. The narrator says only that "he did to her what he had vowed". The plain reading is that he put her to death as an offering, a deed the law condemns; some have read it instead as dedicating her to the LORD to live unmarried. The decisions set out both readings, are marked uncertain and footnoted.

**The Song of Deborah (5).** The song is old Hebrew poetry, and many of its words are rare or found nowhere else. The draft gives the most likely sense in each place, sets the poem in lines, marks the decisions uncertain and footnotes other readings. See [Hebrew poetry and parallelism](/articles/hebrew-poetry-and-parallelism/).

**Deborah, wife of Lappidoth (4:4).** The words can name her husband, or mean "a woman of torches", a fiery woman. The draft takes the first, as Hebrew usually introduces a woman, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the other.

**Shibboleth (12:6).** The men of Gilead caught the Ephraimites by making them say "Shibboleth"; they said "Sibboleth". The draft keeps both words, and a note explains the test.

**Wordplay (15:16).** Samson's boast over the jawbone of a donkey plays on a word that means both "donkey" and "heap". English cannot keep it, and a note explains. See [Wordplay that cannot be translated](/articles/wordplay-that-cannot-be-translated/).

**Moses or Manasseh? (18:30).** The priest of the tribe of Dan was Jonathan, "son of Gershom, son of Manasseh". In Hebrew manuscripts one letter of "Manasseh" is written above the line; without it, the name reads "Moses", whose son was Gershom. Jewish tradition explains the raised letter as added out of respect for Moses, so that a descendant of his serving an image would not be named as his. The draft keeps the written name; the decision explains, is marked uncertain and footnoted.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
