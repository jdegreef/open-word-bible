## What the book is

2 John is a letter that would fit on a single sheet of papyrus. "The elder" writes to "the chosen lady and her children", glad that some of them are living in the truth. He repeats the command they have had from the beginning, that we love one another, and he warns them about deceivers who have gone out into the world:

> {{OWB:2JN.1.7.s1:B}}

Such teachers are not to be welcomed into the house where the believers meet, since to give them a greeting is to share in their work. The elder has much more to say, but would rather say it face to face.

## Who wrote it, and when

The writer calls himself only "the elder". His language is so close to 1 John, with its truth, love, remaining and the command from the beginning, that the two letters plainly come from the same circle, and 3 John is from the same elder. From the second century the letters were credited to John the apostle; some early writers already thought of another John, an elder of Ephesus, and many scholars today think the elder was a leader of the communities that treasured John's Gospel. The letter is usually dated around AD 90–110, at about the time of 1 John.

## How the book is laid out

- **Greeting** (1–3): to the chosen lady and her children, whom the elder loves in truth.
- **Love one another** (4–6): the command they have had from the beginning.
- **Deceivers and antichrists** (7–11): do not receive into your home anyone who does not bring this teaching.
- **Closing** (12–13): the hope of meeting face to face, and greetings from the chosen sister's children.

## How Open Word Bible translates 2 John

**The chosen lady (1, 13).** The letter may be written to a church, spoken of as a mother and her children, with greetings at the end from a sister church; or to a believing woman and her household; or to a woman named Kyria, since the Greek word for "lady" can be a name. The "you" of the letter moves between singular and plural, and its commands fit a congregation, so the draft takes the first reading. It marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the others.

**Come in the flesh (7).** The deceivers do not confess Jesus Christ "as one who has come in the flesh". 1 John 4:2 says the same with a form meaning "has come"; here the Greek uses a present form, which the draft reads as the lasting fact of the incarnation. The footnote gives the readings that refer it to his coming again, or take it as a title, "the one who comes".

**The teaching of Christ (9).** The phrase can mean the teaching about Christ or the teaching Christ himself gave. The draft takes the first, since verses 7 and 10 are about who Jesus is, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the second. See [The genitive problem](/articles/the-genitive-problem/).

**Do not receive him (10).** The command is about a travelling teacher who comes to the house where the church meets to spread the denial of verse 7. A decision explains that it forbids the hospitality that would support his teaching, and does not speak of ordinary kindness to unbelievers or to neighbours and family who disagree. A footnote says so.

**The text (3, 8, 12).** The manuscripts differ over small words: "with us" or "with you" in the greeting; whether "we" or "you" may lose what was worked for in verse 8; and whether the joy of verse 12 is "your joy" or "our joy". The draft follows its Greek text in each place and footnotes the others. See [When manuscripts disagree](/articles/when-manuscripts-disagree/).
