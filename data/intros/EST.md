## What the book is

Esther is a story set at the court of the Persian king Ahasuerus, in the citadel of Susa, full of banquets, decrees and sudden reversals. When Queen Vashti refuses to appear at the king's feast, a new queen is sought throughout the empire, and the one chosen is Esther, a young Jewish woman raised by her cousin Mordecai, who tells her to keep her people secret. Mordecai refuses to bow down to Haman, the king's chief minister, and Haman, in his rage, gains the king's consent to destroy all the Jews of the empire on a day chosen by lot. Mordecai sends word to Esther that she must go to the king, though to approach him unbidden could mean death:

Esther answers, "if I perish, I perish". She goes in, the king receives her, and at two banquets she brings Haman's plot to light. Haman is hanged on the gallows he had built for Mordecai, Mordecai is honoured in his place, and the Jews are allowed to defend themselves on the day set for their destruction. The book ends by explaining the festival of Purim, from the word *pur*, "the lot", kept every year to remember how sorrow was turned to joy.

## Who wrote it, and when

The book does not say who wrote it. Ahasuerus is the king known in Greek as Xerxes, who ruled Persia from 486 to 465 BC, and the story shows close knowledge of the Persian court. Most scholars date the writing to the late Persian or early Greek period, in the fourth or third century BC. Readers differ over how far it is history and how far a story told to explain the festival; the draft takes no side, which does not change how a sentence is translated.

The Greek translation of Esther is much longer. It adds six passages, among them prayers of Mordecai and Esther and the texts of the king's decrees, and in them God is named often. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include these additions; the Hebrew text, which the draft translates, does not have them.

## How the book is laid out

- **Vashti deposed, Esther made queen** (1–2): and Mordecai uncovers a plot against the king.
- **Haman's plot** (3): the lot is cast, and the decree sent out.
- **For a time like this** (4–5): Esther's fast, and her first banquet.
- **The reversal** (6–7): Mordecai honoured, and Haman hanged.
- **The Jews defend themselves** (8–9:19): the second decree.
- **The festival of Purim** (9:20–10:3).

## How Open Word Bible translates Esther

**God is not named.** Esther is one of only two books of the Bible, with the Song of Songs, that never names God, and the draft keeps that silence at every level and in every language. When Mordecai says that relief will come "from another place" (4:14), many readers hear a hint of God's hidden care; later Jewish writers sometimes used "the Place" as a name for God. The decision keeps the phrase as open as the Hebrew, and marks it uncertain.

**Why Mordecai would not bow (3:2).** The text gives no reason beyond his being a Jew. A decision sets out the explanations readers have given, that he would not give a man honour due to God alone, or that he would not bow to a descendant of Agag, Israel's old enemy, and marks the decision uncertain.

**Haman the Agagite (3:1).** The story invites the reader to hear in "Agagite" a descendant of Agag, the king of Amalek whom Saul spared (1 Samuel 15), while Mordecai, like Saul, is a Benjaminite descended from Kish. The decision says so and is marked uncertain.

**The gallows (5:14).** The word is literally "a tree" or "a tall wooden pole", on which a man was hanged or impaled, or a body displayed. The draft says "gallows", marks the decisions uncertain, and footnotes "impaling stake".

**Numbers (9:16).** The Hebrew says that the Jews killed seventy-five thousand of their enemies in the provinces; the Greek translation says fifteen thousand. The draft follows the Hebrew and footnotes the Greek. A decision at 9:10 notes that the Jews took no plunder, though the decree allowed it.
