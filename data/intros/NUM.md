## What the book is

Numbers takes its English name from the two counts of the people that frame it, in chapters 1 and 26. Its Hebrew name is "In the wilderness", and that is where the book takes place. It opens at Mount Sinai, a year after the exodus, with the tribes counted and arranged around the tent of meeting, the Levites given their duties, and the priests given the blessing they are to speak over the people:

Then the cloud lifts and the people set out, and almost at once they complain: about hardship, about the manna, about Moses. Twelve scouts go into Canaan, and ten of them report that the land cannot be taken. The people refuse to go in, and God declares that the whole generation that left Egypt, except Caleb and Joshua, will die in the wilderness. Rebellions follow, among them Korah's, and even Moses and Aaron fail at the waters of Meribah and are told they will not enter the land.

In the last part of the book a new generation is counted on the plains of Moab, across the Jordan from Jericho. The king of Moab hires the prophet Balaam to curse them, but Balaam can speak only blessing, and sees a star coming out of Jacob. The book ends with laws for life in the land and with the eastern tribes given their territory.

## Who wrote it, and when

Numbers is the fourth of the five books of the Law. Jewish and Christian tradition has held that Moses wrote it, and it says that Moses recorded the stages of the journey (33:2). Many scholars think instead that it gathers older traditions, lists and laws that reached their final form after the exile. The draft takes no side on these questions; they do not change how a sentence is translated.

## How the book is laid out

- **At Sinai** (1:1–10:10): the census, the camp, the Levites, the priestly blessing, and the Passover.
- **From Sinai to Kadesh** (10:11–12:16): complaints, the seventy elders, quail, and Miriam and Aaron against Moses.
- **The scouts and the refusal** (13–14): forty years in the wilderness.
- **Rebellion and the priesthood** (15–19): Korah, Aaron's staff that budded, and the red heifer.
- **From Kadesh to Moab** (20–21): the waters of Meribah, the death of Aaron, and the bronze snake.
- **Balaam** (22–24): the donkey, the angel, and four oracles of blessing.
- **On the plains of Moab** (25–36): Baal of Peor, the second census, the daughters of Zelophehad, Joshua appointed, the festivals, and the land east of the Jordan.

## How Open Word Bible translates Numbers

**How many? (1; 26).** The counts give 603,550 men of fighting age at Sinai. The Hebrew word for "thousand" can also mean a clan or a military unit, and some read the figures that way, which would make the people far fewer. The text treats them as numbers, since the twelve totals add up exactly, and the draft does the same; the decisions are marked uncertain and the footnotes give the other reading, as in Exodus 12:37.

**Verse numbers.** The verses are numbered as in the Hebrew text. In a few places this differs from most English Bibles: Hebrew 17:1–15 is their 16:36–50, and Hebrew 17:16–28 is their 17:1–13; Hebrew 25:19 is part of their 26:1; and Hebrew 30:1 is their 29:40. Notes explain.

**Moses at Meribah (20:10–12).** Moses asks, "Are we to bring water out of this rock for you?" The question may be angry or doubting, and Moses says "we", not naming the LORD as the one who gives the water. The decision sets out why God says Moses "did not trust me" and marks it uncertain. Which staff Moses took, his own or Aaron's, is also uncertain.

**The bronze snake (21:9).** Whoever had been bitten and looked at the bronze snake lived. Jesus speaks of it in John 3:14, and 2 Kings 18:4 tells how Hezekiah later broke it in pieces because the people burned incense to it. The decisions note both.

**Balaam (22–24).** God allows Balaam to go, and then his anger burns because he is going (22:22). The text does not explain it; the decision suggests that the story shows Balaam's heart set on Balak's reward, and is marked uncertain. Balaam's oracles are in Hebrew verse, set in lines, and the star and sceptre of 24:17 came to be read by Jews and Christians as the promised Messiah; a footnote explains.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
