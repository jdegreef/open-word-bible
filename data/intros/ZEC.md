## What the book is

Zechariah began to prophesy in 520 BC, two months after Haggai, to the same small community of exiles back from Babylon, rebuilding the temple among the ruins of Jerusalem. His first word is a call: "Return to me ... and I will return to you". Then come eight visions in a single night: horsemen among the myrtle trees, four horns and four smiths, a man with a measuring line, the high priest Joshua in filthy clothes before the Accuser, a golden lampstand between two olive trees, a flying scroll, a woman in a basket, and four chariots. An angel explains each. Their message is that God has not forgotten Jerusalem, that he will cleanse his people, and that the temple will be finished, not by human strength:

> {{OWB:ZEC.4.6.s1:B}}

The second half of the book is different in style, two long oracles without dates or names. It speaks of a king coming to Jerusalem:

> {{OWB:ZEC.9.9.s1:B}}

of a shepherd paid off with thirty pieces of silver, of one who is pierced and mourned, and of a shepherd struck so that the sheep scatter. It ends with the nations gathered against Jerusalem, the LORD standing on the Mount of Olives, and living water flowing from the city, when "the LORD will be king over all the earth". The Gospels quote these chapters more than any other part of the prophets in telling of Jesus' last days.

## Who wrote it, and when

Zechariah son of Berechiah, son of Iddo, is named with Haggai in Ezra 5:1 and 6:14 as one of the prophets whose preaching led to the rebuilding of the temple, finished in 515 BC. His messages in chapters 1–8 are dated between 520 and 518 BC. Many scholars think chapters 9–14, which mention Greece (9:13) and have no dates or mention of Zechariah, come from a later prophet or prophets, and they are sometimes called "Second Zechariah". Others hold that Zechariah wrote them later in his life.

## How the book is laid out

- **Return to me** (1:1–6).
- **Eight night visions** (1:7–6:8): with the cleansing of Joshua the high priest and the word to Zerubbabel.
- **The crown for Joshua** (6:9–15): the man called the Branch.
- **Fasting and the future** (7–8): justice, not fasts, and old people sitting again in Jerusalem's squares.
- **The coming king and the shepherds** (9–11): the king on a donkey, and thirty pieces of silver.
- **The day of the LORD** (12–14): the one they pierced, the struck shepherd, and the LORD king over all the earth.

## How Open Word Bible translates Zechariah

**Verse numbers.** The verses are numbered as in the Hebrew text, which begins chapter 2 with the second vision. Hebrew 2:1–4 is 1:18–21 in most English Bibles, and Hebrew 2:5–17 is their 2:1–13. Notes at 2:1 and 2:5 explain.

**The one they pierced (12:10).** "They will look on me, the one they pierced, and they will mourn for him". The LORD speaks, says "me", and then the verse speaks of "him". A number of medieval Hebrew manuscripts read "on him", which runs more smoothly. The draft follows the standard Hebrew text, and the decisions set out the main readings of who is pierced; both are marked uncertain and footnoted. John 19:37 and Revelation 1:7 quote the verse of Jesus.

**Thirty pieces of silver (11:12–13).** The shepherd's pay is thrown "to the potter" in the house of the LORD. With a change of one letter, the Syriac reads "to the treasury", and the footnote gives that reading. Matthew 27:9–10 sees the verse fulfilled when the chief priests buy the potter's field with the money Judas threw into the temple; Matthew names Jeremiah, and a note explains why.

**The king on a donkey (9:9).** The king is "righteous and victorious": a just king to whom God has given the victory. The Greek translation reads "bringing salvation", and Matthew 21:5 and John 12:15 quote the verse of Jesus' entry into Jerusalem. The footnotes explain.

**The visions.** Many details of the visions are uncertain: the "seven eyes" on the stone (3:9), the spouts of the lampstand (4:2), the "two anointed ones", in Hebrew "two sons of fresh oil" (4:14), the two mountains of bronze (6:1). In each place the draft gives the most likely sense, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the others.

**The Accuser (3:1).** The one who stands at Joshua's right hand to accuse him is "the satan" in Hebrew, with the article, so the word is a title, "the accuser", one who brings a charge in court, as in Job 1–2. The draft translates the title, "the Accuser", and a decision explains.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. Zechariah's favourite title is "the LORD of Armies". See [Names and titles of God](/articles/names-and-titles-of-god/).
