## What the book is

Zephaniah begins with the widest judgment in the prophets: God will sweep away everything from the face of the earth, people and animals, birds and fish. Then he narrows to Judah and Jerusalem, where people bow to Baal and to the stars on the rooftops, and say to themselves, "The LORD will do nothing, neither good nor bad." The heart of the book is the day of the LORD:

> {{OWB:ZEP.1.15.s1:B}}

These lines, in Latin, gave the church its hymn *Dies irae*, "day of wrath". The prophet goes on to the nations around, from the Philistines in the west to Assyria in the north, and back to Jerusalem, the city that would not listen. But the book ends in song. God will purify the speech of the peoples so that all of them call on his name, he will leave in Jerusalem "a people humble and lowly", and he will rejoice over them:

> {{OWB:ZEP.3.17.s1:B}}

## Who wrote it, and when

Zephaniah spoke "during the reign of Josiah son of Amon, king of Judah" (1:1), between 640 and 609 BC. His family line is traced back four generations, to a Hezekiah, perhaps the king of that name, which would make him a member of the royal family. The worship of Baal and of the stars that he condemns was swept away by Josiah's reform in 622 BC (2 Kings 23), so many date his preaching before it. Most of the book is generally thought to come from Zephaniah himself; some think the closing songs of hope were added after the exile.

## How the book is laid out

- **The day of the LORD against Judah** (1): the sweeping judgment, and the great day close at hand.
- **Seek the LORD** (2:1–3): perhaps you will be sheltered.
- **Against the nations** (2:4–15): Philistia, Moab and Ammon, Cush, and Assyria.
- **Against Jerusalem** (3:1–8): the city that would not listen.
- **A humble people, and a song of joy** (3:9–20): God in your midst, rejoicing over you.

## How Open Word Bible translates Zephaniah

**The poetry.** Zephaniah is written in Hebrew verse, and the draft sets it in lines. Several of its lines play on sounds that English cannot keep, such as "Gaza will be deserted" in 2:4, where the name and the word sound alike; a footnote explains. See [Hebrew poetry and parallelism](/articles/hebrew-poetry-and-parallelism/) and [Wordplay that cannot be translated](/articles/wordplay-that-cannot-be-translated/).

**Quiet in his love (3:17).** The Hebrew says God "will be silent in his love". That may mean his love is too deep for words, or that he rests content in it. The Greek translation read "he will renew you in his love", and some modern Bibles follow it. The draft keeps the Hebrew, as "be quiet in his love", marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the others.

**Their king (1:5).** The same Hebrew letters can be read "their king" or "Milcom", the god of the Ammonites. The draft reads "their king", meaning the god they honour as king, perhaps Milcom, and footnotes the other.

**Hard places.** Some lines are very hard in the Hebrew: the "stumbling blocks", rendered "the idols that make people fall" (1:3), "before the decree gives birth", rendered "before the decree takes effect" (2:2), and the last verses of the book (3:18). In each place the draft gives the most likely sense, marks the decision uncertain and footnotes the others. Where the Greek translation reads differently, as with "ravens" for "desolation" in 2:14 and "as a witness" for "for plunder" in 3:8, the draft follows the Hebrew and footnotes the Greek.

**The divine name.** The text writes God's personal name "the LORD" in small capitals, and a reader setting shows "Yahweh" instead. See [the divine name](/terms/the-divine-name-yhwh/).
